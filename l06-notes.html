#1. Eigenvectors and eigenvalues

Definition

For square matrices, eigenvectors and eigenvalues are vectors and numbers that represent the eigen-decomposition of a matrix; they analyse its structure. For A∈Rn×nA\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}A∈Rn×n, a vector q∈Rnq\in\mathbb{R}^nq∈Rn, q≠0q\neq0q=0, is an eigenvector of AAA if
Aq=λq(eigenvalue equation),Aq=\lambda q\qquad(\textbf{eigenvalue equation}),Aq=λq(eigenvalue equation),
and λ\lambdaλ is the eigenvalue associated with qqq.

Linear-transformation interpretation: q∈Rn→A→λq∈Rnq\in\mathbb{R}^n\to\boxed{A}\to\lambda q\in\mathbb{R}^nq∈Rn→A​→λq∈Rn — only scaling. Graphically, AqAqAq lies on the same line through the origin as qqq. On that line AAA stretches (∣λ∣>1|\lambda|>1∣λ∣>1), shrinks (∣λ∣<1|\lambda|<1∣λ∣<1), flips (λ<0\lambda<0λ<0) or squashes to 0 (λ=0\lambda=0λ=0) — but never moves points off the line.

Remarks:

  • q≠0q\neq0q=0 matters (A0=λ0A0=\lambda0A0=λ0 for every λ\lambdaλ says nothing).
  • Eigenvectors are defined only up to scale: A(cq)=λ(cq)A(cq)=\lambda(cq)A(cq)=λ(cq). We usually normalise to ∥q∥=1\|q\|=1∥q∥=1; the sign is also free.
  • Only square matrices have eigenvalues (AqAqAq and qqq must live in the same space).
  • Is (0,1)(0,1)(0,1) an eigenvector of SSS? S(0,1)⊤=(1,2)⊤S(0,1)^\top=(1,2)^\topS(0,1)⊤=(1,2)⊤ — no. Is (−2,−2)(-2,-2)(−2,−2)? S(−2,−2)⊤=(−6,−6)⊤=3(−2,−2)⊤S(-2,-2)^\top=(-6,-6)^\top=3(-2,-2)^\topS(−2,−2)⊤=(−6,−6)⊤=3(−2,−2)⊤ — yes, λ=3\lambda=3λ=3.
Remember

An eigenvector is a non-zero direction that AAA only scales: Aq=λqAq=\lambda qAq=λq.

#8. Zero eigenvalues ⇔ dependent columns

If λ=0\lambda=0λ=0 with eigenvector q≠0q\neq0q=0:
Aq=λq=0.Aq=\lambda q=0 .Aq=λq=0.
AqAqAq is a linear combination of the columns of AAA with weights qqq (not all zero) that equals 0 — so the columns of AAA are not linearly independent.

Theorem / Key result: Four equivalent facts

zero eigenvalue ⇔ dependent columns ⇔ det⁡A=0\det A=0detA=0 ⇔ AAA has no inverse (information is lost).
A negative eigenvalue is harmless (a flip can be undone); only a zero eigenvalue loses information.

#9. Symmetric matrices — the spectral theorem

Theorem / Key result: Spectral theorem

For a symmetric A∈Rn×nA\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}A∈Rn×n there exists an orthonormal basis of Rn\mathbb{R}^nRn consisting of eigenvectors of AAA, and each eigenvalue is real. Hence Q⊤Q=IQ^\top Q=IQ⊤Q=I, Q−1=Q⊤Q^{-1}=Q^\topQ−1=Q⊤ (orthonormal matrix) and
A=QΛQ⊤.A=Q\Lambda Q^\top .A=QΛQ⊤.

Derivation: Why Q−1=Q⊤Q^{-1}=Q^\topQ−1=Q⊤ fits with symmetry (slide p16)

A=QΛQ−1A=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}A=QΛQ−1 ⇒ A⊤=(Q−1)⊤ΛQ⊤A^\top=(Q^{-1})^\top\Lambda Q^\topA⊤=(Q−1)⊤ΛQ⊤. Since A=A⊤A=A^\topA=A⊤, choosing Q−1=Q⊤Q^{-1}=Q^\topQ−1=Q⊤ makes the two expressions identical term by term — and the spectral theorem guarantees that choice is available.
Bonus (eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal): if Aq1=λ1q1Aq_1=\lambda_1q_1Aq1​=λ1​q1​, Aq2=λ2q2Aq_2=\lambda_2q_2Aq2​=λ2​q2​, λ1≠λ2\lambda_1\neq\lambda_2λ1​=λ2​, then λ1q1⊤q2=(Aq1)⊤q2=q1⊤A⊤q2=q1⊤Aq2=λ2q1⊤q2\lambda_1q_1^\top q_2=(Aq_1)^\top q_2=q_1^\top A^\top q_2=q_1^\top Aq_2=\lambda_2q_1^\top q_2λ1​q1⊤​q2​=(Aq1​)⊤q2​=q1⊤​A⊤q2​=q1⊤​Aq2​=λ2​q1⊤​q2​, so (λ1−λ2)q1⊤q2=0⇒q1⊤q2=0(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)q_1^\top q_2=0\Rightarrow q_1^\top q_2=0(λ1​−λ2​)q1⊤​q2​=0⇒q1⊤​q2​=0.

Summary: for a symmetric matrix, eigenvectors are orthonormal and eigenvalues are real. Geometrically: rotate (orthonormal QQQ), stretch along perpendicular axes, rotate back — the unit circle always becomes a clean ellipse with perpendicular axes along the eigenvectors.
