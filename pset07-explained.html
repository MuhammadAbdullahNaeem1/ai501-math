#The two recipes used on this whole page

SVD recipe — every SVD problem below follows these four steps:

  1. Form A⊤AA^\top AA⊤A.
  2. Its eigenvalues give the singular values: σi=λi\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}σi​=λi​​.
  3. Its unit eigenvectors are the right singular vectors viv_ivi​.
  4. Each left singular vector is ui=Aviσiu_i=\dfrac{Av_i}{\sigma_i}ui​=σi​Avi​​.

PCA recipe — every PCA problem below follows these five steps:

  1. Compute the mean xˉ\bar xxˉ.
  2. Centre every point: si=xi−xˉs_i=x_i-\bar xsi​=xi​−xˉ.
  3. Covariance matrix: C=1n∑isisi⊤C=\frac1n\sum_i s_is_i^\topC=n1​∑i​si​si⊤​.
  4. Eigen-decompose CCC and sort the eigenvalues from largest to smallest.
  5. Keep the top kkk eigenvectors.

Why the SVD recipe works. The SVD says every matrix sends some special input directions viv_ivi​ to output directions uiu_iui​, stretched by σi\sigma_iσi​:

Avi=σi uiAv_i=\sigma_i\,u_iAvi​=σi​ui​

The matrix A⊤AA^\top AA⊤A measures stretching: x⊤A⊤Ax=∥Ax∥2x^\top A^\top Ax=\|Ax\|^2x⊤A⊤Ax=∥Ax∥2 is the squared length of the output. So its eigenvectors are the directions that get stretched the most and least, and its eigenvalues are the squared stretches — hence the square root. Once you know viv_ivi​ and σi\sigma_iσi​, the equation above tells you uiu_iui​: just divide AviAv_iAvi​ by σi\sigma_iσi​.

#Problem 1 — the SVD of a diagonal matrix

📄 Original questionPSet 7 · Problem 1 ↗

Consider A=(3001)A=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}A=(30​01​). (a) Give matrices UUU, Σ\SigmaΣ and VVV such that A=UΣV⊤A=U\Sigma V^\topA=UΣV⊤. (b) What are the singular values of AAA? Which input direction is stretched more by the transformation x↦Axx\mapsto Axx↦Ax?

Worked solution

What it is really asking. The SVD describes any matrix as rotate → stretch → rotate. A diagonal matrix with non-negative entries does only the stretch: it multiplies the xxx-coordinate by 3 and the yyy-coordinate by 1. So the two rotations should be "do nothing" — the identity.

See it first. Feed in the two axis vectors:

The xxx-axis direction is stretched by 3
A[10]=[3001][10]=[30]=3[10]A\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\0\end{bmatrix}=3\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}A[10​]=[30​01​][10​]=[30​]=3[10​]
The yyy-axis direction is stretched by 1 (left as it is)
A[01]=[01]=1[01]A\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}=1\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}A[01​]=[01​]=1[01​]

Now confirm with the recipe (so you can do it for any matrix):

Step 1 · form A⊤AA^\top AA⊤A (AAA is symmetric, so A⊤=AA^\top=AA⊤=A)
A⊤A=[3001][3001]=[9001]A^\top A=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}9&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}A⊤A=[30​01​][30​01​]=[90​01​]
Step 2 · a diagonal matrix has its eigenvalues on the diagonal; take square roots
λ1=9,λ2=1⟹σ1=3,σ2=1\lambda_1=9,\quad\lambda_2=1\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \sigma_1=3,\quad\sigma_2=1λ1​=9,λ2​=1⟹σ1​=3,σ2​=1
Step 3 · the eigenvectors of a diagonal matrix are the axis vectors
v1=[10]v2=[01]v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}\qquad v_2=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}v1​=[10​]v2​=[01​]
Step 4 · ui=Avi/σiu_i=Av_i/\sigma_iui​=Avi​/σi​
u1=13[30]=[10]u2=11[01]=[01]u_1=\frac13\begin{bmatrix}3\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}\qquad u_2=\frac11\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix}u1​=31​[30​]=[10​]u2​=11​[01​]=[01​]
Answer

A=[1001]⏟U=I [3001]⏟Σ=A [1001]⏟V⊤=Iσ1=3,  σ2=1A=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}}_{U=I}\ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}}_{\Sigma=A}\ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}}_{V^\top=I}\qquad\qquad \sigma_1=3,\ \ \sigma_2=1A=U=I[10​01​]​​ Σ=A[30​01​]​​ V⊤=I[10​01​]​​σ1​=3,  σ2​=1

(b) The direction stretched more is (1,0)(1,0)(1,0) — the xxx-axis — by a factor of 3. Picture: the unit circle becomes an ellipse 3 wide and 1 tall.

Singular values are never negative. For diag⁡(3,−1)\operatorname{diag}(3,-1)diag(3,−1) the singular values are 333 and 111, and the minus sign has to go into UUU (a flip): U=diag⁡(1,−1)U=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)U=diag(1,−1), Σ=diag⁡(3,1)\Sigma=\operatorname{diag}(3,1)Σ=diag(3,1), V=IV=IV=I.

#Problem 2 — a rank-one matrix

#Problem 4 — rank and low-rank approximation from singular values

📄 Original questionPSet 7 · Problem 4 ↗

Suppose a data matrix has singular values 101010, 222 and 0.50.50.5. (a) What is the rank of the matrix? (b) If we keep only the largest singular value, what rank approximation do we obtain, and what is the Frobenius norm of the discarded part? (c) Compute the fraction of the squared Frobenius norm retained by this rank-one approximation.

Worked solution

The idea you need. An SVD splits a matrix into a sum of rank-one "layers", one per singular value:

A=σ1u1v1⊤+σ2u2v2⊤+σ3u3v3⊤A=\sigma_1u_1v_1^\top+\sigma_2u_2v_2^\top+\sigma_3u_3v_3^\topA=σ1​u1​v1⊤​+σ2​u2​v2⊤​+σ3​u3​v3⊤​

Each layer is a column times a row — a rank-one matrix — with strength σi\sigma_iσi​. And the "total size" of the matrix splits the same way:

∥A∥F2=σ12+σ22+σ32\|A\|_F^2=\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2+\sigma_3^2∥A∥F2​=σ12​+σ22​+σ32​

(The Frobenius norm is the square root of the sum of the squares of all entries. The rotations UUU and VVV do not change lengths, so only the stretches σi\sigma_iσi​ count.)

(a) Count the non-zero singular values
10, 2, 0.5 all≠0⟹rank⁡=310,\ 2,\ 0.5\ \text{all}\ne0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\operatorname{rank}=310, 2, 0.5 all=0⟹rank=3
(b) Keep one layer: a rank-one approximation
A1=σ1u1v1⊤=10 u1v1⊤A_1=\sigma_1u_1v_1^\top=10\,u_1v_1^\topA1​=σ1​u1​v1⊤​=10u1​v1⊤​
The thrown-away part is the other two layers, so its size uses σ2\sigma_2σ2​ and σ3\sigma_3σ3​
∥A−A1∥F=σ22+σ32=4+0.25=4.25=172≈2.06\|A-A_1\|_F=\sqrt{\sigma_2^2+\sigma_3^2}=\sqrt{4+0.25}=\sqrt{4.25}=\frac{\sqrt{17}}{2}\approx2.06∥A−A1​∥F​=σ22​+σ32​​=4+0.25​=4.25​=217​​≈2.06
(c) Fraction of the total kept
σ12σ12+σ22+σ32=100100+4+0.25=100104.25=400417≈0.959\frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2+\sigma_3^2}=\frac{100}{100+4+0.25}=\frac{100}{104.25}=\frac{400}{417}\approx0.959σ12​+σ22​+σ32​σ12​​=100+4+0.25100​=104.25100​=417400​≈0.959

So one layer keeps 95.9% of the matrix's "energy". This is why low-rank approximation works for images and data: the first few singular values usually hold almost everything.

#Problem 5 — PCA by hand: covariance and principal components

📄 Original questionPSet 7 · Problem 5 ↗

Four observations, each with two features, are x1=(2,1)⊤x_1=(2,1)^\topx1​=(2,1)⊤, x2=(0,−1)⊤x_2=(0,-1)^\topx2​=(0,−1)⊤, x3=(1,2)⊤x_3=(1,2)^\topx3​=(1,2)⊤, x4=(−1,0)⊤x_4=(-1,0)^\topx4​=(−1,0)⊤. (a) Find the sample mean and write the centered observations as rows of a matrix XcX_cXc​. (b) Compute the covariance matrix C=14Xc⊤XcC=\tfrac14X_c^\top X_cC=41​Xc⊤​Xc​. (c) Find the eigenvalues and corresponding unit eigenvectors of CCC. Which direction is the first principal component?

Worked solution

What it is really asking. Find the direction in which these four points are most spread out. PCA does that in three moves: move the cloud so its centre is at the origin, measure how the two features vary together (the covariance matrix), and take the eigenvector with the biggest eigenvalue.

(a) The mean and the centred points.

Average each feature separately
xˉ=14[2+0+1+(−1)1+(−1)+2+0]=14[22]=[1/21/2]\bar x=\frac14\begin{bmatrix}2+0+1+(-1)\\1+(-1)+2+0\end{bmatrix}=\frac14\begin{bmatrix}2\\2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1/2\\1/2\end{bmatrix}xˉ=41​[2+0+1+(−1)1+(−1)+2+0​]=41​[22​]=[1/21/2​]
Subtract the mean from each point
s1=[3/21/2]s2=[−1/2−3/2]s3=[1/23/2]s4=[−3/2−1/2]s_1=\begin{bmatrix}3/2\\1/2\end{bmatrix}\quad s_2=\begin{bmatrix}-1/2\\-3/2\end{bmatrix}\quad s_3=\begin{bmatrix}1/2\\3/2\end{bmatrix}\quad s_4=\begin{bmatrix}-3/2\\-1/2\end{bmatrix}s1​=[3/21/2​]s2​=[−1/2−3/2​]s3​=[1/23/2​]s4​=[−3/2−1/2​]
Stack them as the rows of XcX_cXc​
Xc=[3/21/2−1/2−3/21/23/2−3/2−1/2]X_c=\begin{bmatrix}3/2&1/2\\-1/2&-3/2\\1/2&3/2\\-3/2&-1/2\end{bmatrix}Xc​=​3/2−1/21/2−3/2​1/2−3/23/2−1/2​​

Why centre? Variance measures spread around the centre. If you skip this step, the covariance would mix in where the cloud is, not just how it is shaped.

(b) The covariance matrix, entry by entry. Xc⊤XcX_c^\top X_cXc⊤​Xc​ is a 2×22\times22×2 table: each entry is a dot product of two columns of XcX_cXc​.

Top-left: column 1 with itself (spread of feature 1)
(32)2+(−12)2+(12)2+(−32)2=94+14+14+94=5\big(\tfrac32\big)^2+\big(-\tfrac12\big)^2+\big(\tfrac12\big)^2+\big(-\tfrac32\big)^2=\tfrac94+\tfrac14+\tfrac14+\tfrac94=5(23​)2+(−21​)2+(21​)2+(−23​)2=49​+41​+41​+49​=5
Bottom-right: column 2 with itself (spread of feature 2)
(12)2+(−32)2+(32)2+(−12)2=5\big(\tfrac12\big)^2+\big(-\tfrac32\big)^2+\big(\tfrac32\big)^2+\big(-\tfrac12\big)^2=5(21​)2+(−23​)2+(23​)2+(−21​)2=5
Off-diagonal: column 1 with column 2 (how they move together)
32⋅12+(−12)(−32)+12⋅32+(−32)(−12)=34⋅4=3\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac12+\big(-\tfrac12\big)\big(-\tfrac32\big)+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac32+\big(-\tfrac32\big)\big(-\tfrac12\big)=\tfrac34\cdot4=323​⋅21​+(−21​)(−23​)+21​⋅23​+(−23​)(−21​)=43​⋅4=3
Divide by n=4n=4n=4
C=14[5335]=[5/43/43/45/4]C=\frac14\begin{bmatrix}5&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5/4&3/4\\3/4&5/4\end{bmatrix}C=41​[53​35​]=[5/43/4​3/45/4​]

How to read it: each feature has variance 5/45/45/4; the covariance 3/43/43/4 is positive and large, so when one feature is above its average the other usually is too — the points lie along a rising diagonal.

(c) Eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

Characteristic equation
(54−λ)2−(34)2=0⟹54−λ=±34\Big(\tfrac54-\lambda\Big)^2-\Big(\tfrac34\Big)^2=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\tfrac54-\lambda=\pm\tfrac34(45​−λ)2−(43​)2=0⟹45​−λ=±43​
So

#Problem 10 — designing a PCA pipeline

📄 Original questionPSet 7 · Problem 10 ↗

You are asked to build a compact representation of a dataset containing thousands of customer records, each described by hundreds of numeric measurements. The downstream model is too slow with all measurements. (a) Describe a PCA-based preprocessing pipeline from raw measurements to reduced features, including how you handle centering and how you choose the number of components. (b) Two measurements use very different units (annual income in dollars and age in years). What should you check before applying PCA, and what choice would you make if scale differences are not meaningful? (c) Give one measure of information retained and one downstream check you would use to decide whether the reduction is useful.

Worked solution

(a) The pipeline, step by step, with the reason for each step.

  1. Split the data into a training set and an evaluation set. Why: you must judge the final model on data it never saw, and that includes the PCA step itself.
  2. Compute the feature means on the training set only, and centre the training data with them. Why: using the evaluation data to compute the mean would leak information from it into training.
  3. Fit PCA on the centred training data: covariance, eigenvectors, keep the top kkk in WWW.
  4. Apply the same mean and the same WWW to the evaluation data: z=W⊤(x−xˉtrain)z=W^\top(x-\bar x_{\text{train}})z=W⊤(x−xˉtrain​).
  5. Train the model on the reduced training features zzz.
  6. Choose kkk either by a target fraction of variance (for example, the smallest kkk with Tk≥0.95T_k\ge0.95Tk​≥0.95) or by trying several kkk and keeping the one with the best validation score.

(b) Units. PCA looks for large variance, and variance depends on units: income in dollars has variance in the millions, age in years only in the hundreds. Without fixing this, the first principal component would simply be "income" — not because income matters more, but because its numbers are bigger. If the scale differences are not meaningful, standardise each feature (subtract its mean, divide by its standard deviation, both from the training set) before PCA.

(c) Two checks.

  • Information kept: the fraction of variance retained, Tk=∑i≤kλi/∑iλiT_k=\sum_{i\le k}\lambda_i\big/\sum_i\lambda_iTk​=∑i≤k​λi​/∑i​λi​.
  • Usefulness downstream: compare the model's validation accuracy (and its speed) with and without the reduction. If accuracy barely drops while the model becomes much faster, the reduction is worth it.

#Problem 12 — movie ratings with missing entries

📄 Original questionPSet 7 · Problem 12 ↗

A movie service has a user-by-movie table of ratings, but most entries are missing because users have not watched most movies. The team wants to predict some missing ratings using a low-rank model. (a) Explain why directly applying an ordinary SVD to a table that replaces every missing rating by zero can give misleading results. (b) Propose a reasonable workflow that uses low-rank structure while respecting which ratings were actually observed. State what the model should be fitted to and how its rank could be selected. (c) Name a suitable validation strategy and one metric for judging rating predictions. Explain why the training error alone is not enough.

Worked solution

(a) Why zeros mislead. A zero means "this user hated this movie", but a missing entry means "we don't know". Filling every gap with 0 invents thousands of terrible ratings. Since most entries are missing, those fake zeros dominate the matrix, and the SVD faithfully learns the pattern of the zeros instead of people's real taste.

(b) A better workflow. Model each rating as a dot product of a small user vector and a small movie vector (a rank-kkk model), and fit those vectors by minimising the squared error only over the ratings that were actually observed, with a regularisation penalty to stop overfitting. Pick the rank kkk and the penalty strength by validation.

(c) Validation. Hide some known ratings (or, to predict the future, train on older ratings and test on newer ones), fit on the rest, and measure the RMSE on the hidden ones. Training error alone is not enough: a more complex model always fits the training ratings better, even while its predictions on unseen ratings get worse — that is overfitting.

PCA: centre → covariance → top eigenvectors; the error of keeping kkk components is the sum of the dropped eigenvalues.
