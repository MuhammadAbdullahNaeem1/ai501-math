L09 · Multivariate & Matrix Calculus

Lecture 9 (1 November, second half; slides not yet uploaded — these notes follow the class notes and the outline's Week 8) · Deisenroth Ch. 5 · Boyd & Vandenberghe Appendix C

Course outline (Week 8) Read them all on this page ↓

The course now moves from linear algebra to calculus, which machine learning needs for one reason: to optimise — to find the parameters that make a loss as small as possible (Week 9). In order, this lecture covers: functions from Rn\mathbb R^nRn to Rm\mathbb R^mRm with their domain, codomain and range; the derivative as a slope and a rate of change, the second derivative, and the differentiation rules; partial derivatives and the gradient; gradients of linear and quadratic forms, ∇(x⊤x)=2x\nabla(x^\top x)=2x∇(x⊤x)=2x and ∇(x⊤Px)\nabla(x^\top Px)∇(x⊤Px), derived with index sums; the Jacobian and the Hessian; the multivariate chain rule; and Taylor series in one and many variables. We end with the payoff: least squares re-derived by setting a gradient to zero.

Intuition: Zoom in, zoom out

Zoom far enough into any smooth curve and it looks like a straight line; the slope of that line is the derivative. That is why the derivative is sometimes called "zooming in", and integration — adding up many tiny pieces — "zooming out". In many dimensions a smooth surface zoomed in looks like a flat plane, and the numbers that describe the tilt of that plane are the gradient. Everything in this lecture is about describing these local straight lines, planes and (one order further) bowls.

#1. Functions f:Rn→Rmf:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^mf:Rn→Rm

Definition: Domain, codomain, range

A function f:Rn→Rmf:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^mf:Rn→Rm maps each input x∈Rnx\in\mathbb R^nx∈Rn to exactly one output f(x)∈Rmf(x)\in\mathbb R^mf(x)∈Rm.

  • Domain: the set of allowed inputs.
  • Codomain: the set in which the outputs are declared to lie (here Rm\mathbb R^mRm — for a real-valued function, all of R\mathbb RR).
  • Range (image): the set of outputs that actually occur. It is a subset of the codomain, and often a strict one.
Worked example: Two scalar functions
  • f(x)=log⁡xf(x)=\log xf(x)=logx: domain (0,∞)(0,\infty)(0,∞) (only positive inputs), codomain and range R\mathbb RR. Logarithms compress large numbers: log⁡1010=1\log_{10}10=1log10​10=1, log⁡10100=2\log_{10}100=2log10​100=2, log⁡10106=6\log_{10}10^6=6log10​106=6, so a factor of ten becomes a step of one. This is why log scales and log-likelihoods are everywhere in ML.
  • f(x)=eaxf(x)=e^{ax}f(x)=eax with a≠0a\neq0a=0: domain R\mathbb RR, codomain R\mathbb RR, but range (0,∞)(0,\infty)(0,∞) — an exponential is never zero or negative. (For a=0a=0a=0 the range is just {1}\{1\}{1}.)

The four shapes. What a function looks like depends on nnn (inputs) and mmm (outputs):

m=1m=1m=1 (one output) m>1m>1m>1 (several outputs)
n=1n=1n=1 scalar function, a curve: f(x)=x2f(x)=x^2f(x)=x2 a curve in space: f(t)=(cos⁡t,sin⁡t)f(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)f(t)=(cost,sint)
n=2n=2n=2 a surface over the plane: f(x,y)=x2+y2f(x,y)=x^2+y^2f(x,y)=x2+y2 a map of the plane: f(x,y)=(xy, x+y)f(x,y)=(xy,\ x+y)f(x,y)=(xy, x+y)
n>2n>2n>2 a scalar field, e.g. a loss L(w)L(w)L(w) of many weights a vector field, e.g. a layer f(x)=Wx+bf(x)=Wx+bf(x)=Wx+b of a network

The ML cases that matter most are the bottom row: a loss takes many parameters to one number (m=1m=1m=1), and a network layer takes a vector to a vector (m>1m>1m>1).

Common trap

The graph of f(x,y)=x2+y2f(x,y)=x^2+y^2f(x,y)=x2+y2 is not two-dimensional: the domain is the 2-D plane, and the graph {(x,y,f(x,y))}\{(x,y,f(x,y))\}{(x,y,f(x,y))} is a surface in 3-D (a bowl). In general the graph of f:Rn→Rf:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb Rf:Rn→R lives in Rn+1\mathbb R^{n+1}Rn+1.

Remember

f:Rn→Rmf:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^mf:Rn→Rm; domain = allowed inputs, codomain = declared target, range = outputs that actually happen (⊆\subseteq⊆ codomain).

#3. Partial derivatives and the gradient

When fff depends on several variables, we can vary one at a time and hold the others fixed. That is a partial derivative, written with ∂\partial∂ instead of ddd to signal that fff has other inputs too.

Definition: Partial derivative and gradient

For f:Rn→Rf:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb Rf:Rn→R,
∂f∂xk=lim⁡h→0f(x1,…,xk+h,…,xn)−f(x)h.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_k}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x_1,\dots,x_k+h,\dots,x_n)-f(x)}{h}.∂xk​∂f​=h→0lim​hf(x1​,…,xk​+h,…,xn​)−f(x)​.
The gradient collects all of them in a column vector (the layout convention used in this course):
∇f(x)=[∂f/∂x1⋮∂f/∂xn]∈Rn.\nabla f(x)=\begin{bmatrix}\partial f/\partial x_1\\\vdots\\\partial f/\partial x_n\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^n .∇f(x)=​∂f/∂x1​⋮∂f/∂xn​​​∈Rn.

Worked example: The bowl f(x,y)=x2+y2f(x,y)=x^2+y^2f(x,y)=x2+y2

Treat yyy as a constant and differentiate in xxx: ∂f∂x=2x\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2x∂x∂f​=2x. Likewise ∂f∂y=2y\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}=2y∂y∂f​=2y. So ∇f=(2x,2y)\nabla f=(2x,2y)∇f=(2x,2y). At (1,2)(1,2)(1,2), ∇f=(2,4)\nabla f=(2,4)∇f=(2,4): the bowl rises fastest in the direction (1,2)(1,2)(1,2), i.e. straight away from the bottom.

A mixed one. f(x,y)=x2y+3yf(x,y)=x^2y+3yf(x,y)=x2y+3y:  ∂f∂x=2xy\ \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2xy ∂x∂f​=2xy (the 3y3y3y is a constant in xxx),  ∂f∂y=x2+3\ \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}=x^2+3 ∂y∂f​=x2+3.

What the gradient means. Near xxx, f(x+Δx)≈f(x)+∇f(x)⊤Δxf(x+\Delta x)\approx f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\top\Delta xf(x+Δx)≈f(x)+∇f(x)⊤Δx — the multivariate version of f′(x)Δxf'(x)\Delta xf′(x)Δx. Two consequences:

  • The directional derivative along a unit vector uuu is Duf=∇f⊤u=∥∇f∥cos⁡θD_uf=\nabla f^\top u=\|\nabla f\|\cos\thetaDu​f=∇f⊤u=∥∇f∥cosθ (L01's dot product). It is largest when uuu points along ∇f\nabla f∇f: the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, and −∇f-\nabla f−∇f in the direction of steepest descent — the idea behind gradient descent.
  • At a minimum or maximum every partial derivative is zero: ∇f=0\nabla f=\mathbf 0∇f=0.
Remember

∂f/∂xk\partial f/\partial x_k∂f/∂xk​: vary only xkx_kxk​. ∇f\nabla f∇f = column of partials = direction of steepest ascent; ∇f=0\nabla f=\mathbf 0∇f=0 at an optimum.

#5. The Jacobian

For a vector-valued f:Rn→Rmf:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^mf:Rn→Rm, each of the mmm outputs has nnn partial derivatives. Arrange them in an m×nm\times nm×n matrix: one row per output, one column per input.

#8. Taylor series

Taylor's idea: near a point aaa, approximate fff by a polynomial whose derivatives at aaa match those of fff, one order at a time.

Definition: Taylor series in one variable

f(x)=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)+f′′(a)2!(x−a)2+f′′′(a)3!(x−a)3+⋯f(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+\frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2+\frac{f'''(a)}{3!}(x-a)^3+\cdotsf(x)=f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)+2!f′′(a)​(x−a)2+3!f′′′(a)​(x−a)3+⋯

  • order 0, f(x)≈f(a)f(x)\approx f(a)f(x)≈f(a): exact only at x=ax=ax=a (or for a constant fff);
  • order 1, f(a)+f′(a)(x−a)f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)f(a)+f′(a)(x−a): the tangent line — exact if fff is linear;
  • order 2, adds 12f′′(a)(x−a)2\tfrac12f''(a)(x-a)^221​f′′(a)(x−a)2: the best-fitting parabola — exact if fff is quadratic;
  • and so on: each term captures one more order of change. A polynomial of degree ppp is reproduced exactly by its order-ppp expansion, because all higher derivatives are zero.
Note

A handy picture of "each order adds a bend": a polynomial of degree ppp has at most p−1p-1p−1 turning points, so a line (degree 1) has none, a parabola (degree 2) one, a cubic up to two. Higher-order Taylor terms let the approximation bend more and so follow fff further from aaa.
