## 1. The regression set-up

**Regression** = quantitative prediction on a **continuous scale**: given a data sample, predict a numerical value.

$$
x\ (\text{input})\ \longrightarrow\ \boxed{\text{Process or System } f(x)}\ \xrightarrow{\ \oplus\, n\ (\text{noise})\ }\ y\ (\text{observed output}),\qquad y=f(x)+n .
$$

The process/system is any underlying physical or logical phenomenon mapping inputs to noisy outputs. **True model**: we assume an inherent but **unknown** relationship $f$. **Goal**: given noisy observations, estimate the unknown functional relationship as accurately as possible. (Two 40-year-olds may exercise 3 and 5 hours — age cannot explain everything; $n$ is everything we didn't measure.)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| one-variable regression | output $y$ is a scalar (**we cover this**) |
| multi-variable regression | output $\mathbf y$ is a vector |
| single-feature regression | input $x$ is a scalar (**covered**) |
| multiple-feature regression | input $\mathbf x$ is a vector (**covered**) |

**Examples** — single feature: course score from hours of effort per week; monthly e-commerce sales vs advertising cost. Multiple features: machine efficiency from sensors (temperature, vibration); **remaining useful life (RUL) of a battery** from charge/discharge data; sales volume from demographics, GDP, climate; crop yield from satellite images and gravity data; surge pricing (Uber); driver fatigue/distraction from video; driving quality from car sensors.

**Model formulation.** Training data $D=\{(x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_n,y_n)\}\subseteq\mathcal X^d\times\mathcal Y$ (slide example: true $f(x)=4.2+2.4x$). The model $\hat y=\hat f(x,\theta)$ has (1) a **structure** chosen in advance (linear, polynomial, inverse, …) and (2) **parameters** $\theta=[\theta_1,\dots,\theta_M]$ tuned from data. The **model error** is $e=y-\hat y$.

:::def The modular recipe (linear regression is the course's second learning algorithm)
Unlike kNN, linear regression is **modular** — the approach used for most of the course:
1. **Select a model.** 2. **Define a loss function.** 3. **Formulate an optimisation problem**: find parameters minimising the loss. 4. **Solve it** — analytically (set the derivative to zero) or numerically (an iterative algorithm).
:::

:::remember
$y=f(x)+n$: we never see $f$, only noisy samples; choose a model structure, a loss, then optimise the parameters.
:::

## 2. The linear model

:::def What is linear?
$$
d=1:\ \hat f(x,\theta)=\theta_0+\theta_1x\ (\text{line});\qquad d=2:\ \theta_0+\theta_1x_1+\theta_2x_2\ (\text{plane});\qquad
\hat f(x,\theta)=\theta_0+\sum_{i=1}^d\theta_ix_i=\theta_0+\theta^\top x\ (\text{hyperplane in }\mathbb{R}^{d+1}).
$$
$\theta_0$ = **bias/intercept**; $\theta=[\theta_1,\dots,\theta_d]$ = **weights/slope**; $\theta_i$ quantifies the contribution of feature $x_i$. Number of parameters = $d+1$. Different $\theta_0,\theta$ give different hyperplanes — **how do we find the "best" one, and what does "best" mean?**
:::

**Matrix form.** Treat the intercept as a weight on a feature that is always 1:
$$
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\hat y_1\\ \hat y_2\\ \vdots\\ \hat y_n\end{bmatrix}}_{\hat{\mathbf y}}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&x_1^\top\\1&x_2^\top\\ \vdots&\vdots\\1&x_n^\top\end{bmatrix}}_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(d+1)}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\theta_0\\ \theta\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf w}.
$$
$X$ is the **design matrix** (one row per sample, a leading column of ones). **Always write shapes first**: 100 customers × 5 features ⇒ $X$ is $100\times6$, $w$ is $6\times1$, $X^\top X$ is $6\times6$.

### Slide example: hours of exercise per week

**Step 1 — one feature (age).** $y=w_0\cdot1+w_1x_1$; $w_0$ bias, $w_1$ weight for age.

| Age | 25 | 30 | 40 | 50 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exercise (h/week) | 4 | 5 | 3 | 2 |

$$
X=\begin{bmatrix}1&25\\1&30\\1&40\\1&50\end{bmatrix},\quad Y=\begin{bmatrix}4\\5\\3\\2\end{bmatrix},\quad W=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top Y\ \ (\text{normal equation}).
$$

**Step 2 — two features (age, sleep).** $y=w_0+w_1x_1+w_2x_2$ with sleep $=(7,6,6,5)$:
$$
X=\begin{bmatrix}1&25&7\\1&30&6\\1&40&6\\1&50&5\end{bmatrix},\qquad W=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top Y .
$$
(Both worked out in §5.)

:::remember
Linear regression predicts $\hat y=Xw$ with a column of ones in $X$ for the intercept; $d$ features give $d+1$ parameters.
:::

## 3. The loss function

The loss is a function of the **model parameters**. For input $x$ the residual error is $e=y-\hat y=y-\theta_0-\theta^\top x$ — the vertical gap between the observed value and the line.

:::def Three squared losses — one minimiser
$$
\text{LSE: }\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\theta_0-\theta^\top x_i)^2,\qquad
\text{MSE: }\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(\cdots)^2,\qquad
\text{RMSE: }\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(\cdots)^2}.
$$
Scaling by $1/n$ and taking a square root are increasing operations, so **all three have the same minimiser**. We minimise
$$
\underset{\theta_0,\theta}{\text{minimize}}\quad\mathcal L(\theta_0,\theta)=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\big(y_i-\theta_0-\theta^\top x_i\big)^2
$$
(unconstrained). The factor $\tfrac12$ is for mathematical convenience (it cancels the 2 from differentiation).
:::

**Why squares?** Signs would cancel in a plain sum; squares punish big errors more (4 costs 16× an error of 1), are smooth (differentiable everywhere, unlike $|e|$), give a clean formula, and are the right loss under Gaussian noise (probability part of the course).

**How to solve?** **Analytically**: find the critical point where the derivative (if it exists) is zero. **Numerically**: an algorithm that iteratively moves closer to the minimiser.

**Reformulation.** Stack residuals $e=[e_1,\dots,e_n]^\top$, $e_i=y_i-\theta_0-x_i^\top\theta$:
$$
\begin{bmatrix}e_1\\ \vdots\\ e_n\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}y_1\\ \vdots\\ y_n\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}1&x_1^\top\\ \vdots&\vdots\\1&x_n^\top\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\theta_0\\ \theta\end{bmatrix}
\ \Rightarrow\ e=y-Xw,\qquad
\mathcal L(w)=\tfrac12e^\top e=\tfrac12(y-Xw)^\top(y-Xw)=\tfrac12\|y-Xw\|_2^2 .
$$
"Very beautiful, elegant function": fitting = making $Xw$ as close as possible to $y$ in Euclidean distance.

:::remember
LSE, MSE and RMSE share one minimiser; write the loss as $\tfrac12\|y-Xw\|^2$ with residual $e=y-Xw$.
:::

## 4. The closed-form solution

### 4a. By calculus (slides p25–27)

Expand (using $(Xw)^\top=w^\top X^\top$, and that the scalar $y^\top Xw$ equals its transpose $w^\top X^\top y$):
$$
\mathcal L(w)=\tfrac12\big(y^\top y-y^\top Xw-w^\top X^\top y+w^\top X^\top Xw\big)=\tfrac12\big(y^\top y-2w^\top X^\top y+w^\top X^\top Xw\big).
$$

:::def Gradient
For $f:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$, $\nabla f(x)=\Big[\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\dots,\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_d}\Big]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^d$ — the rate of change along each direction; it is $0$ at a flat spot.
**Rules**: $\nabla(a^\top x)=\nabla(x^\top a)=a$; $\ \nabla(x^\top x)=2x$; $\ \nabla(x^\top Px)=2Px$ for symmetric $P$ (in general $(P+P^\top)x$).
:::

:::proof $\nabla\big(\tfrac12x^\top Ax+b^\top x\big)=Ax+b$ for symmetric $A$ (PSet 5, P1)
$f=\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}x_ix_j+\sum_ib_ix_i$, so $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_k}=\tfrac12\Big(\sum_jA_{kj}x_j+\sum_iA_{ik}x_i\Big)+b_k=(Ax)_k+b_k$ using $A=A^\top$.
Least squares is this form with $A=X^\top X$ (symmetric Gram matrix), $b=-X^\top y$, plus the constant $\tfrac12y^\top y$.
:::

Applying the rules:
$$
\nabla\mathcal L(w)=\tfrac12\big(-2X^\top y+2X^\top Xw\big)=X^\top Xw-X^\top y=0
\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{X^\top Xw=X^\top y}\ \ (\textbf{normal equations})\ \Longrightarrow\ \boxed{w=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y}.
$$
This $w$ minimises the LSE, MSE, RMSE and the norm of the residual: the **least-squares solution**. $(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top=X^\dagger$ is the **left pseudo-inverse** of L02.

### 4b. By orthogonal projection — no calculus (outline, Week 4)

The predictions $Xw$ always lie in the column space $\mathcal C(X)$ (L03). $y$ is usually not in it (tall $X$). The closest point of $\mathcal C(X)$ to $y$ is the **orthogonal projection** $\hat y$: the error $e=y-\hat y$ must be **orthogonal to every column of $X$**:
$$
X^\top(y-Xw)=0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ X^\top Xw=X^\top y .
$$
**Why closest** (Pythagoras): for any other $p=Xv\in\mathcal C(X)$, $y-p=e+(\hat y-p)$ with $\hat y-p\in\mathcal C(X)\perp e$, so $\|y-p\|^2=\|e\|^2+\|\hat y-p\|^2\ge\|e\|^2$. Same normal equations, derived geometrically — the course derives least squares **twice** on purpose (calculus again in L08).

**Projection matrix**: $\hat y=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y=Py$ with $P=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$, which is symmetric and satisfies $P^2=P$. For one column $u$ this is $\frac{uu^\top}{u^\top u}$ — the vector projection of L01.

**Sanity checks** (the first column of $X$ is all ones): $\mathbf 1^\top e=0$ ⇒ **residuals sum to zero**; the fitted line passes through the **point of averages** $(\bar x,\bar y)$.

### 4c. It is a minimum, and when the inverse exists

The Hessian (matrix of second derivatives) of $\mathcal L$ is $X^\top X$, and $v^\top X^\top Xv=\|Xv\|^2\ge0$ — the loss is a **bowl**, so the flat spot is the **global minimum**.

$X^\top X$ is invertible **iff the columns of $X$ are linearly independent** (L02 Gram-matrix proof). It fails with:
- **too little data**: need $n\ge d+1$;
- **redundant features** (one column a combination of others — height in cm and in m; feature 50 = 2 × feature 3).

Then $X^\top X$ is singular and there is a whole line/plane of equally good $w$ ⇒ remove the redundant feature or regularise (§10).

:::example 2024 Mid, Problem 9 — perfect multicollinearity
$x_2=2x_1$, $x_3=3x_1$, $x_4=4x_1$, $y=20x_1$ for $x_1=1,\dots,5$. With intercept, $X\in\mathbb{R}^{5\times5}$ has dependent columns ⇒ $\det(X^\top X)=0$ ⇒ $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ does not exist; the formula cannot be used directly.
Reduce: $y=\beta_0+\tilde\beta x_1$ with $\tilde\beta=\beta_1+2\beta_2+3\beta_3+4\beta_4$. $X_r=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&2\\1&3\\1&4\\1&5\end{bmatrix}$, $X_r^\top X_r=\begin{bmatrix}5&15\\15&55\end{bmatrix}$, inverse $\tfrac1{50}\begin{bmatrix}55&-15\\-15&5\end{bmatrix}$, $X_r^\top y=\begin{bmatrix}300\\1100\end{bmatrix}$ ⇒ $\begin{bmatrix}\beta_0\\ \tilde\beta\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\20\end{bmatrix}$. Individual $\beta_j$ are not identifiable; the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse picks the **minimum-norm** split.
:::

:::remember
Least squares: $X^\top Xw=X^\top y$, $w=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ — derived by setting $\nabla\mathcal L=0$ **or** by requiring the residual to be orthogonal to $\mathcal C(X)$; it needs independent columns.
:::

w=\frac1{1475}\begin{bmatrix}5625&-145\\-145&4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}14\\470\end{bmatrix}=\frac1{1475}\begin{bmatrix}10600\\-150\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}424/59\\-6/59\end{bmatrix}\approx\begin{bmatrix}7.186\\-0.1017\end{bmatrix}.

Interpretation: −0.10 h (≈6 min) per extra year; intercept = prediction at age 0 (meaningless); extrapolation is untrustworthy (age 80 ⇒ −0.95 h). Recording age in months divides $w_1$ by 12, predictions unchanged.

## 6. Polynomial regression

**Overview.** If the relationship between inputs and output is **not linear**, use a polynomial (single-feature case). Often called **non-linear regression** or, more accurately, **linear-in-parameters regression**. We also revisit over-fitting.

:::def Model
$d=1$, scalar input $x$:
$$
\hat f(x,\theta)=\theta_0+\theta_1x+\theta_2x^2+\dots+\theta_Mx^M=\sum_{i=0}^M\theta_ix^i=g(x)^\top\theta,\qquad g(x)=[1,x,x^2,\dots,x^M]^\top .
$$
$M$ = degree, $M+1$ coefficients. **$M$ is a hyper-parameter** that determines model complexity ($M=1$: linear regression; $M=0$: a constant, the mean).
:::

The model is **linear in the parameters** — that is all the least-squares derivation needed. $g(x)$ can be **any** fixed functions, e.g. $g(x)=[1/x,\ \sin(2\pi x),\ x^2,\ e^x,\dots]^\top$. With $n$ points:
$$
\begin{bmatrix}e_1\\ \vdots\\ e_n\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}y_1\\ \vdots\\ y_n\end{bmatrix}-\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&x_1&x_1^2&\cdots&x_1^M\\ \vdots&&&&\vdots\\1&x_n&x_n^2&\cdots&x_n^M\end{bmatrix}}_{X\ (\text{Vandermonde})}\begin{bmatrix}\theta_0\\ \vdots\\ \theta_M\end{bmatrix}
\ \Rightarrow\ e=y-X\theta,\quad \theta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y .
$$
"We have seen this before & we are capable to solve this!"

:::warn "Linear" means linear in the parameters
$y=w_0+w_1\sin x+w_2e^x$ **is** linear regression. $y=w_0+e^{w_1x}$ is **not** ($w_1$ is inside the exponential). A curve in $x$ is a straight line in the space of the new features (plot $y$ against $z=x^2$).
:::

:::example Age data with $M=0,1,2,3$

| $M$ | params | fit | training SSE |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | $3.5$ | 5.0 |
| 1 | 2 | $7.186-0.1017x$ | 1.19 |
| 2 | 3 | $1.819+0.2043x-0.004070x^2$ | 0.91 |
| 3 | 4 | $-59+5.403x-0.1470x^2+0.001267x^3$ | **0** |

The cubic (4 parameters, 4 points, square invertible Vandermonde $X$) interpolates exactly — and predicts 9.6 h/week at age 60. Zero training error is not success.
:::

:::remember
Polynomial regression = linear regression on features $1,x,\dots,x^M$; same normal equations, wider $X$; $M$ is a hyper-parameter.
:::

## 7. Underfitting, overfitting — the Bishop sine example

**Set-up** (Bishop PRML §1.1): process $f(x)=\sin(2\pi x)$, observations $y=f(x)+n$, $n=10$ points, model $\hat f(x,\theta)=\theta_0+\dots+\theta_Mx^M$. If $M$ is not known, **how do we choose it?**

- $M=0,1$: **underfitting — model too simple** (flat or tilted line).
- $M=3$: follows the sine well.
- $M=9$: **overfitting — model too complex**: zero residual error ($y=\hat f(x,\theta)$ at every point) but oscillating wildly, not close to the true function. The model fits the **data**, not the **true function**.

**Coefficients explode with $M$** (slide table):

| | $M=0$ | $M=1$ | $M=3$ | $M=9$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\theta_0$ | 0.19 | 0.82 | 0.31 | 0.35 |
| $\theta_1$ | | −1.27 | 7.99 | 232.37 |
| $\theta_2$ | | | −25.43 | −5321.83 |
| $\theta_3$ | | | 17.37 | 48568.31 |
| $\theta_4\ldots\theta_9$ | | | | −231639.30, 640042.26, −1061800.52, 1042400.18, −557682.99, 125201.43 |

**Puzzle**: $M=3$ is a special case of $M=9$ (set $\theta_4..\theta_9=0$) — so why is $M=9$ worse? Because 10 coefficients are tuned to 10 data points; least squares spends the extra freedom fitting the **noise**, with huge cancelling coefficients.

**How to tell overfitting without knowing the true function?**

- **Solution 1 — train/validation split.** Overfitting ⇒ poor generalisation ⇒ large validation/test error. Plot $E_{RMS}$ vs $M$: training error falls to 0 at $M=9$; validation error falls until $M\approx3$, stays flat to $M=8$, then **shoots up** at $M=9$ ⇒ a good choice is $M\approx3$. (Never pick $M$ by training error — it always prefers the most complex model.) With tiny data, use **leave-one-out**: for the 4 customers LOO-RMSE is 1.49 ($M=0$), **1.06** ($M=1$), 3.57 ($M=2$) — the line wins.
- **Solution 2 — more data.** $M=9$ with $n=10$ oscillates; $n=15$ settles; $n=100$ lies almost on $\sin(2\pi x)$. Overfitting happened because we did not have sufficient data for that many parameters ($M$, like $k$ in kNN, controls complexity; sticking to $M=3$ restricts the number of parameters).
- **Solution 3 — regularization** (§9).

:::def Underfitting vs overfitting
**Underfitting**: model too simple — large error on training **and** new data (high bias). **Overfitting**: model fits noise — tiny training error, large error on new data (high variance). Zero training error is guaranteed once $M\ge n-1$ (distinct $x$): that only proves the model can memorise.
:::

:::thm Bias–variance (outline Week 5; PSet 5 P8, PSet 6 P1)
Expected test error $=\text{Bias}^2+\text{Variance}+\text{Noise}$ (L01). More flexible models (higher $M$, smaller $\lambda$, smaller $k$) have lower bias and higher variance.
PSet 5 P8 ($n=100$, linear vs cubic): **training** error of the cubic is always $\le$ the linear model's (nested models), whatever the truth. If the truth is **linear**, expect the linear model to have lower **test** error (cubic adds variance, no bias reduction). If the truth is non-linear by an unknown amount, test error is **not determinable** — decide by validation.
:::

:::remember
Training error always falls with complexity; choose $M$ (or $\lambda$, $k$) on validation data — underfit = too simple, overfit = memorised noise with exploding coefficients.
:::

## 8. The closed form in practice

- **Don't compute the inverse.** Forming $X^\top X$ squares the sensitivity to rounding; libraries solve least squares with stable factorisations (`np.linalg.lstsq(X, y)` returns $(7.186,-0.1017)$ for the age data).
- **Scale your features.** Raw ages make the loss surface a long thin valley; centring age makes $X^\top X=\operatorname{diag}(4,368.75)$ and the condition number drops ~21 000 → ~92. For a cubic in raw age the condition number is ~$9\times10^{13}$; after standardising $z=(x-36.25)/9.60$ it is ~80. Standardising is essential for polynomial features. (The condition number measures how much input errors are magnified — the outline's "conditioning and numerical considerations".)
- **Cost**: forming $X^\top X$ ≈ $n(d+1)^2$, solving ≈ $(d+1)^3$; fine for hundreds of features, impractical for millions ⇒ iterative methods (gradient descent, L09). Linear regression is one of the few models with an analytical solution.
- **Battery RUL** workflow: features (cycles, temperature, charge rate) → linear model → polynomial features for non-linear degradation → keep **whole batteries** out for validation → standardise, use `lstsq`, report RMSE in cycles.

**So far and moving forward (slide p28):** we assumed a linear structure; #parameters = feature dimension + 1; loss from residual error; optimisation problem with an analytical solution; next, a numerical algorithm.

## 9. Regularization

**Overview.** Encourage the coefficients to be **small** by adding a **penalty term (regularizer)** to the error:
$$
\underset{\theta}{\text{minimize}}\quad\mathcal L_{reg}(\theta)=\mathcal L(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathcal R(\theta),\qquad\mathcal L(\theta)=\tfrac12\|y-X\theta\|_2^2 .
$$
$\lambda\ge0$ maintains the **trade-off** between the regularizer and the original loss (relative importance of the penalty). $\lambda=0$: ordinary least squares (OLS); $\lambda\to\infty$: all slopes → 0 (constant model $\bar y$). It cures three symptoms of one disease (too much freedom for the data): memorised noise, exploding coefficients, singular $X^\top X$.

### 9a. Ridge regression ($L^2$ / $\ell^2$ penalty)

:::def Ridge
$\mathcal R(\theta)=\tfrac12\|\theta\|_2^2$:
$$
\underset{\theta}{\text{minimize}}\quad\mathcal L_{reg}(\theta)=\tfrac12\|y-X\theta\|_2^2+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|_2^2 .
$$
Trade-off between **fit of the model to the data** (first term large if fitted poorly) and **squared norm of the coefficients** (second term large if coefficients are large). Large $\lambda$ penalises coefficient values more. **Intuitive interpretation: minimise the error while keeping the norm of the coefficients bounded.** (In deep learning: weight decay.)
:::

:::proof Closed form (slide p41; 2025 Mid P7a)
Still quadratic ⇒ closed form. $\nabla\mathcal L_{reg}(\theta)=\tfrac12\big(-2X^\top y+2X^\top X\theta+2\lambda\theta\big)=0$
$$
\Rightarrow X^\top X\theta+\lambda\theta=X^\top y\ \Rightarrow\ (X^\top X+\lambda I)\theta=X^\top y\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\theta(\lambda)=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y}.
$$
$\lambda=0$: the non-regularised solution. $\lambda=\infty$: the zero vector. Only change from OLS: $+\lambda$ on the diagonal of $X^\top X$ — the "ridge".
:::

:::example 2025 Mid, Problem 7(b)
$X=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$, $Y=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}$, $\lambda=1$:
$$
X^\top X=\begin{bmatrix}2&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix},\ X^\top X+I=\begin{bmatrix}3&3\\3&6\end{bmatrix},\ (\cdot)^{-1}=\frac19\begin{bmatrix}6&-3\\-3&3\end{bmatrix},\ X^\top Y=\begin{bmatrix}3\\5\end{bmatrix}\ \Rightarrow\ w_{ridge}=\frac19\begin{bmatrix}3\\6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1/3\\2/3\end{bmatrix}.
$$
(c) As $\lambda$ grows, the penalty pushes coefficients toward zero (more bias, less variance); $\lambda\to\infty\Rightarrow w_{ridge}\to0$. (OLS here: $w=(0,1)$ exactly.)
:::

:::warn Leave the intercept alone
Penalising $w_0$ would pull all predictions toward 0 (a point fixed by units, not data). Use $\lambda D$ with $D=\operatorname{diag}(0,1,\dots,1)$, or centre features and $y$ and use $\lambda I$ on the slopes. The textbook formula $(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$ applied to an $X$ containing the ones column **does** penalise the intercept (the 2025 mid used it that way — follow the question's loss).
:::

:::example Ridge on the age data, $\lambda=100$, intercept unpenalised
$$
\begin{bmatrix}4&145\\145&5625+100\end{bmatrix}w=\begin{bmatrix}14\\470\end{bmatrix},\ \det=1875\ \Rightarrow\ w=\frac1{1875}\begin{bmatrix}12000\\-150\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}6.4\\-0.08\end{bmatrix}.
$$
Slope shrinks −0.1017 → −0.08; training SSE rises 1.186 → 1.36 (must — OLS minimises SSE) but the ridge objective falls 1.110 → 1.00. Residuals still sum to 0 and the line still passes through $(\bar x,\bar y)$.
Centred shortcut: $w_1(\lambda)=\dfrac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}+\lambda}=\dfrac{S_{xx}}{S_{xx}+\lambda}\cdot\dfrac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}$ — the OLS slope times a **shrink factor** in $(0,1)$. $\lambda=368.75$ halves the slope; $\lambda\to\infty$ gives the constant 3.5. Features that barely vary (small $S_{xx}$) are shrunk most — exactly where the data carry least information.
PSet 6 P1: one feature, no intercept: $w^\star=\dfrac{\sum x_ny_n}{\sum x_n^2+\lambda}$, second derivative $\sum x_n^2+\lambda>0$ ⇒ minimum; increasing $\lambda$ ⇒ bias ↑, variance ↓.
:::

:::thm Ridge is always solvable
For $\lambda>0$ and $v\neq0$: $v^\top(X^\top X+\lambda I)v=\|Xv\|^2+\lambda\|v\|^2\ge\lambda\|v\|^2>0$ ⇒ $X^\top X+\lambda I$ is invertible ⇒ **unique** minimiser, even with $d>n$ or duplicate columns. With an exact duplicate feature, ridge **splits the weight equally** (symmetry + uniqueness). PSet 6 P2: identical features $x_{i1}=x_{i2}$ ⇒ $w_1^\star=w_2^\star=\dfrac{2ay_1}{4a^2+\lambda}$; $a=1,y_1=2,\lambda=1$ ⇒ $0.8$ each.
:::

**Slide example (sine, $M=9$):** too small $\lambda$ — no regularisation; too large $\lambda$ — no weight to the data. In practice $\lambda$ is very small, so use $\ln\lambda$ ($\lambda=e^{\ln\lambda}$):

| | $\ln\lambda=-\infty$ | $\ln\lambda=-18$ | $\ln\lambda=0$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\theta_0$ | 0.35 | 0.35 | 0.13 |
| $\theta_1$ | 232.37 | 4.74 | −0.05 |
| $\theta_2$ | −5321.83 | −0.77 | −0.06 |
| $\theta_3$ | 48568.31 | −31.97 | −0.05 |
| $\theta_4$ | −231639.30 | −3.89 | −0.03 |

$\lambda$ restricts the coefficients from exploding because the squared norm is in the loss. **$\lambda$ is a hyper-parameter learned using validation data**: the validation $E_{RMS}$ vs $\ln\lambda$ curve is U-shaped (overfit on the left, underfit on the right; minimum near $\ln\lambda\approx-30$ to $-25$ on the slide).

**Graphical visualisation (two coefficients $\theta_1,\theta_2$).** Contours of $\tfrac12\|y-X\theta\|^2$ are ellipses/circles around the OLS solution; contours of $\tfrac\lambda2\|\theta\|^2$ are circles around 0. The solution $\theta^\ast$ is where they touch. A good $\lambda$ avoids overfitting. **Irrelevant features get small but non-zero values** in the regularised solution — ideally we'd assign them **zero** weight ⇒ lasso.

:::warn Standardise before you penalise
Penalties are **not unit-free**: in months instead of years, $\lambda=100$ barely shrinks the age slope ($-0.1015$/year vs $-0.08$). Income in rupees (≈$10^5$) gets a tiny weight whose penalty is negligible, while "number of children" pays full price. Standardise features (training statistics), leave the intercept unpenalised.
:::

### 9b. Lasso regression ($L^1$ / $\ell^1$ penalty)

:::def Lasso
$\mathcal R(\theta)=\|\theta\|_1=\sum_i|\theta_i|$:
$$
\underset{\theta}{\text{minimize}}\quad\mathcal L_{reg}(\theta)=\tfrac12\|y-X\theta\|_2^2+\lambda\|\theta\|_1 .
$$
**L**east **a**bsolute **s**hrinkage and **s**election **o**perator. Graphically the constraint region is a **diamond**; the intersection with the error contours is at a **corner** of the diamond, which lies on an axis ⇒ some weights exactly 0 ⇒ **sparse solution** ("ridge shrinks, lasso selects").
:::

**Why exact zeros — algebra (soft thresholding).** One centred feature, $a=\sum x_i^2$, $b=\sum x_iy_i$: minimise $f(w)=\tfrac a2w^2-bw+\lambda|w|$.
- $w>0$: $f'=aw-b+\lambda=0\Rightarrow w=(b-\lambda)/a$, valid if $b>\lambda$.
- $w<0$: $w=(b+\lambda)/a$, valid if $b<-\lambda$.
- otherwise ($|b|\le\lambda$) the minimum is at the **kink** $w=0$ (slope $\ge0$ to the right, $\le0$ to the left).

$$
w^\star_{lasso}=\frac{S(b,\lambda)}{a},\quad S(b,\lambda)=\operatorname{sign}(b)\max(|b|-\lambda,0);\qquad w^\star_{ridge}=\frac{b}{a+\lambda};\qquad w^\star_{OLS}=\frac ba .
$$
Ridge shrinks by a **factor**, lasso by an **amount** and sets weights with weak evidence ($|b|\le\lambda$) to **exactly zero**. Example $a=2,b=5,\lambda=6$: OLS 2.5, ridge $5/8=0.625$, lasso 0 (zero for every $\lambda\ge5$). Age data: $w_1=-\max(37.5-\lambda,0)/368.75$, hitting 0 at $\lambda=37.5$.

**Many weights**: no closed form; **coordinate descent** — cycle through $j$, set $w_j\leftarrow S(x_j^\top r^{(j)},\lambda)/x_j^\top x_j$ with $r^{(j)}$ the residual ignoring feature $j$; convex ⇒ converges to the global minimum. Lasso optimisation is **computationally more expensive** than ridge. Age+sleep (standardised): sleep drops out exactly at $\lambda\approx0.40$, age at $\lambda=3.906$; ridge keeps both. Simulated 12-feature example (3 real): lasso zeroed 5 of 9 noise features and predicted best.

:::example 2024 Mid, Problem 5 — Lasso vs Ridge (6 pts)
(a) **Lasso** ($\|\theta\|_1$) can shrink some coefficients **exactly to zero** ⇒ sparse model, feature selection. **Ridge** ($\|\theta\|_2^2$) shrinks toward zero but **not exactly** to zero ⇒ keeps all features with reduced impact.
(b) $\mathcal L(\theta)=\tfrac12\|A\theta-y\|^2+\lambda\|\theta\|_1$; the $L^1$ penalty is non-differentiable at 0 — a sharp corner/kink — making it likely that the optimum sets coefficients exactly to zero.
(c) Increasing $\lambda$ strengthens the penalty ⇒ more coefficients driven to zero (more sparsity); decreasing $\lambda$ ⇒ more features kept. $\lambda$ balances complexity and sparsity.
:::

### 9c. Elastic net and comparison

:::def Elastic net — hybrid of $L^1$ and $L^2$
$$
\underset{\theta}{\text{minimize}}\quad\mathcal L_{reg}(\theta)=\tfrac12\|y-X\theta\|_2^2+\lambda_1\|\theta\|_1+\tfrac{\lambda_2}{2}\|\theta\|_2^2 .
$$
Ridge and lasso are special cases. Combines both strengths (selects like lasso, keeps correlated groups together like ridge) but requires tuning **two** hyper-parameters $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ on validation data.
:::

| | Ridge $\tfrac\lambda2\|w\|_2^2$ | Lasso $\lambda\|w\|_1$ |
|---|---|---|
| Penalty | error + λ × sum of **squares** of coefficients | error + λ × sum of **absolute values** |
| Solution | closed form $(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$ | iterative (coordinate descent); more expensive |
| Weights | shrinks all by a factor, none exactly 0 | shrinks by an amount, many exactly 0 (**sparse**, hence **biased**) |
| Geometry | disc (round) | diamond (corners on axes) |
| Uniqueness | always unique for $\lambda>0$ | can be non-unique with perfectly correlated features |
| Correlated features | spreads weight | picks one |
| Best when | many small effects | few features matter |

2024 Mid MCQ 9: the regularizer that promotes a **sparse** weight vector is **$L^1$**. MCQ 14 (diamond figure): sparse, $L^1$ penalty — not ridge. 2025 MCQ 21: ridge formula ✓; coefficients shrink as λ grows ✓; ridge sets some exactly zero ✗; ridge increases variance ✗ (it **decreases** variance, increases bias). Bias–variance trade-off is controlled by λ and by polynomial degree (2024 MCQ 6). To reduce variance: add more data ✓, lower model complexity ✓ (MCQ 5).

## 10. Choosing $\lambda$

- **Never on training error** — it always picks $\lambda=0$.
- **Leave-one-out** on tiny data. For the 4 customers (standardised age, unpenalised intercept), LOO-RMSE: line 1.06 / 1.05 / **1.03** / 1.04 / 1.15 / 1.33 for $\lambda=0,0.1,0.3,1,3,10$; parabola 3.57 → **1.06** at $\lambda=1$; cubic best 1.29 at $\lambda=3$. Regularisation rescues the parabola; the best model is still a slightly penalised line. Degree and penalty are chosen together.
- **$k$-fold cross-validation**: grid of $\lambda$ (even in $\ln\lambda$); fit on $k-1$ folds, validate on the rest, average; pick the best $\lambda$, refit on all training data, report on the untouched test set. Compute standardisation inside folds; split grouped data by group.
- **In practice**: ridge = OLS on augmented data $\tilde X=\begin{bmatrix}X\\0\ \ \sqrt\lambda I_d\end{bmatrix}$, $\tilde y=\begin{bmatrix}y\\0\end{bmatrix}$ ($d$ imaginary customers pulling slopes to 0) — solve with `lstsq`. A gradient step for ridge multiplies weights by $(1-\eta\lambda)$ ⇒ "weight decay". Probabilistically, ridge = Gaussian prior, lasso = Laplace prior (MAP, L13).
