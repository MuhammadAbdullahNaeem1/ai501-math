This lecture builds the course's second learning algorithm and the template for most of what follows. In order, it covers: the regression set-up of process, noise, model and error; the four-step modular recipe; what "linear" means, with the fitness-tracker example; the loss functions LSE, MSE and RMSE and why they share a minimiser; the closed-form least-squares solution, derived **twice** — by calculus and by orthogonal projection — together with the conditions under which it exists; polynomial regression as linear regression in disguise; underfitting, overfitting and their three remedies in Bishop's sine example; bias and variance; and regularization — ridge regression with its closed form and geometry, the lasso and its sparsity, the elastic net — and how to choose the penalty.

:::background functions, noise and models
**A function** is a rule that turns an input into an output, such as $f(x)=4.2+2.4x$: input $x=1$ gives $6.6$.

**Noise.** Real measurements never sit exactly on a neat rule. Two 40-year-olds may exercise 3 and 5 hours a week; age alone cannot explain the difference. We write the observed value as "rule plus a random wobble":
$$
y=f(x)+n .
$$
**A model** is our *guess* at the unknown rule $f$. We pick its **shape** in advance (for example, a straight line $\theta_0+\theta_1x$) and then choose the **numbers** $\theta_0,\theta_1$ — the parameters — to fit the data as well as possible.

**The error (residual)** for one data point is the vertical gap between what was observed and what the model predicts: $e=y-\hat y$.
:::




**Regression** is quantitative prediction on a **continuous scale**: given a data sample, predict a numerical value. We picture the data as produced by an unknown process corrupted by noise:
$$
x\ \longrightarrow\ \boxed{\ \text{process or system } f(x)\ }\ \longrightarrow\ \oplus\ \longleftarrow\ n\ \ (\text{noise}),
\qquad\qquad
y=f(x)+n .
$$
Here the *process or system* is whatever physical or logical phenomenon maps the inputs to the observed, noisy outputs. **The true model** is an inherent but **unknown** relationship $f$ between input and output. **The goal** is to estimate that unknown functional relationship, as accurately as possible, from noisy observations. (Two customers of the same age may exercise three and five hours a week: age cannot explain everything, and the noise $n$ stands for all that we did not measure.)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| one-variable regression | the output $y$ is a scalar (**covered in this course**) |
| multi-variable regression | the output $\mathbf y$ is a vector |
| single-feature regression | the input $x$ is a scalar (**covered**) |
| multiple-feature regression | the input $\mathbf x$ is a vector (**covered**) |

**Examples.** With a single feature: a course score from the hours of effort per week, or monthly e-commerce sales from advertising spend. With several features: machine efficiency from temperature and vibration sensors; the **remaining useful life of a battery** from its charging and discharging history; sales volume from demographics, GDP and climate; crop yield from satellite and gravity data; surge pricing in ride-hailing; driver fatigue from video; and driving quality from in-car sensors.

**Formulating the model.** The training data are $D=\{(x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_n,y_n)\}\subseteq\mathcal X^d\times\mathcal Y$; the slide's example uses the true function $f(x)=4.2+2.4x$. Our model $\hat y=\hat f(x,\theta)$ has two ingredients: a **structure** chosen in advance (linear, polynomial, inverse, …) and **parameters** $\theta=[\theta_1,\dots,\theta_M]$ tuned from the data. The **model error** is $e=y-\hat y$.

:::def The modular recipe
Linear regression is the course's second learning algorithm and, unlike kNN, it is **modular**. Almost every later method follows the same four steps:

1. **Select a model.**
2. **Define a loss function** that measures how badly the model fits the data.
3. **Formulate an optimisation problem:** find the parameters that minimise the loss.
4. **Solve it**, either analytically (set the derivative to zero) or numerically (an algorithm that moves step by step towards the minimiser).
:::

:::remember
$y=f(x)+n$: we never see $f$, only noisy samples of it; we choose a model structure and a loss, and then optimise the parameters.
:::

## 2. The linear model

:::figs
l05-p11|Lines, planes, hyperplanes
l05-p20|Linear model in matrix form
:::

:::background building the design matrix by hand
**A line** $\hat y=w_0+w_1x$: $w_0$ is where the line crosses the vertical axis (the intercept) and $w_1$ is how much $\hat y$ rises when $x$ rises by $1$ (the slope).

**Why the column of ones?** We want to write all the predictions as one matrix product. The trick is to treat the intercept as "$w_0$ times $1$":
$$
\hat y_i=w_0\cdot1+w_1x_i .
$$
For the four customers aged $25,30,40,50$:
$$
\begin{bmatrix}\hat y_1\\ \hat y_2\\ \hat y_3\\ \hat y_4\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}w_0\cdot1+w_1\cdot25\\ w_0\cdot1+w_1\cdot30\\ w_0\cdot1+w_1\cdot40\\ w_0\cdot1+w_1\cdot50\end{bmatrix}
=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&25\\1&30\\1&40\\1&50\end{bmatrix}}_{X}\begin{bmatrix}w_0\\w_1\end{bmatrix}.
$$
Each row of $X$ is "$1$, then that person's features". With two features, each row becomes $(1,\ \text{age},\ \text{sleep})$ and $w$ gets a third entry.
:::




:::def What "linear" means
$$
\begin{aligned}
d=1:&\quad \hat f(x,\theta)=\theta_0+\theta_1x &&(\text{a line}),\\
d=2:&\quad \hat f(x,\theta)=\theta_0+\theta_1x_1+\theta_2x_2 &&(\text{a plane}),\\
\text{general } d:&\quad \hat f(x,\theta)=\theta_0+\sum_{i=1}^d\theta_ix_i=\theta_0+\theta^\top x &&(\text{a hyperplane in }\mathbb{R}^{d+1}).
\end{aligned}
$$
The parameter $\theta_0$ is the **bias** or **intercept**; $\theta=[\theta_1,\dots,\theta_d]$ are the **weights** or slopes; and $\theta_i$ measures the contribution of feature $x_i$. There are $d+1$ parameters in all. Different choices give different hyperplanes, which raises the question: **how do we find the "best" one, and what does "best" mean?**
:::

**Matrix form.** Treat the intercept as the weight of an extra feature that always equals $1$, and stack the $n$ predictions:
$$
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\hat y_1\\ \hat y_2\\ \vdots\\ \hat y_n\end{bmatrix}}_{\hat{\mathbf y}}
=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&x_1^\top\\1&x_2^\top\\ \vdots&\vdots\\1&x_n^\top\end{bmatrix}}_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(d+1)}}
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\theta_0\\ \theta\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf w}.
$$
The matrix $X$ is the **design matrix**: one row per sample, one column per feature, plus the leading column of ones. **Always write down the shapes first.** With $100$ customers and $5$ features, $X$ is $100\times6$, $w$ is $6\times1$, and $X^\top X$ is $6\times6$.

## 3. The loss function

:::figs
l05-p21|Residual errors
l05-p24|Loss in vector form
:::

:::background why a squared length is $e^\top e$
**One error squared:** $e_i^2$. **All of them added up:** $e_1^2+e_2^2+\cdots+e_n^2$.

From L01, that sum is exactly the squared length of the error vector, and also the inner product of the error vector with itself:
$$
e_1^2+\cdots+e_n^2=\|e\|^2=e^\top e .
$$
And the error vector is "observed minus predicted", $e=y-Xw$. Putting the two together,
$$
\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\hat y_i)^2=\|y-Xw\|^2 .
$$
So "make the total squared error small" is the same as "make the vector $Xw$ close to the vector $y$".
:::




The loss must be a function of the **model parameters**. For an input $x$, the residual error is
$$
e=y-\hat y=y-\theta_0-\theta^\top x ,
$$
which is the vertical gap between the observed value and the fitted line.

:::def Three squared losses with one minimiser
$$
\text{LSE}=\sum_{i=1}^n\big(y_i-\theta_0-\theta^\top x_i\big)^2,
\qquad
\text{MSE}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big(y_i-\theta_0-\theta^\top x_i\big)^2,
\qquad
\text{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big(y_i-\theta_0-\theta^\top x_i\big)^2}.
$$
Dividing by $n$ and taking a square root are both increasing operations, so **all three losses have the same minimiser**. We therefore solve the unconstrained problem
$$
\underset{\theta_0,\,\theta}{\text{minimize}}\quad \mathcal L(\theta_0,\theta)=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\big(y_i-\theta_0-\theta^\top x_i\big)^2 ,
$$
where the factor $\tfrac12$ is a mathematical convenience: it cancels the $2$ produced by differentiation.
:::

**Why squares?** A plain sum of residuals would let positive and negative errors cancel. Squares remove the sign; they punish large errors more heavily (an error of $4$ costs sixteen times an error of $1$); they are smooth, unlike $|e|$, which has a kink at $0$; they lead to a clean formula; and, as the probability part of the course will show, they are the natural loss when the noise is Gaussian.

**How to solve the problem?** **Analytically**, by finding the critical point where the derivative (if it exists) is zero; or **numerically**, by an algorithm that iteratively approaches the minimiser.

**Reformulation in vector form.** Stack the residuals $e_i=y_i-\theta_0-x_i^\top\theta$ into a vector:
$$
\begin{bmatrix}e_1\\ \vdots\\ e_n\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}y_1\\ \vdots\\ y_n\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}1&x_1^\top\\ \vdots&\vdots\\1&x_n^\top\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\theta_0\\ \theta\end{bmatrix},
\qquad\text{that is,}\qquad e=y-Xw .
$$
The loss then takes the compact form
$$
\mathcal L(w)=\tfrac12\,e^\top e=\tfrac12\,(y-Xw)^\top(y-Xw)=\tfrac12\,\|y-Xw\|_2^2 .
$$
In the slide's words, this is a "very beautiful, elegant function": fitting the model means making $Xw$ as close as possible to $y$ in Euclidean distance.

:::remember
LSE, MSE and RMSE share a single minimiser; write the loss as $\tfrac12\|y-Xw\|_2^2$ with residual $e=y-Xw$.
:::

## 4. The closed-form solution

:::background derivatives, partial derivatives and the gradient — from scratch
#### Derivatives of one variable
The derivative $f'(x)$ is the **slope** of $f$ at $x$. At the bottom of a valley the slope is zero, which is how we find minimum points. The rules you need:
$$
\frac{d}{dx}c=0,\qquad \frac{d}{dx}(ax)=a,\qquad \frac{d}{dx}(ax^2)=2ax,\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\big(\tfrac12ax^2\big)=ax .
$$
**Example.** $f(w)=(w-3)^2=w^2-6w+9$ has $f'(w)=2w-6$, which is zero at $w=3$ — the bottom of the parabola.

#### Partial derivatives
When a function has several inputs, $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}$ is the slope in the $x_1$ direction: **treat every other variable as a constant** and differentiate as usual.
$$
f(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2+3x_1x_2+5x_2\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}=2x_1+3x_2,\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}=3x_1+5 .
$$
**Term by term**, for $\partial f/\partial x_1$ — pretend $x_2$ is a fixed number such as $7$:

| Term | With $x_2$ held fixed it looks like… | Derivative with respect to $x_1$ |
|---|---|---|
| $x_1^2$ | $x_1^2$ | $2x_1$ |
| $3x_1x_2$ | $(3x_2)\cdot x_1$ — a constant times $x_1$, like $21\,x_1$ | $3x_2$ |
| $5x_2$ | a constant with no $x_1$ in it, like $35$ | $0$ |

Adding the column gives $2x_1+3x_2$. **Check with $x_2=7$:** then $f=x_1^2+21x_1+35$, whose derivative is $2x_1+21$, matching $2x_1+3\cdot7$.

For $\partial f/\partial x_2$, hold $x_1$ fixed instead: $x_1^2\to0$, $\ 3x_1x_2\to3x_1$, $\ 5x_2\to5$, giving $3x_1+5$.

#### The gradient
The gradient $\nabla f$ stacks all the partial derivatives into one vector:
$$
\nabla f=\begin{bmatrix}\partial f/\partial x_1\\ \partial f/\partial x_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2x_1+3x_2\\3x_1+5\end{bmatrix}.
$$
At a minimum of a smooth function, **every** slope is zero, so $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$. Solving $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$ is the vector version of solving $f'(x)=0$.

#### A transpose fact used in the expansion
A $1\times1$ matrix is just a number, and a number equals its own transpose. Since $y^\top Xw$ is a single number, $y^\top Xw=(y^\top Xw)^\top=w^\top X^\top y$.
:::


### 4a. By calculus (slides p25–27)

**Step 1 — expand the loss.** Using $(Xw)^\top=w^\top X^\top$,
$$
\mathcal L(w)=\tfrac12\big(y^\top y-y^\top Xw-w^\top X^\top y+w^\top X^\top Xw\big).
$$
The two middle terms are scalars, and a scalar equals its own transpose, so $y^\top Xw=(y^\top Xw)^\top=w^\top X^\top y$. Hence
$$
\mathcal L(w)=\tfrac12\big(y^\top y-2\,w^\top X^\top y+w^\top X^\top Xw\big).
$$

:::def The gradient
For a function $f:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathbb{R}$, the gradient is the vector of partial derivatives,
$$
\nabla f(x)=\left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1},\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2},\ \dots,\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_d}\right]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^d ,
$$
which records the rate of change of $f$ along each direction; it is the zero vector at a flat point. Three rules are needed:
$$
\nabla\big(a^\top x\big)=\nabla\big(x^\top a\big)=a,\qquad
\nabla\big(x^\top x\big)=2x,\qquad
\nabla\big(x^\top Px\big)=2Px\quad\text{for symmetric }P
$$
(for a general $P$ the last gradient is $(P+P^\top)x$).
:::

:::proof The gradient of a quadratic (PSet 5, Problem 1)
:::origc [PSet 5 · Problem 1 ↗](pdfs/psets/PSet-05.pdf#page=1)
Let $f(x)=\tfrac12x^\top Ax+b^\top x$ with $A$ symmetric. (a) Find $\nabla f(x)$. *(Hint: differentiate with respect to one coordinate $x_k$ and use the symmetry of $A$.)* (b) Write the least-squares loss $\mathcal L(\theta)=\tfrac12\|y-X\theta\|_2^2$ in this form (up to a constant), and use (a) to find $\nabla\mathcal L(\theta)$.
:::
**The claim.** If $f(x)=\tfrac12x^\top Ax+b^\top x$ with $A$ symmetric, then $\nabla f(x)=Ax+b$. This is the vector version of the school rule $\frac{d}{dx}\big(\tfrac12ax^2+bx\big)=ax+b$.

**Step 1 — see it in two dimensions.** Take
$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\ (\text{symmetric}),\qquad b=\begin{bmatrix}b_1\\b_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad x=\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}.
$$
Multiply out the quadratic form: first $Ax$, then $x^\top(Ax)$.
$$
Ax=\begin{bmatrix}ax_1+cx_2\\cx_1+dx_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad
x^\top Ax=x_1(ax_1+cx_2)+x_2(cx_1+dx_2)=ax_1^2+2c\,x_1x_2+dx_2^2 .
$$
So $f$ is an ordinary function of two numbers:
$$
f(x_1,x_2)=\tfrac12\big(ax_1^2+2c\,x_1x_2+dx_2^2\big)+b_1x_1+b_2x_2 .
$$
Take each partial derivative, treating the other variable as a constant:
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}=\tfrac12\big(2ax_1+2cx_2\big)+b_1=ax_1+cx_2+b_1,\qquad
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}=\tfrac12\big(2cx_1+2dx_2\big)+b_2=cx_1+dx_2+b_2 .
$$
Stack them into the gradient — and recognise $Ax$:
$$
\nabla f(x)=\begin{bmatrix}ax_1+cx_2+b_1\\cx_1+dx_2+b_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}a&c\\c&d\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}b_1\\b_2\end{bmatrix}=Ax+b .
$$
Notice where the $\tfrac12$ went: the cross term $2c\,x_1x_2$ appears **twice** in $x^\top Ax$ (once from $A_{12}$, once from $A_{21}$), and each square $ax_1^2$ produces a $2$ when differentiated. The $\tfrac12$ cancels exactly that $2$.

**Step 2 — the same argument for any $n$.** Since $(Ax)_i=\sum_jA_{ij}x_j$, the quadratic form is a double sum, one term for every entry of $A$:
$$
x^\top Ax=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i(Ax)_i=\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n A_{ij}\,x_ix_j .
$$
Differentiate one term $A_{ij}x_ix_j$ with respect to $x_k$. It depends on $x_k$ only if $i=k$ or $j=k$ (or both):
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}\big(x_ix_j\big)=\underbrace{[\,i=k\,]\,x_j}_{x_k\text{ is the first factor}}+\underbrace{[\,j=k\,]\,x_i}_{x_k\text{ is the second factor}} ,
$$
where $[\,i=k\,]$ is $1$ when $i=k$ and $0$ otherwise. (When $i=j=k$ both brackets fire, giving $2x_k$, the derivative of $x_k^2$.) Summing over all $i,j$, the first bracket keeps only row $k$ of $A$ and the second keeps only column $k$:
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}\,x^\top Ax=\underbrace{\sum_{j}A_{kj}x_j}_{\text{row }k\text{ of }A\text{ times }x}+\underbrace{\sum_{i}A_{ik}x_i}_{\text{column }k\text{ of }A\text{ times }x}=(Ax)_k+(A^\top x)_k .
$$
In the $2\times2$ case with $k=1$, the four terms are $a\,x_1x_1$, $c\,x_1x_2$, $c\,x_2x_1$ and $d\,x_2x_2$; their derivatives are $2ax_1$, $cx_2$, $cx_2$ and $0$, which add to $(ax_1+cx_2)+(ax_1+cx_2)$ — row 1 times $x$ plus column 1 times $x$.

**Step 3 — use symmetry and the linear term.** Because $A=A^\top$, column $k$ equals row $k$, so the two sums are equal and
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}\Big(\tfrac12x^\top Ax\Big)=\tfrac12\cdot2\,(Ax)_k=(Ax)_k .
$$
The linear term is easier: $b^\top x=\sum_ib_ix_i$, and only the $i=k$ term contains $x_k$, so $\frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}b^\top x=b_k$. Putting the two together and stacking over $k=1,\dots,n$,
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_k}=(Ax)_k+b_k\quad\text{for every }k\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\nabla f(x)=Ax+b}.
$$
(For a non-symmetric $A$, Step 2 gives $\nabla\big(\tfrac12x^\top Ax\big)=\tfrac12(A+A^\top)x$ instead.)

**Step 4 — why this solves least squares.** Expanding the loss,
$$
\tfrac12\|y-X\theta\|^2=\tfrac12\theta^\top\underbrace{(X^\top X)}_{A}\theta+\underbrace{(-X^\top y)}_{b}{}^{\!\top}\theta+\tfrac12y^\top y ,
$$
which is exactly $f$ with $A=X^\top X$ (symmetric, being a Gram matrix) and $b=-X^\top y$; the constant $\tfrac12y^\top y$ has zero gradient. Therefore
$$
\nabla\mathcal L(\theta)=X^\top X\theta-X^\top y .
$$
:::

**Step 2 — differentiate and set the gradient to zero.** Applying the three rules term by term,
$$
\nabla\mathcal L(w)=\tfrac12\big(-2X^\top y+2X^\top Xw\big)=X^\top Xw-X^\top y .
$$
Setting this equal to zero gives the **normal equations** and, when $X^\top X$ is invertible, their solution:
$$
\boxed{\;X^\top Xw=X^\top y\;}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\boxed{\;w=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\;}
$$
This $w$ minimises the LSE, the MSE, the RMSE and the norm of the residual simultaneously; it is called the **least-squares solution**. The matrix $(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top=X^\dagger$ is the **left pseudo-inverse** of L02.

### 4b. By orthogonal projection — no calculus (outline, Week 4)

Every prediction vector $Xw$ lies in the column space $\mathcal C(X)$ (L03), while $y$, for a tall $X$, usually does not. The point of $\mathcal C(X)$ closest to $y$ is the **orthogonal projection** $\hat y$, and the defining property of an orthogonal projection is that the error $e=y-\hat y$ is **orthogonal to every column of $X$**:
$$
X^\top(y-Xw)=\mathbf 0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad X^\top Xw=X^\top y .
$$
These are the same normal equations, obtained this time by geometry alone.

**Why the projection is the closest point.** Take any other point $p=Xv\in\mathcal C(X)$. Then
$$
y-p=\underbrace{(y-\hat y)}_{e}+\underbrace{(\hat y-p)}_{\in\,\mathcal C(X)},
$$
and the two pieces are orthogonal, so Pythagoras gives
$$
\|y-p\|^2=\|e\|^2+\|\hat y-p\|^2\ \ge\ \|e\|^2 .
$$
The course derives least squares twice on purpose; the calculus derivation returns once more in L08.

**The projection matrix.** The fitted values are $\hat y=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y=Py$, where $P=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$ is symmetric and satisfies $P^2=P$. For a single column $u$ it reduces to $P=\dfrac{uu^\top}{u^\top u}$, the vector projection of L01.

**Two sanity checks.** Because the first column of $X$ is the vector of ones, the condition $\mathbf 1^\top e=0$ says that **the residuals sum to zero**, and it follows that the fitted line passes through the **point of averages** $(\bar x,\bar y)$.

### 4c. It is a minimum, and when the inverse exists

The Hessian — the matrix of second derivatives — of $\mathcal L$ is $X^\top X$, and for every $v$
$$
v^\top X^\top Xv=\|Xv\|_2^2\ \ge\ 0 .
$$
The loss therefore curves upwards in every direction; it is a **bowl**, and its flat point is the **global minimum**.

$X^\top X$ is invertible **exactly when the columns of $X$ are linearly independent** (the Gram-matrix argument of L02). This fails in two situations:

- **too little data:** we need $n\ge d+1$, at least as many samples as parameters;
- **redundant features:** if a column is a combination of others — height in centimetres and in metres, or feature $50$ equal to twice feature $3$ — then $X^\top X$ is singular and there is a whole line or plane of equally good $w$.

The remedies are to remove the redundant feature or to regularise (§9).

Note the perfect multicollinearity: $x_2=2x_1$, $x_3=3x_1$, $x_4=4x_1$. (a) Attempt a standard linear regression of $y$ on $x_1,\dots,x_4$ without regularisation, and explain why the pseudo-inverse $(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$ cannot be used directly. (b) Remove the redundant features, formulate a model relating input and output, and use the pseudo-inverse to determine its parameters.
:::
The data satisfy $x_2=2x_1$, $x_3=3x_1$, $x_4=4x_1$ and $y=20x_1$, for $x_1=1,\dots,5$.

**(a)** With an intercept, $X\in\mathbb{R}^{5\times5}$ has dependent columns, so $\det(X^\top X)=0$; the inverse $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ does not exist, and the formula cannot be applied directly.

**(b)** Reduce the model to $y=\beta_0+\tilde\beta x_1$, where $\tilde\beta=\beta_1+2\beta_2+3\beta_3+4\beta_4$. Then
$$
X_r=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&2\\1&3\\1&4\\1&5\end{bmatrix},\qquad
X_r^\top X_r=\begin{bmatrix}5&15\\15&55\end{bmatrix},\qquad
(X_r^\top X_r)^{-1}=\frac{1}{50}\begin{bmatrix}55&-15\\-15&5\end{bmatrix},\qquad
X_r^\top y=\begin{bmatrix}300\\1100\end{bmatrix},
$$
and therefore
$$
\begin{bmatrix}\beta_0\\ \tilde\beta\end{bmatrix}=\frac1{50}\begin{bmatrix}55&-15\\-15&5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}300\\1100\end{bmatrix}=\frac1{50}\begin{bmatrix}16500-16500\\-4500+5500\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\20\end{bmatrix}.
$$
The individual $\beta_j$ cannot be identified; the Moore–Penrose pseudo-inverse would choose the split of $\tilde\beta=20$ with the **smallest norm**.
:::




:::derive expanding $(y-Xw)^\top(y-Xw)$, bracket by bracket
Treat it like $(a-b)(a-b)=a^2-ab-ba+b^2$, keeping the order of the matrix products.

**Step 1 — transpose the first bracket.** $(y-Xw)^\top=y^\top-(Xw)^\top=y^\top-w^\top X^\top$.

**Step 2 — multiply the four pairs:**
$$
(y^\top-w^\top X^\top)(y-Xw)=y^\top y-y^\top Xw-w^\top X^\top y+w^\top X^\top Xw .
$$
**Step 3 — combine the two middle terms.** Each is a single number and they are transposes of each other, so they are equal:
$$
=y^\top y-2\,w^\top X^\top y+w^\top(X^\top X)\,w .
$$
This has the shape "constant $-$ (linear in $w$) $+$ (quadratic in $w$)" — a bowl — so it has a lowest point.
:::

:::derive from the gradient to the normal equations
Differentiate the three terms of $\mathcal L(w)=\tfrac12\big(y^\top y-2w^\top X^\top y+w^\top X^\top Xw\big)$:

- $y^\top y$ contains no $w$, so its gradient is $\mathbf 0$ (like the derivative of a constant).
- $w^\top(X^\top y)$ is "vector $\cdot$ $w$", like $a\,w$; its gradient is the vector $X^\top y$ (like $\frac{d}{dw}aw=a$).
- $w^\top(X^\top X)w$ is the matrix version of $a\,w^2$; its gradient is $2X^\top Xw$ (like $\frac{d}{dw}aw^2=2aw$). See the box on the gradient of a quadratic.

So
$$
\nabla\mathcal L=\tfrac12\big(\mathbf 0-2X^\top y+2X^\top Xw\big)=X^\top Xw-X^\top y .
$$
Setting it to zero gives $X^\top Xw=X^\top y$. If $X^\top X$ has an inverse, multiply both sides on the left by it:
$$
w=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y .
$$
**A one-variable sanity check.** With a single feature and no intercept, $X^\top X=\sum x_i^2$ and $X^\top y=\sum x_iy_i$ are numbers, and the formula says $w=\dfrac{\sum x_iy_i}{\sum x_i^2}$ — the slope of the best line through the origin.
:::

:::derive the projection argument, in pictures
Every possible prediction $Xw$ lies in the **column space** of $X$ (L03) — think of it as a flat sheet. The target $y$ is usually **off** the sheet.

The closest point of the sheet to $y$ is found the way you find the closest point of a floor to a lamp: **drop a perpendicular**. The error arrow $e=y-Xw$ must then be perpendicular to the sheet, which means perpendicular to every column of $X$:
$$
(\text{column }j)^\top e=0\ \text{for every }j\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad X^\top e=\mathbf 0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad X^\top(y-Xw)=\mathbf 0 .
$$
Multiplying out the last form gives the normal equations again, $X^\top Xw=X^\top y$ — no calculus needed.
:::

:::remember
Least squares solves $X^\top Xw=X^\top y$, giving $w=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ — derived either by setting $\nabla\mathcal L=\mathbf 0$ or by requiring the residual to be orthogonal to $\mathcal C(X)$ — and it requires independent columns.
:::

## 6. Polynomial regression

:::figs
l05-p30|Is it linear?
l05-p32|Polynomial regression as linear-in-parameters
:::

:::background polynomials and 'linear in the parameters'
**A polynomial** of degree $M$ is a sum of powers: $\theta_0+\theta_1x+\theta_2x^2+\cdots+\theta_Mx^M$. Degree $1$ is a line, degree $2$ a parabola, and so on.

**The key idea.** Treat each power of $x$ as if it were a separate feature. For the data point $x=3$, the "features" are
$$
(1,\ x,\ x^2,\ x^3)=(1,\ 3,\ 9,\ 27).
$$
The prediction $\theta_0\cdot1+\theta_1\cdot3+\theta_2\cdot9+\theta_3\cdot27$ is a plain weighted sum of these numbers — exactly the linear model of §2. The curve is bent in $x$, but the **parameters** $\theta$ still appear only multiplied by known numbers and added up. That is all least squares needs, so the same formula $\theta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$ applies, with one column of $X$ per power.
:::




**Overview.** When the relationship between the inputs and the output is **not linear**, we can model it with a polynomial; we formulate the single-feature case. This is often called **non-linear regression**, but it is more accurately described as **linear-in-parameters regression**. It also brings us back to overfitting.

:::def The polynomial model
With $d=1$ and a scalar input $x$,
$$
\hat f(x,\theta)=\theta_0+\theta_1x+\theta_2x^2+\cdots+\theta_Mx^M=\sum_{i=0}^M\theta_ix^i=g(x)^\top\theta,
\qquad g(x)=\big[1,\ x,\ x^2,\ \dots,\ x^M\big]^\top .
$$
$M$ is the degree, and there are $M+1$ coefficients. **$M$ is a hyper-parameter** that controls the complexity of the model; $M=1$ is linear regression and $M=0$ is a constant (the mean).
:::

The model is **linear in the parameters** $\theta$, and that is all the least-squares derivation used. The feature map $g(x)$ may contain **any** fixed functions of $x$, for instance $g(x)=\big[1/x,\ \sin(2\pi x),\ x^2,\ e^x,\ \dots\big]^\top$. With $n$ data points,
$$
\begin{bmatrix}e_1\\ \vdots\\ e_n\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}y_1\\ \vdots\\ y_n\end{bmatrix}-
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1&x_1&x_1^2&\cdots&x_1^M\\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\1&x_n&x_n^2&\cdots&x_n^M\end{bmatrix}}_{X\ (\text{a Vandermonde matrix})}
\begin{bmatrix}\theta_0\\ \vdots\\ \theta_M\end{bmatrix},
\qquad e=y-X\theta,\qquad \theta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y .
$$
As the slide puts it, we have seen this before and are capable of solving it.

:::warn "Linear" means linear in the parameters
$y=w_0+w_1\sin x+w_2e^x$ **is** linear regression. $y=w_0+e^{w_1x}$ is **not**, because $w_1$ sits inside the exponential. A curve in $x$ becomes a straight line in the space of the new features: plotting $y$ against $z=x^2$, for example.
:::

:::example The age data with $M=0,1,2,3$

| $M$ | Parameters | Fitted polynomial | Training SSE |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $1$ | $3.5$ | $5.0$ |
| $1$ | $2$ | $7.186-0.1017x$ | $1.19$ |
| $2$ | $3$ | $1.819+0.2043x-0.004070x^2$ | $0.91$ |
| $3$ | $4$ | $-59+5.403x-0.1470x^2+0.001267x^3$ | $0$ |

The cubic has four parameters for four points, and its square Vandermonde matrix is invertible, so it interpolates exactly. It also predicts $9.6$ hours of exercise a week at age $60$. Zero training error is not a success.
:::

:::remember
Polynomial regression is linear regression on the features $1,x,\dots,x^M$: the normal equations are unchanged, only $X$ is wider, and $M$ is a hyper-parameter.
:::

## 7. Underfitting, overfitting — the Bishop sine example

:::figs
l05-p33|Bishop's sine example
l05-p34|M = 0, 1, 3, 9
l05-p35|Train vs validation error
l05-p36|Coefficients explode with M
l05-p37|More data tames M = 9
:::

:::background why more parameters can be worse
**Counting.** A line has 2 parameters, a cubic 4, a degree-9 polynomial 10. With 10 data points, a degree-9 polynomial has as many knobs as there are points, so it can pass **exactly** through every point — including through the random noise.

**Why that is bad.** The noise is different every time you collect data. A curve that has bent itself to fit this particular noise will be wrong on the next data set. A simpler curve cannot chase the noise, so it captures only the real trend.

**Bias and variance in plain words.**
- **Bias:** being wrong *on average* because the model is too simple (a straight line through a curved trend).
- **Variance:** being *unstable* — the fitted curve changes a lot if the data change a little — because the model is too flexible.
:::




**The set-up** (Bishop, *Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning*, §1.1). The process is $f(x)=\sin(2\pi x)$, the observations are $y=f(x)+n$ at $n=10$ points, and the model is $\hat f(x,\theta)=\theta_0+\theta_1x+\cdots+\theta_Mx^M$. If $M$ is not known, **how do we choose it?**

- With $M=0$ or $M=1$ the fit is a flat or tilted line: **underfitting**, because the model is too simple.
- With $M=3$ the fit follows the sine curve well.
- With $M=9$ the residual error is zero — $y=\hat f(x,\theta)$ at every data point — but the curve oscillates wildly and is nothing like the true function. This is **overfitting**: the model is too complex and fits the **data** rather than the **true function**.

**The coefficients explode as $M$ grows** (slide table):

| | $M=0$ | $M=1$ | $M=3$ | $M=9$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\theta_0$ | $0.19$ | $0.82$ | $0.31$ | $0.35$ |
| $\theta_1$ | | $-1.27$ | $7.99$ | $232.37$ |
| $\theta_2$ | | | $-25.43$ | $-5321.83$ |
| $\theta_3$ | | | $17.37$ | $48\,568.31$ |
| $\theta_4,\dots,\theta_9$ | | | | $-231\,639.30$, $640\,042.26$, $-1\,061\,800.52$, $1\,042\,400.18$, $-557\,682.99$, $125\,201.43$ |

**A puzzle.** The cubic is a special case of the degree-9 polynomial (set $\theta_4=\cdots=\theta_9=0$), so why does $M=9$ perform worse? Because ten coefficients are tuned to ten data points, and least squares spends the extra freedom fitting the **noise**, with enormous coefficients that cancel one another between the data points.

**How can we detect overfitting without knowing the true function?** The slides give three remedies.

- **Solution 1 — a train/validation split.** Overfitting shows up as poor generalisation: a large error on validation or test data. Plotting $E_{\mathrm{RMS}}$ against $M$, the training error falls all the way to zero at $M=9$, while the validation error falls until about $M=3$, stays flat up to $M=8$ and then **shoots up** at $M=9$. A good choice is therefore around $M=3$. (Training error can never be used to choose $M$: it always prefers the most complex model.) With very little data, use **leave-one-out** validation; for the four customers the leave-one-out RMSE is $1.49$ for $M=0$, $1.06$ for $M=1$ and $3.57$ for $M=2$, so the line wins.
- **Solution 2 — more data.** The degree-9 fit oscillates with $n=10$ points, settles down with $n=15$, and lies almost on top of $\sin(2\pi x)$ with $n=100$. We overfitted because we did not have enough data for that many parameters. ($M$ plays the role that $k$ plays in kNN: it controls the complexity of the model, and fixing $M=3$ restricts the number of parameters.)
- **Solution 3 — regularization** (§9).

:::def Underfitting and overfitting
**Underfitting:** the model is too simple, and its error is large on both the training data and new data (high bias). **Overfitting:** the model fits the noise, with a tiny training error and a large error on new data (high variance). With $n$ distinct inputs, zero training error is guaranteed as soon as $M\ge n-1$; this proves only that the model can memorise.
:::

:::thm Bias and variance (outline Week 5; PSet 5 P8; PSet 6 P1)
:::origc [PSet 5 · Problem 8 ↗](pdfs/psets/PSet-05.pdf#page=2) · [PSet 6 · Problem 1 ↗](pdfs/psets/PSet-06.pdf#page=1)
**PSet 5, P8.** $n=100$ observations with one input $X$ and output $Y$. Fit a linear model $Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+\varepsilon$ and a cubic $Y=\beta_0+\dots+\beta_3X^3+\varepsilon$ by least squares. (a) If the true relationship is linear, compare their training errors $\|e_{\text{train}}\|^2$: lower, equal, or not enough information? (b) The same for the test error. (c) If the truth is non-linear (by an unknown amount), compare the training errors. (d) The same for the test errors.
**PSet 6, P1.** One feature, no intercept: $\mathcal L(w)=\tfrac12\sum_n(y_n-x_nw)^2+\tfrac\lambda2w^2$. (a) Derive the minimiser $w^\star$ and show it is a minimum. (b) What does increasing $\lambda$ do to the bias and the variance of $w^\star$?

The expected test error decomposes as
$$
\text{Bias}^2+\text{Variance}+\text{Noise}\qquad(\text{L01}).
$$
More flexible models — a higher degree $M$, a smaller penalty $\lambda$, a smaller $k$ — have lower bias and higher variance.

In PSet 5, Problem 8, a linear and a cubic model are fitted to $100$ points. The cubic's **training** error is always at most the linear model's, whatever the truth, because the models are nested. If the truth is **linear**, we expect the linear model to have the lower **test** error, since the extra cubic terms add variance without reducing bias. If the truth is non-linear by an unknown amount, the comparison of test errors **cannot be decided in advance**; it must be settled with validation data.
:::

:::remember
Training error always falls as complexity grows, so choose $M$ (or $\lambda$, or $k$) on validation data; underfitting means too simple, and overfitting means memorised noise with exploding coefficients.
:::

## 8. The closed form in practice

- **Do not compute the inverse.** Forming $X^\top X$ squares the sensitivity of the problem to rounding error. Software solves least squares with stable factorisations instead; `np.linalg.lstsq(X, y)` returns $(7.186,\,-0.1017)$ for the age data.
- **Scale your features.** Raw ages turn the loss surface into a long, thin valley. Centring the ages makes $X^\top X=\operatorname{diag}(4,\,368.75)$, and the **condition number** — which measures how much errors in the input are magnified in the solution — falls from about $21\,000$ to about $92$. For a cubic in raw age the condition number is about $9\times10^{13}$; after standardising, $z=(x-36.25)/9.60$, it is about $80$. Standardising is essential for polynomial features.
- **Cost.** Forming $X^\top X$ takes about $n(d+1)^2$ operations and solving the system about $(d+1)^3$. This is instantaneous for hundreds of features but impractical for millions, where iterative methods such as gradient descent (L09) take over. Linear regression is one of the few models with an analytical solution.
- **The battery problem.** Features such as cycle count, temperature and charge rate feed a linear model; polynomial features capture non-linear degradation; **whole batteries** are held out for validation; and one standardises, uses `lstsq`, and reports the RMSE in cycles.

**"So far and moving forward" (slide p28).** We assumed a linear structure; the number of parameters is the dimension of the feature space plus one; the loss was built from the residual error; and the optimisation problem has an analytical solution. A numerical algorithm comes next.

## 9. Regularization

:::background what a penalty does
Think of the loss as a **price** to be minimised. Regularisation adds a second price: a charge for having large weights.
$$
\text{total price}=\underbrace{\text{how badly we fit the data}}_{\text{squared error}}+\lambda\times\underbrace{\text{how large the weights are}}_{\text{penalty}} .
$$
The number $\lambda\ge0$ is the **exchange rate** between the two:

- $\lambda=0$: weights are free, and we get ordinary least squares.
- $\lambda$ huge: weights are very expensive, so the best choice is weights near zero (a flat line).
- In between: the model fits the data, but only as tightly as it can afford.

Since overfitted models have enormous weights (look at the $M=9$ table), charging for large weights stops them from overfitting.
:::


**Overview.** Regularization encourages the coefficients to be **small** by adding a **penalty term** (a **regulariser**) to the error:
$$
\underset{\theta}{\text{minimize}}\quad \mathcal L_{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)=\mathcal L(\theta)+\lambda\,\mathcal R(\theta),\qquad\text{where}\quad \mathcal L(\theta)=\tfrac12\|y-X\theta\|_2^2 .
$$
The parameter $\lambda\ge0$ sets the **trade-off** between the regulariser and the original loss. With $\lambda=0$ we recover ordinary least squares (OLS); as $\lambda\to\infty$ every slope is driven to zero and the model becomes the constant $\bar y$. Regularization treats three symptoms of a single disease — too much freedom for the amount of data: memorised noise, exploding coefficients, and a singular $X^\top X$.

### 9a. Ridge regression ($L^2$ / $\ell^2$ penalty)

:::figs
l05-p40|Ridge regulariser

l05-p43|Coefficients and validation error vs ln λ
l05-p44|Ridge geometry

:::def Ridge regression
With $\mathcal R(\theta)=\tfrac12\|\theta\|_2^2$,
$$
\underset{\theta}{\text{minimize}}\quad \mathcal L_{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)=\tfrac12\|y-X\theta\|_2^2+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|_2^2 .
$$
The penalty trades off the **fit of the model to the data** (the first term is large if the model fits poorly) against the **squared norm of the coefficients** (the second term is large if the coefficients are large); a large $\lambda$ penalises the coefficients more. **Intuitively, we minimise the error while keeping the norm of the coefficients bounded.** In deep learning the same penalty is called weight decay.
:::

:::proof The closed form (slide p41; 2025 Mid P7a)
:::origc [2025 Mid Part II · Problem 7(a) ↗](pdfs/past/Mid-2025-Part2.pdf#page=5)
Write down the loss function for ridge regression, and derive the closed-form solution for the ridge estimator.

The regularised loss is still quadratic, so it has a closed-form minimiser. Its gradient is
$$
\nabla\mathcal L_{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)=\tfrac12\big(-2X^\top y+2X^\top X\theta+2\lambda\theta\big)=X^\top X\theta+\lambda\theta-X^\top y .
$$
Setting it equal to zero,
$$
X^\top X\theta+\lambda\theta=X^\top y\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad (X^\top X+\lambda I)\,\theta=X^\top y\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;\theta(\lambda)=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y\;}
$$
At $\lambda=0$ this is the unregularised solution; as $\lambda\to\infty$ it tends to the zero vector. The only change from OLS is the addition of $\lambda$ to the diagonal of $X^\top X$ — the "ridge" that gives the method its name.
:::

:::example 2025 Mid, Problem 7(b)–(c)
:::orig [2025 Mid Part II · Problem 7 (5 pts) ↗](pdfs/past/Mid-2025-Part2.pdf#page=5)

Compute the ridge regression estimate. (c) How does $\lambda$ influence the magnitude of $w_{\text{ridge}}$? What happens as $\lambda\to\infty$?
:::
Take $X=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$, $Y=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}$ and $\lambda=1$.

**Step 1.**
$$
X^\top X=\begin{bmatrix}2&3\\3&5\end{bmatrix},\qquad X^\top X+I=\begin{bmatrix}3&3\\3&6\end{bmatrix},\qquad X^\top Y=\begin{bmatrix}3\\5\end{bmatrix}.
$$
**Step 2.** The determinant is $18-9=9$, so
$$
(X^\top X+I)^{-1}=\frac19\begin{bmatrix}6&-3\\-3&3\end{bmatrix}.
$$
**Step 3.**
$$
w_{\mathrm{ridge}}=\frac19\begin{bmatrix}6&-3\\-3&3\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3\\5\end{bmatrix}=\frac19\begin{bmatrix}18-15\\-9+15\end{bmatrix}=\boxed{\begin{bmatrix}1/3\\2/3\end{bmatrix}}.

**(c)** As $\lambda$ increases, the penalty pushes the coefficients towards zero, increasing the bias and reducing the variance; in the limit $\lambda\to\infty$, $w_{\mathrm{ridge}}\to\mathbf 0$. (For comparison, OLS fits these two points exactly with $w=(0,1)$.)
:::

:::warn Leave the intercept alone
:::origc [2025 Mid Part II · Problem 7(b) ↗](pdfs/past/Mid-2025-Part2.pdf#page=5)
Let $X=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ (first column = intercept), $Y=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}$ and $\lambda=1$. Compute the ridge estimate. *(The official solution uses $(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top Y$, which also penalises the intercept.)*

Penalising the intercept $w_0$ would pull every prediction towards zero — a point fixed by the units of measurement rather than by the data. Either use $\lambda D$ with $D=\operatorname{diag}(0,1,\dots,1)$, or centre the features and $y$ and apply $\lambda I$ to the slopes only. Note that the textbook formula $(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$, applied to an $X$ that contains the column of ones, **does** penalise the intercept; the 2025 Mid used it that way, so follow the loss as the question states it.
:::

:::example Ridge on the age data, with the intercept unpenalised and $\lambda=100$
:::orig [PSet 6 · Problem 1 ↗](pdfs/psets/PSet-06.pdf#page=1) · [solution](pdfs/psets/PSet-06-Solution.pdf)
*(The age-data calculation is the lecture's running example; the PSet question answered at the end is below.)*
Consider a one-dimensional data set $(x_n,y_n)$, $n=1,\dots,N$, and ridge regression with the intercept fixed at zero:
$$\mathcal L(w)=\frac12\sum_{n=1}^N(y_n-x_nw)^2+\frac\lambda2w^2,\qquad\lambda\ge0.$$
(a) Derive, step by step, a closed-form expression for the minimiser $w^\star$, and show it is a minimiser, not just a stationary point. (b) Intuitively, what effect does increasing $\lambda$ have on the bias of $w^\star$? On its variance?
:::
Only the bottom-right entry of $X^\top X$ changes:
$$
\begin{bmatrix}4&145\\145&5625+100\end{bmatrix}w=\begin{bmatrix}14\\470\end{bmatrix},\qquad \det=4\cdot5725-145^2=1875,
$$
$$
w=\frac{1}{1875}\begin{bmatrix}5725&-145\\-145&4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}14\\470\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{1875}\begin{bmatrix}12\,000\\-150\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}6.4\\-0.08\end{bmatrix}.
$$
The slope shrinks from $-0.1017$ to $-0.08$. The training SSE rises from $1.186$ to $1.36$ — it must, since OLS minimises the SSE — but the ridge objective falls from $1.110$ to $1.00$. The residuals still sum to zero and the line still passes through $(\bar x,\bar y)$, because the intercept is not penalised.

**The centred shortcut.** For one feature,
$$
w_1(\lambda)=\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}+\lambda}=\underbrace{\frac{S_{xx}}{S_{xx}+\lambda}}_{\text{shrink factor}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}}_{\text{OLS slope}} .
$$
Ridge multiplies the OLS slope by a factor between $0$ and $1$. With $\lambda=S_{xx}=368.75$ the slope is exactly halved, and as $\lambda\to\infty$ the line flattens into the constant $3.5$. Features that hardly vary (small $S_{xx}$) are shrunk the most — exactly where the data carry the least information.

**PSet 6, Problem 1.** With one feature and no intercept,
$$
\frac{d\mathcal L}{dw}=\sum_n\big(x_n^2w-x_ny_n\big)+\lambda w=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad w^\star=\frac{\sum_n x_ny_n}{\sum_n x_n^2+\lambda},
$$
and the second derivative $\sum_n x_n^2+\lambda>0$ confirms a minimum. Increasing $\lambda$ increases the bias and decreases the variance.
:::

:::thm Ridge always has a unique solution
:::origc [PSet 6 · Problem 2 ↗](pdfs/psets/PSet-06.pdf#page=1)
Let $n=d=2$ with $x_{11}=x_{12}=a$, $x_{21}=x_{22}=-a$ ($a\ne0$) and $y_1+y_2=0$, so the intercept is zero and can be dropped. The ridge loss is $\mathcal L(w)=\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^2(y_i-x_{i1}w_1-x_{i2}w_2)^2+\tfrac\lambda2(w_1^2+w_2^2)$, $\lambda>0$. (a) Show that $\mathcal L(w)=\big(y_1-a(w_1+w_2)\big)^2+\tfrac\lambda2(w_1^2+w_2^2)$. (b) Show that $w_1^\star=w_2^\star$ and find their common value. (c) Compute $w^\star$ for $a=1$, $y_1=2$, $\lambda=1$.
:::
For $\lambda>0$ and every $v\neq\mathbf 0$,
$$
v^\top\big(X^\top X+\lambda I\big)v=\|Xv\|_2^2+\lambda\|v\|_2^2\ \ge\ \lambda\|v\|_2^2\ >\ 0 ,
$$
so $(X^\top X+\lambda I)v$ can never be the zero vector. The matrix is therefore invertible and the minimiser is **unique**, even when $d>n$ or when columns are exact duplicates.

With an exact duplicate feature, ridge **splits the weight equally**: swapping the two weights leaves both the fit and the penalty unchanged, and the minimiser is unique, so the two weights must be equal. In PSet 6, Problem 2, with identical features $x_{i1}=x_{i2}$,
$$
w_1^\star=w_2^\star=\frac{2ay_1}{4a^2+\lambda},\qquad\text{which equals }0.8\text{ for }a=1,\ y_1=2,\ \lambda=1 .
$$
:::

**The slide's example ($M=9$ on the sine data).** Too small a $\lambda$ means no regularisation; too large a $\lambda$ gives no weight to the data. Useful values of $\lambda$ are very small, so it is convenient to work with $\ln\lambda$ and write $\lambda=e^{\ln\lambda}$.

| | $\ln\lambda=-\infty$ | $\ln\lambda=-18$ | $\ln\lambda=0$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\theta_0$ | $0.35$ | $0.35$ | $0.13$ |
| $\theta_1$ | $232.37$ | $4.74$ | $-0.05$ |
| $\theta_2$ | $-5321.83$ | $-0.77$ | $-0.06$ |
| $\theta_3$ | $48\,568.31$ | $-31.97$ | $-0.05$ |
| $\theta_4$ | $-231\,639.30$ | $-3.89$ | $-0.03$ |
| $\theta_5$ | $640\,042.26$ | $55.28$ | $-0.02$ |
| $\theta_6$ | $-1\,061\,800.52$ | $41.32$ | $-0.01$ |
| $\theta_7$ | $1\,042\,400.18$ | $-45.95$ | $-0.00$ |

| $\theta_9$ | $125\,201.43$ | $72.68$ | $0.01$ |

Including the squared norm of the coefficients in the loss stops them from exploding. **$\lambda$ is a hyper-parameter, learned in practice from validation data:** the validation $E_{\mathrm{RMS}}$ plotted against $\ln\lambda$ is U-shaped, overfitting on the left and underfitting on the right.

**The graphical view (two coefficients $\theta_1,\theta_2$).** The contours of $\tfrac12\|y-X\theta\|^2$ are ellipses around the OLS solution, and the contours of $\tfrac\lambda2\|\theta\|^2$ are circles around the origin; the solution $\theta^\ast$ lies where the two families touch. A good $\lambda$ avoids overfitting. **Irrelevant features receive small but non-zero weights** in the ridge solution, whereas ideally we would like to give them **zero** weight — which is what the lasso achieves.

:::warn Standardise before you penalise
Penalties are **not** unit-free. If age is recorded in months rather than years, the same $\lambda=100$ hardly shrinks the slope at all ($-0.1015$ per year instead of $-0.08$), because the slope's numerical value becomes twelve times smaller and its penalty $144$ times cheaper. Income in rupees (values near $10^5$) receives a tiny weight whose penalty is negligible, while "number of children" pays in full. Standardise the features, using training-set statistics, and leave the intercept unpenalised.
:::

### 9b. Lasso regression ($L^1$ / $\ell^1$ penalty)

:::figs
l05-p45|Lasso geometry: the diamond
:::



:::def The lasso
With $\mathcal R(\theta)=\|\theta\|_1=\sum_i|\theta_i|$,
$$
\underset{\theta}{\text{minimize}}\quad \mathcal L_{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)=\tfrac12\|y-X\theta\|_2^2+\lambda\|\theta\|_1 .
$$
The name stands for **l**east **a**bsolute **s**hrinkage and **s**election **o**perator. Graphically, the constraint region is a **diamond**, and the error contours typically meet it at a **corner**. Every corner lies on an axis, where some coefficients are exactly zero, so the lasso gives a **sparse solution**. In short: ridge shrinks, the lasso selects.
:::

**Why absolute values produce exact zeros: soft thresholding.** For a single centred feature, write $a=\sum_ix_i^2$ and $b=\sum_ix_iy_i$. Up to a constant, the lasso loss is
$$
f(w)=\frac a2w^2-bw+\lambda|w| .
$$
Consider the three possible locations of the minimum.

- If it lies at some $w>0$, then $f'(w)=aw-b+\lambda=0$, so $w=(b-\lambda)/a$, which is positive only when $b>\lambda$.
- If it lies at some $w<0$, then $f'(w)=aw-b-\lambda=0$, so $w=(b+\lambda)/a$, which is negative only when $b<-\lambda$.
- Otherwise, when $|b|\le\lambda$, the minimum is at the kink $w=0$: just to the right of $0$ the slope $-b+\lambda$ is non-negative, and just to the left the slope $-b-\lambda$ is non-positive, so both directions lead uphill.

The three cases combine into one formula, which we set beside its ridge and OLS counterparts:
$$
w^\star_{\mathrm{lasso}}=\frac{S(b,\lambda)}{a},\quad S(b,\lambda)=\operatorname{sign}(b)\max\big(|b|-\lambda,\,0\big);
\qquad
w^\star_{\mathrm{ridge}}=\frac{b}{a+\lambda};
\qquad
w^\star_{\mathrm{OLS}}=\frac{b}{a}.
$$
OLS divides the evidence $b$ by $a$. Ridge enlarges the denominator, shrinking every weight by the same **factor**. The lasso subtracts $\lambda$ from the evidence, shrinking every weight by the same **amount**, and sets to **exactly zero** any weight whose evidence satisfies $|b|\le\lambda$.

:::example One weight by hand
Take $a=2$, $b=5$ and $\lambda=6$. Then
$$
w_{\mathrm{OLS}}=\frac52=2.5,\qquad w_{\mathrm{ridge}}=\frac{5}{2+6}=0.625,\qquad w_{\mathrm{lasso}}=\frac{S(5,6)}{2}=0,
$$
since $|5|\le6$; the lasso weight is zero for every $\lambda\ge5$. For the age data, $w_1(\lambda)=-\max(37.5-\lambda,0)/368.75$, which reaches zero at $\lambda=37.5$.
:::

**Many weights.** There is no closed form. **Coordinate descent** cycles through the features, updating each weight by soft thresholding with the others held fixed,
$$
w_j\ \leftarrow\ \frac{S\big(x_j^\top r^{(j)},\,\lambda\big)}{x_j^\top x_j},\qquad r^{(j)}=y-\sum_{k\neq j}x_kw_k ,
$$
and, because the lasso loss is convex, it converges to the global minimum. Lasso optimisation is **computationally more expensive** than ridge. On the standardised age and sleep features, the sleep weight drops out exactly at $\lambda\approx0.40$ and the age weight at $\lambda=3.906$, whereas ridge keeps both. In a simulated example with twelve features of which three matter, the lasso set five of the nine useless weights exactly to zero and predicted best.

:::example Lasso versus ridge (2024 Mid, Problem 5)
:::orig [2024 Mid Part II · Problem 5 (6 pts) ↗](pdfs/past/Mid-2024-Part2.pdf#page=6)
Consider a dataset with many features, some irrelevant or redundant. (a) Describe the difference between lasso and ridge regression in their effect on the model's coefficients. (b) Write the loss function for lasso regression, and explain why it leads to sparse solutions. (c) Discuss how the choice of $\lambda$ affects the sparsity of the solution.
:::
**(a)** The **lasso** penalises $\|\theta\|_1$ and can shrink some coefficients **exactly to zero**, producing a sparse model that selects features. **Ridge** penalises $\|\theta\|_2^2$ and shrinks coefficients towards zero, but **not exactly** to zero, so it keeps every feature with reduced influence.

**(b)** The lasso loss is $\mathcal L(\theta)=\tfrac12\|A\theta-y\|^2+\lambda\|\theta\|_1$. The $L^1$ penalty is not differentiable at zero; it has a sharp corner there, which makes it likely that the optimum sets coefficients exactly to zero.

**(c)** Increasing $\lambda$ strengthens the penalty and drives more coefficients to zero, increasing sparsity; decreasing it keeps more features. The value of $\lambda$ balances complexity against sparsity.
:::

### 9c. Elastic net and comparison

:::figs
l05-p46|Ridge vs lasso, elastic net
:::



:::def The elastic net: a hybrid of $L^1$ and $L^2$
$$
\underset{\theta}{\text{minimize}}\quad \mathcal L_{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta)=\tfrac12\|y-X\theta\|_2^2+\lambda_1\|\theta\|_1+\tfrac{\lambda_2}{2}\|\theta\|_2^2 .
$$
Ridge and the lasso are special cases. The elastic net combines their strengths — it selects features like the lasso while keeping groups of correlated features together like ridge — at the cost of **two** hyper-parameters, $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$, both tuned on validation data.
:::

| | Ridge, $\tfrac\lambda2\|w\|_2^2$ | Lasso, $\lambda\|w\|_1$ |
|---|---|---|
| penalty | error $+\ \lambda\times$ sum of **squares** of the coefficients | error $+\ \lambda\times$ sum of **absolute values** |
| solution | closed form $(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$ | iterative (coordinate descent), more expensive |
| effect on weights | shrinks all by a factor; none exactly zero | shrinks by an amount; many exactly zero (**sparse**, and therefore **biased**) |
| geometry | a disc (round) | a diamond (corners on the axes) |
| uniqueness | always unique for $\lambda>0$ | can fail with perfectly correlated features |
| correlated features | spreads the weight across them | tends to pick one |
| best when | many features each contribute a little | a few features matter |

Mid MCQs on this material: the regulariser that promotes a **sparse** weight vector is the **$L^1$** penalty (2024, MCQ 9); the diamond figure shows a sparse, $L^1$-penalised solution, not ridge (MCQ 14). For ridge with $\lambda>0$ (2025, MCQ 21): the closed form is correct, and coefficients shrink as $\lambda$ grows; but ridge does not set coefficients exactly to zero, and it **decreases** variance at the cost of bias, rather than increasing it. The bias–variance trade-off is controlled by $\lambda$ and by the polynomial degree (2024, MCQ 6). Variance is reduced by adding more data or by lowering model complexity (2024, MCQ 5).



:::derive why the gradient of $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|^2$ is $\lambda\theta$
Write the penalty out: $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\|\theta\|^2=\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\big(\theta_1^2+\theta_2^2+\cdots+\theta_d^2\big)$.

Its partial derivative with respect to $\theta_k$ uses only the $\theta_k^2$ term:
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_k}\,\frac{\lambda}{2}\theta_k^2=\frac{\lambda}{2}\cdot2\theta_k=\lambda\theta_k .
$$
Stacking over $k$ gives the vector $\lambda\theta$. Adding this to the least-squares gradient,
$$
X^\top X\theta-X^\top y+\lambda\theta=\mathbf 0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad (X^\top X+\lambda I)\theta=X^\top y .
$$
The step $X^\top X\theta+\lambda\theta=(X^\top X+\lambda I)\theta$ uses $\lambda\theta=\lambda I\theta$: multiplying by the identity changes nothing, and writing it this way lets us factor out $\theta$.
:::

:::derive why the lasso sets weights exactly to zero (the slope argument)
For one weight the lasso minimises $f(w)=\tfrac a2w^2-bw+\lambda|w|$. The term $|w|$ has a **corner** at $w=0$: its slope is $-1$ just to the left of $0$ and $+1$ just to the right.

- Just to the **right** of $0$, the slope of $f$ is $-b+\lambda$.
- Just to the **left** of $0$, it is $-b-\lambda$.

If $|b|\le\lambda$, the right-hand slope is $\ge0$ (uphill to the right) and the left-hand slope is $\le0$ (uphill to the left). So $w=0$ is the bottom of the valley — **exactly** zero. A smooth penalty like $w^2$ has slope $0$ at $w=0$ and no corner, so it can never pin the weight there; it only shrinks it.

**Example.** $a=2$, $b=5$: with $\lambda=6$ we have $|b|=5\le6$, so the lasso weight is $0$; with $\lambda=1$ it is $(5-1)/2=2$.
:::

## 10. Choosing $\lambda$

:::background validation and cross-validation, recapped
**Why not choose $\lambda$ by training error?** Training error is smallest when there is no penalty at all, so it would always pick $\lambda=0$ — the overfitting choice.

**Validation.** Hold some data back. Fit the model with each candidate $\lambda$ on the rest, and see which $\lambda$ predicts the held-back data best.

**$k$-fold cross-validation** does this $k$ times, each time holding back a different slice, and averages the results — so every data point gets a turn at being the "unseen" data. **Leave-one-out** is the extreme case where each slice is a single point.
:::


- **Never on training error**, which always selects $\lambda=0$.
- **Leave-one-out validation** on very small data sets. For the four customers (standardised age, unpenalised intercept), the leave-one-out RMSE is:

| $\lambda$ | $0$ | $0.1$ | $0.3$ | $1$ | $3$ | $10$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| line ($M=1$) | $1.06$ | $1.05$ | **$1.03$** | $1.04$ | $1.15$ | $1.33$ |
| parabola ($M=2$) | $3.57$ | $1.98$ | $1.25$ | **$1.06$** | $1.20$ | $1.35$ |
| cubic ($M=3$) | — | $1.77$ | $1.60$ | $1.38$ | **$1.29$** | $1.32$ |

Regularisation rescues the parabola dramatically and makes the cubic usable, but the best model is still a lightly penalised line: four customers cannot support a curve. Degree and penalty are chosen together.
- **$k$-fold cross-validation** in practice: choose a grid of $\lambda$ values evenly spaced in $\ln\lambda$; for each, fit on $k-1$ folds and validate on the remaining one, then average; select the best $\lambda$, refit on all the training data, and report performance on the untouched test set. Compute the standardisation inside each fold, and split grouped data by group.
- **Implementation.** Ridge is OLS on augmented data,
$$
\tilde X=\begin{bmatrix}X\\ 0\ \ \ \sqrt\lambda\,I_d\end{bmatrix},\qquad \tilde y=\begin{bmatrix}y\\ \mathbf 0\end{bmatrix},
$$
that is, $d$ imaginary customers who each pull one slope towards zero, solved with `lstsq`. A gradient step for ridge multiplies the weights by $(1-\eta\lambda)$, which is why it is called weight decay. In probabilistic terms, ridge corresponds to a Gaussian prior on the weights and the lasso to a Laplace prior (maximum a posteriori estimation, L13).
