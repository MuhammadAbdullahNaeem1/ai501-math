Real data often has many features, and high-dimensional spaces behave strangely: they are mostly empty, and almost all their volume sits near the surface. The saving grace is that real data occupies only a thin, low-dimensional part of the space, and **principal component analysis (PCA)** finds that part. In order, this lecture covers: the curse of dimensionality (exponentially many cells; volume concentrated in a thin shell); why real data still lives in a low-dimensional region; dimensionality reduction by feature extraction, $z=W^\top x$; the two objectives of PCA; the five-step algorithm (mean, centre, covariance, eigen-decomposition, reduce); choosing $k$ from the variance kept; the slides' worked example; and the practical caveats (normalise first; PCA ignores labels). From the revision notes we add *why* the eigenvectors of the covariance are optimal, the reconstruction error, and the link to the SVD of L07.

:::intuition Two sensors that say almost the same thing (revision notes)
Two sensors measure the same quantity; each reading is a pair $x=(x_1,x_2)$. Ten readings — the slides' example data —
$$
(2.5,2.4),\ (0.5,0.7),\ (2.2,2.9),\ (1.9,2.2),\ (3.1,3.0),\ (2.3,2.7),\ (2.0,1.6),\ (1.0,1.1),\ (1.5,1.6),\ (1.1,0.9)
$$
hug a line: when one sensor reads high, so does the other. Two numbers per reading are more than we need. The natural single number is the **position along that line**; the distance from the line is mostly noise. PCA finds that line systematically (and, in higher dimensions, the best plane or subspace). We carry this example through the whole page.
:::

## 1. The curse of dimensionality

:::figs
l08-p04|Illustration 1: exponentially many cells
:::

The **curse of dimensionality** is the set of problems that appear when analysing, classifying or organising data in high-dimensional spaces and that do not arise in low dimensions. The core fact: the size of the feature space grows **exponentially** with the number of dimensions $d$, so to keep the points close together the number of data points $n$ must grow exponentially too.

**Illustration 1 — counting cells.** Cut each feature axis into $m$ bins. In $d$ dimensions this gives $m^d$ cells, and to have a training point in every cell we need $n\ge m^d$. With $m=10$:
$$
d=2:\ 10^2=100,\qquad d=3:\ 10^3=1000,\qquad d=10:\ 10^{10},\qquad d=784:\ 10^{784}.
$$
The last is a tiny $28\times28$ image. No dataset fills that many cells, so a new point almost never has a training point nearby — bad news for kNN (L04).

:::figs
l08-p06|Illustration 2: the volume in a thin shell
l08-p07|Probabilities for ε = 0.1
:::

**Illustration 2 — volume near the surface.** The ball of radius $r$ in $\mathbb R^d$, $B(r)=\{x\in\mathbb R^d:\|x\|_2\le r\}$, has volume
$$
V(r)=K_d\,r^d,\qquad K_d=\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(\tfrac d2+1)}
$$
(only the $r^d$ matters here). The fraction of the unit ball's volume in the thin shell between radii $1-\epsilon$ and $1$ is
$$
\frac{V(1)-V(1-\epsilon)}{V(1)}=\frac{K_d-K_d(1-\epsilon)^d}{K_d}=1-(1-\epsilon)^d .
$$
Since $1-\epsilon<1$, raising it to a large power $d$ makes it tiny: almost nothing is left in the inner ball. For a uniformly distributed point, the probabilities of being in the shell and in the inner ball are:

| $d$ | 1 | 2 | 10 | 50 | 400 | 784 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| shell, $\epsilon=0.1$: $1-0.9^d$ | 0.1 | 0.19 | 0.65 | 0.995 | 1.000 | 1.000 |
| inner ball, $0.9^d$ | 0.9 | 0.81 | 0.35 | 0.005 | 0.000 | 0.000 |
| shell, $\epsilon=0.01$: $1-0.99^d$ | 0.01 | 0.02 | 0.096 | 0.395 | 0.982 | 0.999 |
| inner ball, $0.99^d$ | 0.99 | 0.98 | 0.904 | 0.605 | 0.018 | 0.0004 |

For $d=50$, only 5 of 1000 points fall in the inner ball. For $d=400$, $(0.9)^{400}\approx4.98\times10^{-19}$: almost every point lies on the surface, so seen from a test point at the origin **almost every point is at the same Euclidean distance**. When all points look equally far away, "nearest neighbour" loses its meaning.

:::background computing $0.9^{d}$ without a calculator
Use $\ln0.9\approx-0.105$: $0.9^{d}=e^{-0.105d}$. For $d=10$, $e^{-1.05}\approx0.35$; for $d=50$, $e^{-5.27}\approx0.005$. Similarly $\ln0.99\approx-0.01$, so $0.99^{d}\approx e^{-0.01d}$: $d=50$ gives $e^{-0.5}\approx0.61$ and $d=400$ gives $e^{-4}\approx0.018$. A rule of thumb: $(1-\epsilon)^d\approx e^{-\epsilon d}$, which is small once $\epsilon d$ is more than a few.
:::

:::example Check yourself
What fraction of a 100-dimensional ball's volume lies within $\epsilon=0.05$ of the surface? $1-0.95^{100}\approx1-e^{-5}\approx1-0.006=0.994$: more than 99%.
:::

**Why it is not hopeless (slide p9).** High-dimensional space is empty, but real data does not fill it. Real-world data in a high-dimensional space is **confined to a region of much lower effective dimension**, and it is smooth enough to interpolate. Examples: points along a line or a plane; the images of a rotating object, which trace a 1-dimensional curve in a million-dimensional pixel space; faces described by 50–71 features. **Dimensionality reduction** finds that region and works there.

:::remember
In $d$ dimensions you need $\sim m^d$ points to fill the space, and a fraction $1-(1-\epsilon)^d\to1$ of the volume is in a thin shell — but real data lives near a low-dimensional region.
:::

## 2. Dimensionality reduction by feature extraction

**Feature extraction** transforms the existing features into a smaller set of new ones with a mapping $z=f(x)$, linear or non-linear — a projection of the data from the high-dimensional space to a lower-dimensional one that preserves the desired information as well as possible. Finding the best mapping means optimising an objective, and different objectives give different methods:

- **minimise information loss** — represent the data as accurately as possible in fewer dimensions: **PCA**;
- **maximise discriminatory information** — separate the classes as well as possible: linear discriminant analysis (**LDA**).

This lecture covers PCA, a **linear** mapping, because it is simple to compute and analytically tractable.

:::figs
l08-p12|z = Wᵀx and the two objectives
:::

:::def The PCA mapping
Given $x\in\mathbb R^d$, project it to $\mathbb R^k$ ($k<d$) by
$$
z=W^\top x,\qquad W\in\mathbb R^{d\times k},\quad W^\top\in\mathbb R^{k\times d},\quad z\in\mathbb R^k .
$$
The columns $v_1,\dots,v_k$ of $W$ are orthonormal directions — the **principal components** — and the new feature $z_j=v_j^\top x$ is the coordinate of $x$ along $v_j$ (where $x$ lands when you drop a perpendicular onto the line through $v_j$). So PCA is a **change of axes, then keep only $k$ of them**.

We want the mapping to preserve as much information as possible, with

- **Objective 1:** the new features are **uncorrelated** (no feature repeats another, so they cannot be reduced further);
- **Objective 2:** the new features have **large variance** (they capture the variation that distinguishes samples).
:::

:::example The slide's question: what is the size of $W$?
"Best planar approximation" means $k=2$.

- 4-D data: $W\in\mathbb R^{4\times2}$, $W^\top\in\mathbb R^{2\times4}$, $z\in\mathbb R^2$.
- 100-D data: $W\in\mathbb R^{100\times2}$.
- 64×64 images with 30 components: $d=4096$, $W\in\mathbb R^{4096\times30}$, and each image becomes 30 numbers.
:::

:::figs
l08-p14|First and second principal components of a 2-D cloud
l08-p15|Change of coordinates, then ignore the second component
:::

**Geometric intuition (slides p14–15).** In the toy 2-D cloud, most of the spread of each class lies along one direction: the **first principal component**. The second principal component is perpendicular to it. Rotating the axes onto these two directions is just a change of coordinates (each new coordinate is a linear combination of the old features). Ignoring the second coordinate then flattens every point onto the first axis — and since the cloud was thin in that direction, little is lost.

:::remember
PCA: $z=W^\top x$ with orthonormal columns of $W$ chosen so the new features are uncorrelated and have the largest possible variance.
:::

## 3. Mean, centring and the covariance matrix

:::figs
l08-p16|Formulation: x̂ = Σ zᵢvᵢ minimising ‖x − x̂‖
l08-p18|Step 3: the covariance matrix
:::

**The formulation (slide p16).** We have $n$ feature vectors $x\in\mathbb R^d$ ($d$ = number of features). We want to represent each $x$ using only $k<d$ orthonormal basis vectors,
$$
\hat x=\sum_{i=1}^kz_iv_i,\qquad z_i=x^\top v_i,\qquad\text{such that}\quad\|x-\hat x\|_2\ \text{is minimised.}
$$
In words: $z=W^\top x$ is a lower-dimensional transformation of $x$, and $\hat x$ rebuilds $x$ from its $k$ coordinates. Geometrically, PCA squashes a scattered cloud onto the best line (or plane) through it.
How do we find the $v_i$? Through the first three steps of the algorithm:

1. **Sample mean** (sum over the $n$ samples): $\displaystyle\bar x=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nx_i\in\mathbb R^d$ — the centre of the cloud.
2. **Subtract the mean:** $s_i=x_i-\bar x$. Variation is measured around the centre. Stack the centred samples as columns, $S=[s_1,\dots,s_n]\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$.
3. **Covariance matrix:**
$$
\Sigma=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^ns_is_i^\top=\frac1n\,SS^\top\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}.
$$

(The revision notes write $C$ for the covariance, to avoid a clash with the $\Sigma$ of the SVD; the slides use $\Sigma$. Below we write $C$.)

:::background covariance of two features, and what the entries mean
For two lists of numbers $f,g\in\mathbb R^n$ (one feature each, over the $n$ samples), the **covariance** is
$$
\sigma_{fg}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big(f_i-\operatorname{avg}(f)\big)\big(g_i-\operatorname{avg}(g)\big).
$$
Entry $(j,k)$ of $C$ is the covariance of feature $j$ with feature $k$:

- if the two deviations tend to have the **same sign** (both above average, or both below), the products are positive and so is the covariance;
- if one tends to be high when the other is low, it is **negative**;
- if they are unrelated, positive and negative products cancel and it is **near zero**.

In one line: $C_{jk}$ **averages the product of the deviations of feature $j$ and feature $k$ from their means** — the deviations because $s_i=x_i-\bar x$, the average because of the $\tfrac1n$. So $C_{12}$ says how features 1 and 2 vary together. The diagonal entry $C_{jj}$ is the covariance of a feature with itself — its **variance**, how much it spreads (equivalently $\tfrac1n$ times the squared norm of that feature's centred column) — and $\operatorname{tr}C=\sum_jC_{jj}$ is the **total variance**. The **correlation** $\rho=C_{jk}/\sqrt{C_{jj}C_{kk}}$ rescales the covariance to $[-1,1]$.
:::

:::warn
The slides divide by $n$; some books divide by $n-1$. That multiplies every eigenvalue by the same factor and **does not change the principal components**. Use whatever the question says (PSet 7 divides by $n$).
:::

:::example Steps 1–3 for the two sensors (slide p23)
**Step 1.** $\bar x=\tfrac1{10}(18.1,\ 19.1)=(1.81,\ 1.91)$.

**Step 2.** Subtracting it, $s_1=(0.69,0.49)$, $s_2=(-1.31,-1.21)$, $s_3=(0.39,0.99)$, $s_4=(0.09,0.29)$, $s_5=(1.29,1.09)$, $s_6=(0.49,0.79)$, $s_7=(0.19,-0.31)$, $s_8=(-0.81,-0.81)$, $s_9=(-0.31,-0.31)$, $s_{10}=(-0.71,-1.01)$. Each column of $S$ sums to zero.

**Step 3.**
$$
C=\frac1{10}SS^\top=\begin{bmatrix}0.5549&0.5539\\0.5539&0.6449\end{bmatrix},\qquad\operatorname{tr}C=1.1998,\qquad\rho=\frac{0.5539}{\sqrt{0.5549\cdot0.6449}}\approx0.926 .
$$
Each sensor spreads by about $0.6$, and the covariance $0.55$ is nearly as large: when one sensor is above its average, so is the other. $\rho\approx0.93$ is the signature of the line.
:::

:::thm What is special about the covariance matrix (slide p19)
$C$ is $d\times d$, **symmetric** ($C^\top=\tfrac1n(SS^\top)^\top=\tfrac1nSS^\top=C$) and **positive semi-definite**:
$$
w^\top Cw=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n w^\top s_is_i^\top w=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(s_i^\top w)^2\ \ge\ 0 .
$$
It is a Gram matrix, exactly as in L07. So it has real eigenvalues $\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_d\ge0$ and an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors.
:::

:::example Check yourself
The second sensor always reads exactly twice the first, and $\operatorname{var}(x_1)=1$. Then $\operatorname{var}(x_2)=4$, $\operatorname{cov}(x_1,x_2)=2$, so $C=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}$ with trace $5$ and determinant $0$: $\lambda=5,0$. All the variation is along $(1,2)/\sqrt5$, and the second feature adds nothing.
:::

:::remember
Centre the data, then $C=\tfrac1nSS^\top$: diagonal = variances, off-diagonal = covariances, $\operatorname{tr}C$ = total variance; $C$ is symmetric PSD.
:::

## 4. Steps 4–5: eigen-decompose and reduce

:::figs
l08-p19|Step 4: Σ = VDVᵀ
l08-p20|Step 5: keep the k largest
:::

**Step 4.** Eigen-decompose the covariance matrix:
$$
C=VDV^\top,\qquad V=[v_1,\dots,v_d],\qquad D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_d).
$$
The $d$ orthonormal eigenvectors $v_i$ are the **principal components**; they form a basis of $\mathbb R^d$.

**Step 5.** We wanted $k$ basis vectors but have $d$. Which $k$? **Simple: the ones with the $k$ largest eigenvalues.** Build the $d\times k$ mapping matrix and reduce:
$$
W=[v_1,\dots,v_k],\qquad z=W^\top x\in\mathbb R^k .
$$

:::def The algorithm in one box
1. **Mean:** $\bar x=\tfrac1n\sum_ix_i$.
2. **Centre:** $s_i=x_i-\bar x$, $S=[s_1,\dots,s_n]$.
3. **Covariance:** $C=\tfrac1nSS^\top$.
4. **EVD:** $C=VDV^\top$, sort $\lambda_1\ge\cdots\ge\lambda_d$.
5. **Reduce:** $W=[v_1,\dots,v_k]$, $z=W^\top(x-\bar x)$. **Reconstruct** if needed: $\hat x=\bar x+Wz$.
:::

:::warn Two details that cost marks
1. The slides write $z=W^\top x$. That equals $W^\top(x-\bar x)$ only for centred data; for raw data the scores are shifted by the constant $W^\top\bar x$. The spread is identical, but to **reconstruct** you must use centred scores and add $\bar x$ back.
2. Software (and the slides' example) often returns eigenvalues in **ascending** order, so the top component may be the **last** column of $V$. Always pick by eigenvalue, not by position.
:::

:::remember
PCA = EVD of the covariance; keep the eigenvectors of the $k$ largest eigenvalues as the columns of $W$.
:::

## 5. Why the top eigenvectors are the right answer

**Spread along a direction.** Take a unit vector $w$ and drop every centred sample onto the line through it: $s_i$ lands at $a_i=w^\top s_i$. The shadows have mean $0$ and variance
$$
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^na_i^2=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nw^\top s_is_i^\top w=w^\top Cw .
$$
So $w^\top Cw$ measures how much the data vary along $w$.

:::thm Key fact 1 — the direction of largest variance
Among all unit vectors $w$, $w^\top Cw$ is largest for $w=v_1$, where it equals $\lambda_1$. The best direction perpendicular to $v_1$ is $v_2$, with variance $\lambda_2$, and so on: **$\lambda_j$ is the variance along $v_j$.**

*Why:* writing $w=\sum_ic_iv_i$ with $\sum c_i^2=1$, $w^\top Cw=\sum_i\lambda_ic_i^2$ — a weighted average of the eigenvalues (as in L07), which cannot exceed $\lambda_1$. Requiring $w\perp v_1$ forces $c_1=0$, leaving $\lambda_2$ as the best, and so on.
:::

:::thm Key fact 2 — the new features are uncorrelated (Objective 1; class participation, 1 Nov)
*Find the covariance of $z=W^\top x$.* The centred scores are $z_i=W^\top s_i$, so
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(z)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nz_iz_i^\top=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^nW^\top s_is_i^\top W=W^\top\Big(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^ns_is_i^\top\Big)W=W^\top CW .
$$
Entry $(i,j)$ is $v_i^\top Cv_j=v_i^\top(\lambda_jv_j)=\lambda_j\,v_i^\top v_j$, which is $\lambda_i$ if $i=j$ and $0$ if $i\neq j$ (orthonormal eigenvectors). Hence
$$
\operatorname{Cov}(z)=W^\top CW=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_k).
$$ The off-diagonal zeros mean the new features are **uncorrelated** ("PCA decorrelates the features"), thanks to the orthogonality of the principal components; the diagonal holds the **largest variances available** (Objective 2).
:::

:::thm Key fact 3 — what is lost
Reconstructing from $k$ components, $\hat x_i=\bar x+Wz_i$, the average squared error is the sum of the **discarded** eigenvalues:
$$
\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\|x_i-\hat x_i\|^2=\sum_{j=k+1}^d\lambda_j=\operatorname{tr}C-\sum_{j=1}^k\lambda_j .
$$
*Why (Pythagoras):* the reconstruction $p_i$ is the shadow of $s_i$ on the kept directions, and the leftover $r_i=s_i-p_i$ is perpendicular to them, so $\|s_i\|^2=\|p_i\|^2+\|r_i\|^2$. Averaging: total variance $=$ variance kept $+$ error. The total, $\operatorname{tr}C=\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_d$, is fixed, so **minimising the error and maximising the variance kept are the same problem** — and the top-$k$ eigenvectors solve both.
:::

:::remember
$\lambda_j$ = variance along $v_j$; the scores are uncorrelated; the reconstruction error is the sum of the eigenvalues you dropped.
:::

## 6. How do we choose $k$?

:::figs
l08-p22|Preserve a fraction T of the variance
:::

It depends on how much information — variance — we want to preserve. Define the fraction kept
$$
T_k=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^k\lambda_i}{\sum_{i=1}^d\lambda_i}
$$
and choose the **smallest $k$ with $T_k>T$** for a threshold $T$. $T=1$ means $k=d$ (no reduction); $T=0.8$ means 80% of the variation in the data is preserved. If the eigenvalues fall off quickly, a small $k$ suffices — the sign that the data live near a low-dimensional region (the same "look for the gap" idea as approximate rank in L07).

:::example PSet 7, Problem 7
:::orig [PSet 7 · Problem 7 ↗](pdfs/psets/PSet-07-with-Solutions.pdf#page=3)
Centred observations $X_c=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\\-2&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$ (rows are objects). (a) Compute $C=\tfrac14X_c^\top X_c$ and its eigenvalues. (b) What fraction of the variance is kept by the first component? (c) A researcher wants a 2-D view: one or two components?
:::
(a) $C=\tfrac14\begin{bmatrix}8&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&1/2\end{bmatrix}$, eigenvalues $2$ and $\tfrac12$. (b) $T_1=2/2.5=0.8$, i.e. 80%. (c) One component for a compressed 1-D representation; two for the requested 2-D visualisation — which keeps 100% of the variance but reduces nothing.
:::

:::remember
Choose the smallest $k$ with $\sum_{i\le k}\lambda_i/\sum_i\lambda_i$ above the target (e.g. 0.8 or 0.95).
:::

:::example $d=2$, $n=10$, $k=1$
Steps 1–3 gave $\bar x=(1.81,1.91)$ and $C=\begin{bmatrix}0.5549&0.5539\\0.5539&0.6449\end{bmatrix}$.

**Step 4.** The characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2-1.1998\lambda+0.0511$, so
$$
\lambda=\tfrac12\Big[1.1998\pm\sqrt{1.1998^2-4(0.0511)}\Big]=\tfrac12\big[1.1998\pm1.1114\big],\qquad\lambda_1=1.1556,\quad\lambda_2=0.0442 .
$$
Check: $\lambda_1+\lambda_2=1.1998=\operatorname{tr}C$. Normalised eigenvectors:
$$
v_1=\begin{bmatrix}0.6779\\0.7352\end{bmatrix},\qquad v_2=\begin{bmatrix}-0.7352\\0.6779\end{bmatrix}.
$$
The slides print $V=\begin{bmatrix}-0.7352&0.6779\\0.6779&0.7352\end{bmatrix}$ and $D=\operatorname{diag}(0.0442,\,1.1556)$ — ascending order, so the principal component is the **second** column there.

**Reading the answer.** The data spread by $1.1556$ along $v_1$ and only $0.0442$ along $v_2$ — 26 times less. $v_1$ points $47.3^\circ$ above the $x_1$ axis, almost the diagonal: it measures the **overall level** of the two sensors. $v_2$ is the **difference** between them, which is nearly constant.

**Step 5.** $W=[v_1]$. For sample 1, $s_1=(0.69,0.49)$:
$$
z_1=v_1^\top s_1=0.6779(0.69)+0.7352(0.49)=0.828,\qquad \hat x_1=\bar x+z_1v_1=(2.371,\,2.519)\approx x_1=(2.5,2.4).
$$
All ten centred scores: $0.828,\ -1.778,\ 0.992,\ 0.274,\ 1.676,\ 0.913,\ -0.099,\ -1.145,\ -0.438,\ -1.224$. The ten squared reconstruction errors add up to $0.4418$, so their mean is $0.0442=\lambda_2$ — exactly the discarded eigenvalue (Key fact 3). The variance kept is $T_1=1.1556/1.1998\approx0.963$: **one number per reading keeps 96.3% of the variation.**

**The slides' scores** ($3.4591,\ 0.8536,\ 3.6233,\dots$) are $v_1^\top x_i$ from the **uncentred** data. They differ from ours by the constant $v_1^\top\bar x=0.6779(1.81)+0.7352(1.91)=2.6311$: for sample 1, $0.8280+2.6311=3.4591$ ✓.
:::

## 8. Geometric picture, and the link to the SVD

**Change of coordinates, then drop.** $z=V^\top s$ is a rotation of the centred cloud onto the axes $v_1,v_2$ (no distances change); dropping $z_2$ moves every point perpendicularly onto the line through $\bar x$ in direction $v_1$. The residuals are perpendicular to that line — the Pythagoras picture of Key fact 3. For sample 1, $\|s_1\|^2=0.7162=0.6855+0.0307$ (kept + lost). PCA chooses the line that makes these perpendicular residuals as short as possible on average.

**The principal components are the axes of the data cloud.** The cloud is roughly an ellipse with axes along the $v_j$ and semi-axes $\sqrt{\lambda_j}$: here $\sqrt{1.1556}=1.075$ along $v_1$ and $\sqrt{0.0442}=0.210$ along $v_2$, about five times longer than wide. This is L07's circle-to-ellipse picture, drawn around data.

:::thm PCA is the SVD of the centred data
Let $S\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$ (samples as columns) have SVD $S=U\Sigma V^\top$. Then
$$
C=\frac1nSS^\top=\frac1nU\Sigma V^\top V\Sigma^\top U^\top=U\Big(\frac{\Sigma\Sigma^\top}{n}\Big)U^\top ,
$$
an eigen-decomposition of $C$. So **the principal components are the left singular vectors of $S$**, and
$$
\lambda_j=\frac{\sigma_j^2}{n}.
$$
Keeping $k$ components is keeping $k$ layers of the SVD — the best rank-$k$ approximation of L07, which is one more proof that PCA is optimal. For the sensors, $S$ ($2\times10$) has $\sigma=3.3994,\ 0.6646$, and $\sigma_j^2/10=1.1556,\ 0.0442$ ✓.
:::

:::example PSet 7, Problem 8 — the rows convention
:::orig [PSet 7 · Problem 8 ↗](pdfs/psets/PSet-07-with-Solutions.pdf#page=3)
A centred data matrix has SVD $X_c=U\Sigma V^\top$. Show how $C=\tfrac1nX_c^\top X_c$ can be written with $V$ and $\Sigma$. If $\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\cdots$, which columns of $V$ give a $k$-dimensional PCA representation?
:::
Here samples are **rows** ($X_c=S^\top\in\mathbb R^{n\times d}$), as in most software. Using $U^\top U=I$,
$$
C=\frac1nX_c^\top X_c=\frac1nV\Sigma^\top U^\top U\Sigma V^\top=V\Big(\frac{\Sigma^\top\Sigma}{n}\Big)V^\top .
$$
So the columns of $V$ — the **right** singular vectors of $X_c$ — are the covariance eigenvectors, with eigenvalues $\sigma_i^2/n$. Use the first $k$ columns of $V$, which belong to the largest singular values and hence the largest variances. (Samples as columns → left singular vectors; samples as rows → right singular vectors. Same answer, transposed.)

In practice the SVD of the data is preferred to forming $C$, because computing $SS^\top$ squares the numbers and loses small values to rounding.
:::

:::remember
PCs = left singular vectors of the $d\times n$ centred data (right singular vectors if samples are rows), with $\lambda_j=\sigma_j^2/n$.
:::

## 9. Practical considerations and limitations

**Normalise first (slide p27).** PCA looks for variance, and variance depends on units. If sensor 1 reported in millimetres instead of metres (every $x_1$ times $100$), then $C=\begin{bmatrix}5549&55.39\\55.39&0.6449\end{bmatrix}$, the first component would be almost exactly the $x_1$ axis, $v_1\approx(1.000,0.010)$, carrying 99.998% of the variance — only because those numbers are bigger. The usual remedy: **standardise** each feature — subtract its mean and divide by its standard deviation — so every feature has variance 1. The covariance of standardised data is the **correlation matrix**, here $R=\begin{bmatrix}1&0.926\\0.926&1\end{bmatrix}$, with eigenvalues $1\pm\rho=1.926,\ 0.074$ and eigenvectors $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,\pm1)$; the first component keeps $96.3\%$ again.

**PCA decorrelates.** The covariance of the reduced features is diagonal (Key fact 2): the new features are uncorrelated.

**Limitation: variance is not class separation.** PCA never sees the class labels, so there is no guarantee that the directions of largest variance separate the classes. Example (revision notes): class A at $x_2=+1$ and class B at $x_2=-1$, each with $x_1\in\{-4,-2,2,4\}$. Then $C=\operatorname{diag}(10,1)$, so PC1 is the $x_1$ axis — and projecting onto it puts the two classes on top of each other, while the discarded PC2 (only $1/11$ of the variance) separates them perfectly. Standardising does not help (both variances become 1 and PCA has no preferred direction). The solution is **LDA**, which finds the directions along which the classes are best separated: PCA asks "where is the variation?", LDA asks "where is the difference between the classes?".

:::example PSet 7, Problem 10 — a PCA pipeline
:::orig [PSet 7 · Problem 10 ↗](pdfs/psets/PSet-07-with-Solutions.pdf#page=4)
Thousands of customer records with hundreds of numeric measurements; the downstream model is too slow. (a) Describe a PCA preprocessing pipeline, including centring and the choice of $k$. (b) Two measurements use very different units (income in dollars, age in years): what should you check, and what would you do if scale differences are not meaningful? (c) Give one measure of information retained and one downstream check.
:::
(a) Split into training and evaluation sets. Compute feature means **on the training set only**, centre the training data, fit PCA on it, and apply the **same** means and $W$ to the evaluation data. Project both onto the first $k$ components and train the model on the reduced features. Choose $k$ by a target variance fraction $T_k$ or by validation performance.

(b) Check whether the units should influence the analysis. If not, standardise each feature with its training-set mean and standard deviation before PCA, and apply those same values to the evaluation data.

(c) The fraction of variance retained $T_k$; and compare validation performance and run time against the model on all features.
:::

**Applications.** **Compression:** store the scores $z_i\in\mathbb R^k$ plus $\bar x$ and $W$ — for $10\,000$ images of $784$ pixels and $k=50$, that is $10\,000\cdot50+784\cdot50+784=539\,984$ numbers instead of $7\,840\,000$ (about 6.9%). **Denoising:** noise spreads over all directions while the signal sits in a few large-variance ones, so dropping the small components removes most of the noise. **Visualisation:** with $k=2$ or $3$, clusters and outliers in high-dimensional data become visible. **Features for another model:** $k$ uncorrelated high-variance features mean fewer parameters and less overfitting — PCA's answer to the curse of dimensionality.

:::remember
Standardise features with different units; fit the mean and $W$ on training data only; and remember that PCA ignores labels — high variance is not the same as useful for classification.
:::

## 10. Summary

| idea | key fact | in the two-sensor example |
|---|---|---|
| curse of dimensionality | $m^d$ cells; shell fraction $1-(1-\epsilon)^d$ | $d=400$: all but $5\times10^{-19}$ in the shell |
| mapping | $z=W^\top(x-\bar x)$, $W\in\mathbb R^{d\times k}$ orthonormal | $W=[v_1]\in\mathbb R^{2\times1}$ |
| covariance | $C=\tfrac1nSS^\top$, symmetric PSD | $C=\begin{bmatrix}0.5549&0.5539\\0.5539&0.6449\end{bmatrix}$, $\rho=0.926$ |
| directions | $v_j$ eigenvectors of $C$; $\lambda_j$ = variance along $v_j$; scores uncorrelated | $\lambda=1.1556,\ 0.0442$; $v_1$ at $47.3^\circ$ |
| information lost | error $=\sum_{j>k}\lambda_j$; kept $T_k$ | $0.0442$; $T_1=0.963$ |
| geometry | axes of the data ellipse, semi-axes $\sqrt{\lambda_j}$ | $1.075$ and $0.210$ |
| SVD link | $S=U\Sigma V^\top$: PCs $=u_j$, $\lambda_j=\sigma_j^2/n$ | $\sigma=3.40,\ 0.66$ |
| limits | depends on scale; ignores labels | $\times100$ on $x_1$ hijacks PC1 |
