The eigen-decomposition of L06 needs a square matrix, and works best when the matrix is symmetric. The singular value decomposition (SVD) works for **every** matrix, of any shape. In order, this lecture covers: positive and negative (semi-)definite matrices, with their "angle" picture and the eigenvalue test; why $A^\top A$ and $AA^\top$ are always positive semi-definite; the SVD $A=U\Sigma V^\top$ and the shape of $\Sigma$; how to compute it from $A^\top A$; its geometry (rotate, stretch, rotate: a circle becomes an ellipse); the rank as the number of non-zero singular values, and how to estimate the rank of noisy data; and the rank-$k$ approximation used for compression.

:::intuition The lifting filter (revision notes)
A graphics program lifts a flat picture onto a tilted sheet in 3D: a pixel $x\in\mathbb R^2$ goes to $Ax\in\mathbb R^3$ with
$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}1&-0.8\\0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}.
$$
Every output satisfies $y_3=y_1+0.8\,y_2$, so the picture lands on a flat sheet, and a round logo becomes a tilted oval on that sheet. We cannot describe $A$ with eigenvectors — $Aq=\lambda q$ is meaningless when $q\in\mathbb R^2$ and $Aq\in\mathbb R^3$. The SVD fixes this by allowing **different** special directions on the input side ($v_i\in\mathbb R^2$) and on the output side ($u_i\in\mathbb R^3$), with $Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$. It is exactly the matrix the slides use for their geometric example.
:::

## 1. Positive and negative (semi-)definite matrices

:::figs
l07-p03|Definitions: PSD, PD, NSD, ND
:::

:::background quadratic forms — a single number from a vector and a matrix
For a square $A\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ and $x\in\mathbb R^n$, the number
$$
x^\top Ax=x\cdot(Ax)
$$
is a **quadratic form**: a $1\times n$ row times an $n\times n$ matrix times an $n\times1$ column, so the result is $1\times1$. Written out for $n=2$,
$$
\begin{bmatrix}x_1&x_2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}=ax_1^2+2b\,x_1x_2+d\,x_2^2 .
$$
Every term has degree two, hence "quadratic". Example: with $a=2$, $b=1$, $d=2$ and $x=(1,-1)$, the value is $2-2+2=2$.
:::

:::def Definiteness
For $A\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$:

| condition, for all $x$ | name |
|---|---|
| $x^\top Ax\ge0$ | positive semi-definite (**PSD**) |
| $x^\top Ax>0$ for $x\neq\mathbf 0$ | positive definite (**PD**) |
| $x^\top Ax\le0$ | negative semi-definite (**NSD**) |
| $x^\top Ax<0$ for $x\neq\mathbf 0$ | negative definite (**ND**) |
| takes both signs | **indefinite** |

The strict versions must exclude $x=\mathbf 0$, since $\mathbf 0^\top A\mathbf 0=0$ for every matrix. PD is a special case of PSD.
:::

:::figs
l07-p04|Interpretation: Ax stays within 90° of x
:::

**The picture.** Let $y=Ax$. Then $x^\top Ax=x^\top y$ is the dot product of $x$ with its own image, and from L01 a dot product is $\ge0$ exactly when the angle between the vectors is at most $90^\circ$. So

- **PSD:** $A$ never turns a vector by more than a right angle; $Ax$ lies in the half-plane on $x$'s side, the dashed boundary $x^\top Ax=0$ included;
- **PD:** the angle is strictly less than $90^\circ$, so the boundary is excluded;
- **indefinite:** some vectors are turned forward and others turned back.

:::remember
$x^\top Ax$ is the dot product of $x$ with $Ax$. PSD means $A$ never turns any vector by more than $90^\circ$; PD means strictly less than $90^\circ$.
:::

## 2. The eigenvalue test for symmetric matrices

:::figs
l07-p06|Eigenvalues of a symmetric PD/PSD matrix
:::

Testing every $x$ is impossible, but for a **symmetric** matrix the eigenvalues decide everything.

**The slide's argument (one direction).** Suppose $A$ is symmetric and PD, and $q$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$. Taking $x=q$,
$$
q^\top Aq>0\ \Longrightarrow\ \lambda\,q^\top q>0\ \Longrightarrow\ \lambda\,\|q\|_2^2>0\ \Longrightarrow\ \lambda>0 ,
$$
since $\|q\|_2^2>0$. In the same way, PSD gives $\lambda\ge0$, NSD gives $\lambda\le0$ and ND gives $\lambda<0$.

:::derive the other direction: all $\lambda_i>0$ makes $A$ PD
Write $A=Q\Lambda Q^\top$ (the spectral theorem, L06) and set $c=Q^\top x$, the coordinates of $x$ in the eigenvector basis. Then
$$
x^\top Ax=x^\top Q\Lambda Q^\top x=c^\top\Lambda c=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i\,c_i^2 .
$$
Each $c_i^2\ge0$, and $c\neq\mathbf 0$ whenever $x\neq\mathbf 0$ (because $Q$ is invertible). So if every $\lambda_i>0$, the sum is positive; if every $\lambda_i\ge0$ it is non-negative; and if the signs are mixed, choosing $x=q_i$ for a positive and a negative $\lambda_i$ gives both signs.
:::

:::thm Definiteness from eigenvalues (symmetric $A$)
$$
\text{PD}\iff\text{all }\lambda_i>0,\qquad\text{PSD}\iff\text{all }\lambda_i\ge0,\qquad\text{ND}\iff\text{all }\lambda_i<0,\qquad\text{NSD}\iff\text{all }\lambda_i\le0,
$$
and $A$ is **indefinite** when the eigenvalues have mixed signs. For a $2\times2$ symmetric matrix, a quick test: $\det A=\lambda_1\lambda_2$ and $\operatorname{tr}A=\lambda_1+\lambda_2$, so

- $\det A>0$ and $\operatorname{tr}A>0$ ⇒ PD;
- $\det A<0$ ⇒ indefinite;
- $\det A=0$ and $\operatorname{tr}A\ge0$ ⇒ PSD but not PD.
:::

:::example The three filters of L06 (revision notes)
- $S=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ has $\lambda=3,1$: **PD**. Directly, $x^\top Sx=2x_1^2+2x_1x_2+2x_2^2=x_1^2+x_2^2+(x_1+x_2)^2>0$ for $x\ne\mathbf 0$.
- $F=\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$ has $\lambda=1,0$: **PSD, not PD**. Indeed $x^\top Fx=\tfrac12(x_1+x_2)^2$ is never negative, but it is $0$ at $x=(1,-1)$, the eigenvector of $\lambda=0$.
- $M=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\2&0\end{bmatrix}$ has $\lambda=4,-1$: **indefinite**. $x=(1,0)$ gives $x^\top Mx=3$, while $x=(1,-2)$ gives $3-8+0=-5$.

**Check yourself.** Is $\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}$ positive definite? Its trace is $2$ and its determinant is $1-4=-3<0$, so $\lambda=3,-1$: indefinite, not PD — even though every entry is positive.
:::

:::warn
Positive **entries** do not make a matrix positive definite (the example just above), and $\det A>0$ alone does not either: $\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1)$ has determinant $1$ and is negative definite. Use the eigenvalues, or the trace *and* determinant together for $2\times2$.
:::

**Level curves.** For a PD matrix the curves $x^\top Ax=c$ are ellipses whose axes lie along the eigenvectors, with half-lengths $\sqrt{c/\lambda_i}$; a zero eigenvalue stretches the ellipse into a pair of parallel lines; mixed signs open it into hyperbolas (a saddle). This is the bowl shape behind least squares and ridge regression in L05.

:::remember
For symmetric $A$: $x^\top Ax=\sum_i\lambda_ic_i^2$, so the signs of the eigenvalues give the definiteness.
:::

## 3. Gram matrices $A^\top A$ and $AA^\top$ are always PSD

Take **any** $A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$, square or not. We cannot ask for its eigenvalues, but we can form two square symmetric matrices, $A^\top A\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ and $AA^\top\in\mathbb R^{m\times m}$. Their quadratic forms are squared lengths:
$$
x^\top(A^\top A)\,x=(Ax)^\top(Ax)=\|Ax\|_2^2\ \ge\ 0 .
$$
So $A^\top A$ "knows" how much $A$ stretches every vector $x$. This is the observation the whole SVD rests on — and the answer to the slide's "How? You are fully equipped to show this."

:::thm Gram matrices
For every $A$, the matrices $A^\top A$ and $AA^\top$ are **symmetric** ($(A^\top A)^\top=A^\top A$) and **positive semi-definite**, so all their eigenvalues are real and $\ge0$. Moreover $A^\top A$ is **positive definite exactly when the columns of $A$ are linearly independent**: $\|Ax\|^2=0$ forces $Ax=\mathbf 0$, which for independent columns happens only at $x=\mathbf 0$.
:::

:::example The lifting filter, and a quick count
For the lifting filter,
$$
A^\top A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\-0.8&1&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&-0.8\\0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&-0.8\\-0.8&1.64\end{bmatrix},
$$
which is PD because the two columns of $A$ are independent ($\det=3.28-0.64=2.64>0$, trace $3.64>0$). The $3\times3$ matrix $AA^\top$ is only PSD: the output sheet is flat, so one direction of $\mathbb R^3$ is never reached.

**Check yourself.** $A\in\mathbb R^{5\times3}$ has rank $3$. Then $A^\top A$ ($3\times3$, rank $3$) is PD, while $AA^\top$ ($5\times5$, rank $3$) has $5-3=2$ zero eigenvalues.
:::

:::remember
$x^\top A^\top Ax=\|Ax\|^2\ge0$: Gram matrices are symmetric PSD, so their eigenvalues are $\ge0$ and have real square roots — the singular values.
:::

## 4. The SVD: $A=U\Sigma V^\top$

:::figs
l07-p10|Formulation and the shape of Σ
:::

:::thm Singular value decomposition
Every $A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ can be written
$$
A=U\,\Sigma\,V^\top ,
$$
where

- $U\in\mathbb R^{m\times m}$ is orthogonal; its columns $u_i$ are the **left singular vectors** and live in the output space $\mathbb R^m$;
- $V\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}$ is orthogonal; its columns $v_i$ are the **right singular vectors** and live in the input space $\mathbb R^n$;
- $\Sigma\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$ has the shape of $A$ and is zero except on its diagonal, which holds the **singular values**
$$
\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_{\min(m,n)}\ge0 .
$$
The SVD **always exists**, for every matrix — which is why Strang called it the "fundamental theorem of linear algebra".
:::

**The shape of $\Sigma$** (slide p10). There are $\min(m,n)$ singular values:

- $m<n$ (wide $A$): a square diagonal block followed by $n-m$ **zero columns**;
- $m=n$: square and diagonal;
- $m>n$ (tall $A$): a square diagonal block on top of $m-n$ **zero rows**.

For the lifting filter ($m=3$, $n=2$) the shapes are $\underbrace{A}_{3\times2}=\underbrace{U}_{3\times3}\,\underbrace{\Sigma}_{3\times2}\,\underbrace{V^\top}_{2\times2}$. Always track the sizes: $V^\top$ acts on inputs in $\mathbb R^n$, $\Sigma$ converts from $\mathbb R^n$ to $\mathbb R^m$, and $U$ acts on outputs in $\mathbb R^m$.

:::figs
l07-p11|Left and right singular vectors; ordering of the σ's
:::

:::thm The one sentence to remember: $Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$
Multiply $A=U\Sigma V^\top$ on the right by $V$ and use $V^\top V=I$:
$$
AV=U\Sigma\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad A\,v_i=\sigma_i\,u_i\quad\text{(column }i\text{)} .
$$
$A$ sends each right singular vector $v_i$ to the left singular vector $u_i$, stretched by $\sigma_i$. Likewise $A^\top u_i=\sigma_iv_i$.
:::

**Compact (thin) form.** If only $r$ singular values are non-zero, the remaining columns of $U$ and $V$ only ever multiply zeros, so
$$
A=U_r\,\Sigma_r\,V_r^\top,\qquad (m\times r)(r\times r)(r\times n).
$$

:::example Shapes and a diagonal matrix (PSet 7, Problem 1)
:::orig [PSet 7 · Problem 1 ↗](pdfs/psets/PSet-07-with-Solutions.pdf#page=1)
Consider $A=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. (a) Give $U$, $\Sigma$, $V$ with $A=U\Sigma V^\top$. (b) What are the singular values of $A$? Which input direction is stretched more by $x\mapsto Ax$?
:::
(a) $A$ is diagonal with non-negative, already sorted entries, so $U=I_2$, $\Sigma=A$, $V=I_2$ works.

(b) The singular values are $3$ and $1$. The direction $(1,0)^\top$ is stretched by $3$ and $(0,1)^\top$ by $1$.

**Check yourself.** For $A\in\mathbb R^{5\times3}$ the shapes are $U:5\times5$, $\Sigma:5\times3$, $V^\top:3\times3$; there are $3$ singular values and the last $5-3=2$ rows of $\Sigma$ are zero.

**A negative entry.** $D=\operatorname{diag}(3,-2)$ has singular values $3$ and $2$, not $3$ and $-2$: singular values are never negative. The sign moves into $U$:
$$
D=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}.
$$
:::

:::remember
$A=U\Sigma V^\top$ for every $A$; $\Sigma$ has the shape of $A$, $\min(m,n)$ non-negative singular values in decreasing order, and $Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$.
:::

## 5. Computing the SVD

:::figs
l07-p12|AAᵀ = U ΣΣᵀ Uᵀ gives U
l07-p13|AᵀA = V ΣᵀΣ Vᵀ gives V
:::

**The slides' derivation.** Substitute $A=U\Sigma V^\top$ into the two Gram matrices and cancel $V^\top V=I$ or $U^\top U=I$:
$$
AA^\top=U\Sigma V^\top V\Sigma^\top U^\top=U\,(\Sigma\Sigma^\top)\,U^\top,\qquad
A^\top A=V\Sigma^\top U^\top U\Sigma V^\top=V\,(\Sigma^\top\Sigma)\,V^\top .
$$
$\Sigma\Sigma^\top$ is $m\times m$ diagonal and $\Sigma^\top\Sigma$ is $n\times n$ diagonal, both holding $\sigma_i^2$ (padded with zeros). So these are eigen-decompositions:

- the **left singular vectors** (columns of $U$) are eigenvectors of $AA^\top$;
- the **right singular vectors** (columns of $V$) are eigenvectors of $A^\top A$;
- $\sigma_i^2=\lambda_i$, the eigenvalues, which are $\ge0$ because Gram matrices are PSD (§3). **That is why singular values are never negative** — the question at the bottom of slide p13.

:::derive why $v_1$ is the direction of largest stretch
Write a unit vector $x=\sum_ic_iv_i$ in the eigenvector basis of $A^\top A$, with $\sum_ic_i^2=1$. Then
$$
\|Ax\|^2=x^\top A^\top Ax=\sum_i\lambda_ic_i^2 ,
$$
a weighted average of the eigenvalues. It is largest when all the weight is on $\lambda_1$, i.e. $x=v_1$, giving $\|Av_1\|=\sqrt{\lambda_1}=\sigma_1$. The best direction perpendicular to $v_1$ is $v_2$, and so on. So $\sigma_1=\max_{\|x\|=1}\|Ax\|$ is the largest stretch $A$ can produce.
:::

:::def The recipe (revision notes)
1. Form $A^\top A$ and find its eigenvalues $\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge0$. Then $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}$.
2. Find orthonormal eigenvectors $v_i$: the columns of $V$.
3. For each $\sigma_i>0$ set $u_i=\dfrac{Av_i}{\sigma_i}$.
4. If $m>r$, complete $U$ with unit vectors perpendicular to $u_1,\dots,u_r$ (they solve $A^\top w=\mathbf 0$).

Sanity checks: $\sum_i\sigma_i^2=\|A\|_F^2$ (sum of squares of all entries); for square $A$, $\sigma_1\cdots\sigma_n=|\det A|$.
:::

:::warn The sign trap
It is tempting to take $U$ from a **separate** eigen-decomposition of $AA^\top$. Don't: eigenvectors are only defined up to sign, and only one sign of $u_i$ satisfies $Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$. Get $V$ from $A^\top A$, then **define** $u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i$. (If the exam asks for $U$ from $AA^\top$, check each pair with $Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$ and flip signs as needed.)
:::

:::example $B=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix}$ by hand (revision notes)
**Step 1.** $B^\top B=\begin{bmatrix}3&4\\0&5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}25&20\\20&25\end{bmatrix}$.

**Step 2.** $(25-\lambda)^2-400=0$ gives $25-\lambda=\pm20$, so $\lambda=45,5$ and
$$
\sigma_1=\sqrt{45}=3\sqrt5\approx6.71,\qquad\sigma_2=\sqrt5\approx2.24 .
$$
Check: $\sigma_1\sigma_2=15=|\det B|$ and $\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2=50=9+0+16+25$.

**Step 3.** For $\lambda=45$, $(B^\top B-45I)v=\mathbf 0$ gives $v_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$; for $\lambda=5$, $v_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(-1,1)$.

**Step 4.**
$$
u_1=\frac{Bv_1}{\sigma_1}=\frac{1}{3\sqrt5}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}3\\9\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\3\end{bmatrix},\qquad
u_2=\frac{Bv_2}{\sigma_2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}-3\\1\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}-3\\1\end{bmatrix}.
$$
So
$$
B=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-3\\3&1\end{bmatrix}}_{U}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}3\sqrt5&0\\0&\sqrt5\end{bmatrix}}_{\Sigma}\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}}_{V^\top}.
$$
**The sign trap in action.** $BB^\top$ also has $\tfrac1{\sqrt{10}}(3,-1)$ as an eigenvector for $\sigma_2^2=5$; using it in place of $u_2$ gives $\begin{bmatrix}0&3\\5&4\end{bmatrix}\neq B$.

**SVD ≠ EVD.** The eigenvalues of $B$ itself are $3$ and $5$ (it is triangular), its eigenvectors are not perpendicular, and they have nothing to do with $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$.
:::

:::example The lifting filter (the slides' p17 numbers)
**Steps 1–2.** $A^\top A=\begin{bmatrix}2&-0.8\\-0.8&1.64\end{bmatrix}$ has trace $3.64$ and determinant $2.64$, so $\lambda^2-3.64\lambda+2.64=(\lambda-2.64)(\lambda-1)=0$:
$$
\sigma_1=\sqrt{2.64}\approx1.62,\qquad\sigma_2=1 .
$$
**Step 3.** $v_1\approx(-0.78,\,0.62)$ and $v_2\approx(-0.62,\,-0.78)$.

**Step 4.** $u_1=Av_1/\sigma_1\approx(-0.79,\,0.38,\,-0.48)$ and $u_2=Av_2/\sigma_2\approx(0,\,-0.78,\,-0.62)$. Since $m=3>r=2$, one more column is needed: $A^\top w=\mathbf 0$ gives $u_3=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2.64}}(-1,-0.8,1)\approx(-0.62,-0.49,0.62)$, the normal of the sheet $y_3=y_1+0.8y_2$. Altogether
$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}-0.79&0&-0.62\\0.38&-0.78&-0.49\\-0.48&-0.62&0.62\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1.62&0\\0&1.0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-0.78&0.62\\-0.62&-0.78\end{bmatrix},
$$
exactly the decomposition printed on slide p17.
:::

$V$ and $\sigma_i^2$ come from the EVD of $A^\top A$; then $u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i$. Never pair up two independent EVDs.

## 6. Geometry: rotate, stretch, rotate

:::figs
l07-p15|V^T, then Σ, then U
l07-p16|The unit circle becomes an ellipse with semi-axes σᵢuᵢ
:::

Read $Ax=U\Sigma V^\top x$ from right to left (slide p15):

1. **$V^\top$ — change of basis in $\mathbb R^n$.** It re-describes $x$ using the perpendicular directions $v_1,\dots,v_n$ as axes. Lengths and angles do not change, so a circle stays a circle.
2. **$\Sigma$ — scaling in the new basis.** Axis $i$ is stretched by $\sigma_i$, so the circle becomes an axis-aligned ellipse. This step also changes the dimension from $n$ to $m$: if $m<n$ the last $n-m$ coordinates are **dropped** (the zero columns of $\Sigma$); if $m>n$, $m-n$ zero coordinates are **appended** (the zero rows).
3. **$U$ — change of basis in $\mathbb R^m$.** The ellipse is rotated onto the output directions $u_1,\dots,u_m$.

:::thm Circles become ellipses
The image of the unit circle (ball) under $A$ is an ellipse (ellipsoid) with semi-axes $\sigma_1u_1,\ \sigma_2u_2,\ \dots,\ \sigma_ru_r$. The lengths of the axes are the singular values; their directions are the left singular vectors; and $\sigma_1$ is the largest stretch $A$ can produce.

*Why:* a unit $x=\sum_ic_iv_i$ goes to $Ax=\sum_i\sigma_ic_iu_i$, and the $u_i$ are perpendicular, so the coordinate along $u_i$ is $\sigma_ic_i$, at most $\sigma_i$ (reached at $x=v_i$).
:::

:::figs
l07-p17|The lifting filter: a square canvas lands on a tilted sheet in ℝ³
:::

:::example Two pictures
**The lifting filter (slide p17).** $V^\top$ rotates the square canvas; $\Sigma$ stretches it by $1.62$ and $1$ and appends a third coordinate equal to $0$ — the flat square now sits in the $y_1y_2$-plane of $\mathbb R^3$ ("append 1 more basis: the impact of a row of zeros in $\Sigma$"); $U$ rotates that plane onto the tilted sheet. The unit circle becomes an ellipse with semi-axes $1.62\,u_1$ and $1.0\,u_2$, lying in the sheet; $u_3$ is perpendicular to the sheet and never reached.

**$B=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix}$.** $V^\top$ is a rotation by $-45^\circ$, $\Sigma$ stretches the axes by $6.71$ and $2.24$, and $U$ is a rotation by $+71.6^\circ$. The ellipse has area $\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2=15\pi$, so $B$ multiplies areas by $15=|\det B|$.
:::

**What $A$ reaches and what it destroys.** If $r$ singular values are non-zero:

| vectors | span | dimension |
|---|---|---|
| $u_1,\dots,u_r$ | column space $\mathcal C(A)\subseteq\mathbb R^m$: every possible output | $r$ |
| $u_{r+1},\dots,u_m$ | never reached: $\mathcal N(A^\top)$ | $m-r$ |
| $v_1,\dots,v_r$ | row space $\mathcal C(A^\top)$ | $r$ |
| $v_{r+1},\dots,v_n$ | null space $\mathcal N(A)$: squashed to $\mathbf 0$ | $n-r$ |

This is rank–nullity (L03) read off the SVD. For example $R=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\\2&4\end{bmatrix}$ (second column $=2\times$ first) has $\sigma_1=3\sqrt5$ and $\sigma_2=0$: its "ellipse" has zero width, so the unit circle is flattened onto a segment of length $2\sigma_1=6\sqrt5\approx13.4$ along $u_1$, and the direction $v_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt5}(-2,1)$ is lost for good.

:::note Symmetric matrices
If $A$ is symmetric, then $\sigma_i=|\lambda_i|$ and $U$, $V$ agree up to signs; a negative eigenvalue becomes a flip inside $U$. For $M=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\2&0\end{bmatrix}$, $\lambda=4,-1$ gives $\sigma=4,1$. If $A$ is symmetric **PSD**, the SVD and the EVD are the same factorisation.
:::

:::remember
SVD = rotate ($V^\top$), stretch by $\sigma_i$ (and drop or append dimensions), rotate ($U$): a circle becomes an ellipse with semi-axes $\sigma_iu_i$.
:::

## 7. Rank, and estimating the rank of noisy data

:::figs
l07-p18|Rank = number of non-zero singular values; rank estimation
:::

:::thm Rank from the SVD
The rank of $A$ equals the **number of non-zero singular values**.

*Why:* $U$ and $V^\top$ are invertible and multiplying by an invertible matrix does not change the rank, so $\operatorname{rank}A=\operatorname{rank}\Sigma$, the number of non-zero diagonal entries. (The slide's argument: $\operatorname{rank}A^\top A=\operatorname{rank}A$, and the rank of the **symmetric** matrix $A^\top A$ is its number of non-zero eigenvalues, which are the $\sigma_i^2$.)
:::

:::warn
"Rank = number of non-zero **eigenvalues**" is true for symmetric (diagonalisable) matrices such as $A^\top A$, but **not** for every square matrix: $\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ has rank $1$ and both eigenvalues $0$. Its singular values are $1$ and $0$, which count the rank correctly.
:::

**Application — the rank of noisy data (slide p18).** Stack $n$ data points $a_1,\dots,a_n\in\mathbb R^m$ as the columns of $A\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$, with $n\gg m$. If the points satisfy linear relations, so that they lie in an $r$-dimensional subspace, then $\operatorname{rank}A=r$. But measurements carry noise, so in practice $A$ has full rank $m$. The SVD still sees the structure: $r$ singular values are **large** and the rest are **close to zero** (about the size of the noise). Counting the large ones gives the **approximate rank**.

:::example Eight noisy points on a plane (revision notes)
Eight points in $\mathbb R^3$ lie on the plane $y_3=y_1+y_2$ (true rank $2$), with noise of about $0.05$ in every entry. The singular values of the $3\times8$ data matrix are
$$
\sigma_1\approx5.83,\qquad\sigma_2\approx2.20,\qquad\sigma_3\approx0.15 .
$$
Two are large and one is tiny, so the approximate rank is $2$ — found without being told about the plane. The last left singular vector $u_3\approx(-0.60,-0.54,0.59)$ is almost the plane's normal $\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(-1,-1,1)$: the direction in which the data barely vary. There is no universal cut-off; look for a clear **gap** ($2.20\to0.15$ here).

**Check yourself.** A $4\times100$ data matrix has singular values $10,\ 9.5,\ 0.01,\ 0.008$. Approximate rank: $2$.

**PSet 7, Problem 4(a).** Singular values $10,\ 2,\ 0.5$ are all non-zero, so the rank is $3$.
:::

:::remember
$\operatorname{rank}A=\#\{\sigma_i>0\}$; with noisy data, count the singular values before the big gap.
:::

## 8. Low-rank approximation and compression

:::figs
l07-p19|A = Σ uᵢσᵢvᵢᵀ and the k-rank approximation
:::

**A matrix as a sum of layers.** Multiplying out $U\Sigma V^\top$ column by column (the outer-product view of L02),
$$
A=U\Sigma V^\top=\sum_{i=1}^{\min(m,n)}\sigma_i\,u_iv_i^\top=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_i\,u_iv_i^\top .
$$
Each layer $u_iv_i^\top$ is an $m\times n$ matrix of **rank one** (a column times a row) with strength $\sigma_i$. The sum can stop at $r=\operatorname{rank}A$ because the remaining $\sigma_i$ are $0$. Keeping only the first $k$ layers gives the **rank-$k$ approximation**
$$
A\approx A_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_i\,u_iv_i^\top=U_k\Sigma_kV_k^\top,\qquad\operatorname{rank}A_k=k .
$$

:::thm How good is $A_k$? (Eckart–Young, used without proof)
The error is the sum of the discarded layers, so it is controlled by the discarded singular values:
$$
\|A-A_k\|_F^2=\sigma_{k+1}^2+\cdots+\sigma_r^2,\qquad \|A-A_k\|_2=\sigma_{k+1}\ \text{(largest stretch of the error)} .
$$
No other matrix of rank $k$ does better. The fraction of the "energy" kept is
$$
\frac{\sigma_1^2+\cdots+\sigma_k^2}{\sigma_1^2+\cdots+\sigma_r^2}=\frac{\|A_k\|_F^2}{\|A\|_F^2}.
$$
:::

**Storage.** $A$ needs $mn$ numbers. $A_k$ needs $k$ vectors $u_i\in\mathbb R^m$, $k$ numbers $\sigma_i$ and $k$ vectors $v_i\in\mathbb R^n$:
$$
\text{storage}(A_k)=mk+k+kn=k(m+n+1).
$$
Increasing $k$ never increases the error, but each extra layer costs $m+n+1$ more numbers: fidelity against compression.

**Geometry of dropping a layer.** Dropping $\sigma_2u_2v_2^\top$ removes the short axis of the ellipse: $A_1$ sends the circle to the segment along the long axis, and what is lost is the half-width $\sigma_2$. The thinner the ellipse, the better a line approximates it.

:::example $B_1$ and the filter (revision notes)
**$B$.** $B_1=\sigma_1u_1v_1^\top=3\sqrt5\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(1,3)\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)^\top=\tfrac32\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\3&3\end{bmatrix}$, so
$$
B-B_1=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}1.5&1.5\\4.5&4.5\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1.5&-1.5\\-0.5&0.5\end{bmatrix}.
$$
The squared error is $2.25+2.25+0.25+0.25=5=\sigma_2^2$ ✓, and the energy kept is $45/50=90\%$.

**The filter.** $A_1=(Av_1)v_1^\top\approx\begin{bmatrix}1&-0.8\\-0.49&0.39\\0.61&-0.49\end{bmatrix}$ keeps only $2.64/3.64\approx72.5\%$; $\sigma_2=1$ is not small, so one layer is a poor approximation.

**Check yourself.** Singular values $4,3,0,0$: $A_1$ keeps $16/25=64\%$, and $A_2=A$ exactly.
:::

```python
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[1, -0.8], [0, 1], [1, 0]])
U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)          # U: 3x3, s: 2 values, Vt: 2x2

A1 = s[0] * np.outer(U[:, 0], Vt[0]) # rank-1 approximation

```

## 9. Summary

| idea | key fact | in the examples |
|---|---|---|
| definiteness | $x^\top Ax=x\cdot Ax$; symmetric: signs of $\lambda_i$ decide | $S$ PD, $F$ PSD, $M$ indefinite |
| Gram matrices | $x^\top A^\top Ax=\|Ax\|^2$, so $A^\top A$, $AA^\top$ are PSD | filter: $A^\top A$ PD, $AA^\top$ PSD |
| SVD | $A=U\Sigma V^\top$, $Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$, $\sigma_i\ge0$ | shapes $3\times3$, $3\times2$, $2\times2$ |
| computing | $V$, $\sigma_i^2$ from $A^\top A$; $u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i$ | $\sigma=1.62,1$ (filter); $3\sqrt5,\sqrt5$ ($B$) |
| geometry | rotate, stretch, rotate: circle → ellipse with semi-axes $\sigma_iu_i$ | filter: ellipse on the sheet |
| rank | $\operatorname{rank}A=\#\{\sigma_i>0\}$; approximate rank from the gap | $5.83, 2.20, 0.15$ ⇒ rank 2 |
| approximation | $A_k=\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_iu_iv_i^\top$, error$^2=\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2$, storage $k(m+n+1)$ | $B_1$ keeps $90\%$ |

**SVD versus EVD.** The EVD needs a square matrix and may need complex numbers; its eigenvectors need not be perpendicular. The SVD exists for every real matrix of any shape, always has real orthogonal $U$ and $V$, and non-negative $\sigma_i$. For symmetric matrices the two agree up to signs: $\sigma_i=|\lambda_i|$.
