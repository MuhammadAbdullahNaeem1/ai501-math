The eigen-decomposition of L06 needs a **square** matrix, and it is only clean for **symmetric** ones. The singular value decomposition (SVD) works for **every** matrix. In order, this lecture covers: positive and negative (semi-)definite matrices, with their geometric meaning and the eigenvalue test; the SVD $A=U\Sigma V^\top$, its shapes and its vocabulary; how to compute it from $A^\top A$ and $AA^\top$; its geometric meaning (rotate, stretch, rotate: a circle becomes an ellipse); the rank of a matrix and rank estimation from noisy data; and low-rank matrix approximation.

:::intuition The flat-to-3D filter (revision notes)
A graphics program lifts a flat picture onto a tilted sheet in 3D: a pixel $x\in\mathbb{R}^2$ goes to $Ax\in\mathbb{R}^3$ with
$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}1&-0.8\\0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times2}.
$$
A round logo of radius 1 becomes a **tilted oval** lying on one flat sheet inside $\mathbb{R}^3$ (every output satisfies $y_3=y_1+0.8\,y_2$).

We would like the "eigen" description of L06 — directions that $A$ only stretches. But $Aq=\lambda q$ is impossible here: $q\in\mathbb{R}^2$ while $Aq\in\mathbb{R}^3$. The SVD fixes this by allowing **different** special directions on the input side ($v_i\in\mathbb{R}^2$) and on the output side ($u_i\in\mathbb{R}^3$):
$$
Av_i=\sigma_iu_i .
$$
:::

## 1. Positive and negative definite matrices

:::figs
l07-p03|Definition: PSD, PD, NSD, ND
l07-p04|PSD: Ax stays in the half-plane of x
l07-p06|Eigenvalues of a symmetric PSD/PD matrix
:::

:::background the quadratic form $x^\top Ax$, and what a dot product's sign means
#### A $1\times n$ row times an $n\times n$ matrix times an $n\times1$ column is a single number
For $A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ and $x=\begin{bmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{bmatrix}$:
$$
Ax=\begin{bmatrix}2x_1+x_2\\x_1+2x_2\end{bmatrix},\qquad
x^\top Ax=\begin{bmatrix}x_1&x_2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}2x_1+x_2\\x_1+2x_2\end{bmatrix}=2x_1^2+2x_1x_2+2x_2^2 .
$$
Every term has degree 2 in the entries of $x$ — that is why it is called a **quadratic** form.

#### The sign of a dot product is the sign of $\cos\theta$
From L01, $u^\top w=\|u\|\,\|w\|\cos\theta$. The lengths are positive, so
$$
u^\top w>0\iff\theta<90^\circ,\qquad u^\top w=0\iff\theta=90^\circ,\qquad u^\top w<0\iff\theta>90^\circ .
$$
Since $x^\top Ax=x\cdot(Ax)$ is the dot product of $x$ with its own image, its sign says whether $A$ keeps $x$ pointing "forwards" or turns it "backwards".
:::

:::def Definite and semi-definite matrices
For $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
x^\top Ax&\ge0\ \ \text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^n &&\Longrightarrow\ A\text{ is \textbf{positive semi-definite} (PSD), written }A\succeq0,\\
x^\top Ax&>0\ \ \text{for all }x\neq\mathbf 0 &&\Longrightarrow\ A\text{ is \textbf{positive definite} (PD), written }A\succ0,\\
x^\top Ax&\le0\ \ \text{for all }x &&\Longrightarrow\ A\text{ is \textbf{negative semi-definite} (NSD)},\\
x^\top Ax&<0\ \ \text{for all }x\neq\mathbf 0 &&\Longrightarrow\ A\text{ is \textbf{negative definite} (ND)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
If $x^\top Ax$ takes **both** signs, $A$ is **indefinite**. (The strict versions must exclude $x=\mathbf 0$, since $\mathbf 0^\top A\mathbf 0=0$ for every matrix; the slide writes "$\forall x$" as shorthand.)
:::

**The interpretation (slides p4–5).** Let $y=Ax$. Then $x^\top Ax=x^\top y$, and:

- **PSD:** $x^\top y\ge0$, so the angle between $x$ and $Ax$ is **at most** $\pi/2$. Graphically, $Ax$ can be anywhere in the half-plane on the side of $x$, **including** the dashed boundary line where $x^\top Ax=0$.
- **PD:** $x^\top y>0$, so the angle is **strictly less** than $\pi/2$: the half-plane **excluding** the boundary.

A PSD matrix never turns a vector by more than a right angle; an indefinite matrix turns some vectors forwards and others backwards.

### The eigenvalue test (symmetric matrices)

:::thm Eigenvalues decide the sign
For a **symmetric** $A$:
$$
A\succ0\iff\text{all }\lambda_i>0,\qquad A\succeq0\iff\text{all }\lambda_i\ge0,\qquad
\text{ND}\iff\text{all }\lambda_i<0,\qquad \text{NSD}\iff\text{all }\lambda_i\le0,
$$
and $A$ is **indefinite** if it has eigenvalues of both signs.
:::

**The slide's argument (one direction).** The eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix are real (L06). If $A$ is PD, take $x=q$, an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$:
$$
q^\top Aq>0\ \Longrightarrow\ q^\top(\lambda q)>0\ \Longrightarrow\ \lambda\,\|q\|_2^2>0\ \Longrightarrow\ \lambda>0 .
$$
In the same way, PSD gives $\lambda\ge0$, NSD gives $\lambda\le0$ and ND gives $\lambda<0$.

:::derive the other direction: why positive eigenvalues force $x^\top Ax>0$ for **every** $x$
Write the symmetric matrix as $A=Q\Lambda Q^\top$ (L06) and describe $x$ in the eigenvector basis, $c=Q^\top x$ (so $x=Qc$). Then
$$
x^\top Ax=x^\top Q\Lambda Q^\top x=(Q^\top x)^\top\Lambda\,(Q^\top x)=c^\top\Lambda c
=\begin{bmatrix}c_1&\cdots&c_n\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1&&\\&\ddots&\\&&\lambda_n\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}c_1\\\vdots\\c_n\end{bmatrix}
=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i\,c_i^2 .
$$
**A sum of squares, each weighted by an eigenvalue.** If every $\lambda_i>0$ and $x\neq\mathbf 0$ (so $c\neq\mathbf 0$, because $Q$ is invertible), the sum is positive. If one $\lambda_i$ is negative, choose $c=e_i$ (that is, $x=q_i$) to make the sum negative. So the signs of the eigenvalues decide everything.
:::

:::example The three matrices of L06 (revision notes)
1. $S=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ has $\lambda=3,1$, both positive: **PD**. Directly, $x^\top Sx=2x_1^2+2x_1x_2+2x_2^2=x_1^2+x_2^2+(x_1+x_2)^2>0$ for $x\neq\mathbf 0$.
2. $F=\tfrac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$ has $\lambda=1,0$: **PSD but not PD**. Indeed $x^\top Fx=\tfrac12(x_1+x_2)^2\ge0$, but it equals $0$ at $x=(1,-1)$, the eigenvector of $\lambda=0$.
3. $M=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\2&0\end{bmatrix}$ has $\lambda=4,-1$: **indefinite**. For $x=(1,0)$, $x^\top Mx=3>0$; for $x=(1,-2)$, $Mx=(-1,2)$ and $x^\top Mx=-1-4=-5<0$.
4. *Check yourself:* is $\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}$ PD? Its trace is $2$ and its determinant $1-4=-3<0$, so the eigenvalues have opposite signs ($3$ and $-1$): **indefinite**, not PD.
:::

:::warn A positive determinant is not enough
$\det A>0$ only says the **product** of the eigenvalues is positive; two negative eigenvalues also give a positive product. $-I$ has $\det(-I)=1>0$ but is negative definite. (2024 Mid MCQ 22: "$\det A>0$" does **not** guarantee PSD; "all eigenvalues $\ge0$", "$x^\top Ax\ge0$ for all $x$" and "$A=B^\top B$" all do.)

:::origc [2024 Mid Part I · MCQ 22 ↗](pdfs/past/Mid-2024-Part1.pdf#page=4)
- **2024 MCQ 22.** For symmetric $A$, which conditions guarantee that $A$ is PSD? (a) all eigenvalues non-negative (b) $x^\top Ax\ge0$ for all $x$ (c) $A=B^\top B$ for some $B$ (d) $\det A>0$
:::
:::

**The level-curve picture (revision notes, Figure 3).** The curves $x^\top Ax=c$ are **closed ellipses** for a PD matrix (axes along the eigenvectors, half-lengths $\sqrt{c/\lambda_i}$), **parallel lines** for a PSD matrix with a zero eigenvalue (the ellipse stretched to infinity), and **hyperbolas** — a saddle — for an indefinite one.

:::remember
PSD means $x^\top Ax\ge0$ for every $x$ — $Ax$ never turns more than $90^\circ$ from $x$ — and for a symmetric matrix that is the same as all eigenvalues $\ge0$ (PD: all $>0$).
:::

## 2. Gram matrices are always PSD

:::background why $x^\top(A^\top A)x=\|Ax\|^2$
Group the product differently and use $(Ax)^\top=x^\top A^\top$ (L02):
$$
x^\top(A^\top A)x=(x^\top A^\top)(Ax)=(Ax)^\top(Ax)=\|Ax\|_2^2 .
$$
A squared length is never negative. This is the same step that proved the Gram matrix invertible in L02 (Tutorial 1).

:::origc [Tutorial 1 · Question 1 ↗](pdfs/psets/Tutorial-01.pdf#page=1)
Let $A$ have linearly independent columns and $G=A^\top A$. Show that $G$ is invertible, i.e. that $Gx=\mathbf 0$ forces $x=\mathbf 0$: left-multiply $A^\top Ax=\mathbf 0$ by $x^\top$, write $x^\top A^\top Ax$ as $(Ax)^\top(Ax)=\|Ax\|^2$, conclude $Ax=\mathbf 0$, and use the independence of the columns. *(Full question and solution in the L02 notes.)*
:::
:::

Take **any** $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, square or not. We cannot ask for its eigenvalues, but we can form two square, symmetric matrices, $A^\top A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ and $AA^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$.

:::thm $A^\top A$ and $AA^\top$ are symmetric PSD
For every $A$,
$$
x^\top(A^\top A)x=\|Ax\|^2\ge0\qquad\text{and}\qquad y^\top(AA^\top)y=\|A^\top y\|^2\ge0,
$$
so both are PSD, their eigenvalues are real and $\ge0$, and their square roots exist. Moreover, $A^\top A$ is **PD exactly when the columns of $A$ are independent** (then $Ax=\mathbf 0$ only for $x=\mathbf 0$).
:::

**Meaning.** The quadratic form of $A^\top A$ is the **squared length of the image**: $A^\top A$ knows how much $A$ stretches each vector. That is the key to the SVD.

For the filter,
$$
A^\top A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&1\\-0.8&1&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&-0.8\\0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&-0.8\\-0.8&1.64\end{bmatrix}
$$
is PD (the two columns of $A$ are independent), while $AA^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times3}$ is only PSD: the sheet is flat, so one direction of $\mathbb{R}^3$ is never reached.

*Check yourself.* If $A\in\mathbb{R}^{5\times3}$ has rank 3, then $A^\top A$ ($3\times3$, rank 3) is PD, and $AA^\top$ ($5\times5$, rank 3) has $5-3=2$ zero eigenvalues.

:::remember
$A^\top A$ and $AA^\top$ are symmetric PSD for every matrix, because $x^\top A^\top Ax=\|Ax\|^2\ge0$; their eigenvalues are the squared stretch factors of $A$.
:::

## 3. The singular value decomposition

:::figs
l07-p10|Formulation and the shapes of Σ
:::

**Overview (slide p9).** The SVD is a central matrix decomposition. Strang (1993) called it the "fundamental theorem of linear algebra", because it **applies to all matrices, not only square ones, and it always exists**. For $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, the map $x\in\mathbb{R}^n\mapsto Ax\in\mathbb{R}^m$ is a linear transformation, and the SVD explains its underlying geometry.

:::thm Singular value decomposition
Every $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ can be written
$$
A=U\,\Sigma\,V^\top,
$$
where
- $U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ is **orthonormal** (orthogonal): $U^\top U=I_m$;
- $V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ is **orthonormal**: $V^\top V=I_n$;
- $\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is a (special) **diagonal** matrix whose diagonal entries are the **singular values**
$$
\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_{\min(m,n)}\ge0 .
$$
The columns $u_i$ of $U$ are the **left singular vectors** (in the output space $\mathbb{R}^m$); the columns $v_i$ of $V$ are the **right singular vectors** (in the input space $\mathbb{R}^n$).
:::

**The shape of $\Sigma$** is the shape of $A$, so there are $\min(m,n)$ singular values:
$$
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1&&&0&\cdots&0\\&\ddots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\&&\sigma_m&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}}_{m<n:\ \text{extra zero columns}}
\qquad
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1&&\\&\ddots&\\&&\sigma_n\end{bmatrix}}_{m=n}
\qquad
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1&&\\&\ddots&\\&&\sigma_n\\0&\cdots&0\\\vdots&&\vdots\\0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}}_{m>n:\ \text{extra zero rows}}
$$

For the filter ($m=3$, $n=2$):
$$
\underbrace{A}_{3\times2}=\underbrace{U}_{3\times3}\,\underbrace{\Sigma}_{3\times2}\,\underbrace{V^\top}_{2\times2}.
$$
Always track the sizes: $V^\top$ acts on inputs in $\mathbb{R}^n$, $\Sigma$ converts $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$, and $U$ acts on outputs in $\mathbb{R}^m$.

### The one equation to remember

Multiply $A=U\Sigma V^\top$ on the right by $V$ (and use $V^\top V=I$): $AV=U\Sigma$. Reading this column by column,
$$
\boxed{Av_i=\sigma_iu_i}\qquad i=1,\dots,\min(m,n).
$$
$A$ sends each right singular vector $v_i$ to the left singular vector $u_i$, **stretched by $\sigma_i$**. Along the $v_i$, $A$ does nothing but stretch (and move into the output space).

:::derive why $AV=U\Sigma$ reads column by column as $Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$
Column $i$ of $AV$ is $A$ times column $i$ of $V$, i.e. $Av_i$ (L02: $AB=[Ab_1\ \cdots\ Ab_p]$). Column $i$ of $U\Sigma$ is $U$ times column $i$ of $\Sigma$, which is $\sigma_ie_i$ (one non-zero entry), so it equals $\sigma_iUe_i=\sigma_iu_i$. Equating column $i$ of both sides gives $Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$.
:::

**Outer-product (compact) form.** Multiplying out $U\Sigma V^\top$ one column at a time gives a sum of rank-one matrices (L02):
$$
A=\sum_{i=1}^{\min(m,n)}\sigma_i\,u_iv_i^\top .
$$
If only $r$ singular values are non-zero, the remaining columns of $U$ and $V$ multiply zeros and can be dropped: $A=U_r\Sigma_rV_r^\top$ with shapes $(m\times r)(r\times r)(r\times n)$.

*Check yourself.* For $A\in\mathbb{R}^{5\times3}$: $U$ is $5\times5$, $\Sigma$ is $5\times3$, $V^\top$ is $3\times3$; there are 3 singular values and the last $5-3=2$ rows of $\Sigma$ are zero.

:::remember
Every matrix factors as $A=U\Sigma V^\top$ — orthogonal $U$ ($m\times m$), diagonal $\Sigma$ ($m\times n$, entries $\sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge0$), orthogonal $V$ ($n\times n$) — and column by column this says $Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$.
:::

## 4. Computing the SVD

:::figs
l07-p12|Left singular vectors from AAᵀ
l07-p13|Right singular vectors from AᵀA
:::

:::background two facts used below: $Q^\top Q=I$ and "the eigen-decomposition of a symmetric matrix"
- **Orthogonal matrices cancel.** If $V$ is orthogonal, $V^\top V=I$, so in a product like $V\Sigma^\top U^\top U\Sigma V^\top$ the middle $U^\top U$ simply disappears.
- **Spectral theorem (L06).** A symmetric matrix $G$ can be written $G=Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with orthonormal eigenvectors in $Q$ and eigenvalues on the diagonal of $\Lambda$. So **whenever you manage to write a symmetric matrix as (orthogonal)(diagonal)(same orthogonal)$^\top$, you have found its eigenvectors and eigenvalues.**
:::

**Left singular vectors (slide p12).** Define $G=AA^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$. Substituting $A=U\Sigma V^\top$,
$$
G=AA^\top=U\Sigma V^\top\,(U\Sigma V^\top)^\top=U\Sigma V^\top V\Sigma^\top U^\top=U\,(\Sigma\Sigma^\top)\,U^\top .
$$
$G$ is symmetric by definition and PSD (§2). $\Sigma\Sigma^\top$ is an $m\times m$ diagonal matrix. So this **is** an eigen-decomposition of $G$: the columns of $U$ are eigenvectors of $AA^\top$, and its eigenvalues are $\lambda=\sigma^2$. Since $G$ is PSD, $\lambda\ge0$. *The eigen-decomposition of $AA^\top$ gives the $m$ left singular vectors of $A$ and the first $m$ singular values.*

**Right singular vectors (slide p13).** Define $G=A^\top A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$:
$$
G=A^\top A=V\Sigma^\top U^\top U\Sigma V^\top=V\,(\Sigma^\top\Sigma)\,V^\top .
$$
$\Sigma^\top\Sigma$ is an $n\times n$ diagonal matrix, so the columns of $V$ are eigenvectors of $A^\top A$ with eigenvalues $\lambda=\sigma^2\ge0$. *The eigen-decomposition of $A^\top A$ gives the $n$ right singular vectors of $A$ and the first $n$ singular values.*

:::thm Singular values from eigenvalues
$$
\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i(A^\top A)}=\sqrt{\lambda_i(AA^\top)}\ \ (\text{for the non-zero ones}),
$$
and they are **never negative**, because $A^\top A$ is PSD — "now you can explain the non-negativity of the singular values". $A^\top A$ and $AA^\top$ have the **same non-zero eigenvalues** (if $A^\top Av=\lambda v$ then $AA^\top(Av)=\lambda(Av)$); the larger one just has extra zeros.
:::

**SVD summary (slide p14).** EVD of $AA^\top$ gives $U$ and the first $m$ singular values; EVD of $A^\top A$ gives $V$ and the first $n$ singular values; $U$ and $V$ are always orthogonal; the SVD always exists; the singular values are non-negative.

:::derive why $\sigma_1=\max_{\|x\|=1}\|Ax\|$ — the singular values are the stretch factors
Write a unit vector in the eigenvector basis of $A^\top A$: $x=\sum_ic_iv_i$ with $\sum_ic_i^2=1$. Then
$$
\|Ax\|^2=x^\top A^\top Ax=\sum_i\lambda_ic_i^2 ,
$$
a **weighted average** of the eigenvalues (the weights $c_i^2$ are non-negative and sum to 1). An average cannot exceed the largest value, so $\|Ax\|^2\le\lambda_1$, with equality at $x=v_1$. Hence the largest stretch of a unit vector is $\sqrt{\lambda_1}=\sigma_1$, achieved along $v_1$; the best direction perpendicular to $v_1$ is $v_2$, with stretch $\sigma_2$, and so on.
:::

### The recipe — and the sign trap

:::def Computing an SVD by hand
1. Form $A^\top A$ and find its eigenvalues $\lambda_1\ge\lambda_2\ge\cdots\ge0$. Then $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}$.
2. Find **unit** eigenvectors $v_i$ of $A^\top A$: these are the columns of $V$.
3. For each $\sigma_i>0$, set
$$
u_i=\frac{Av_i}{\sigma_i}.
$$
4. If $m>r$ (more output dimensions than non-zero singular values), complete $U$ with unit vectors perpendicular to $u_1,\dots,u_r$; they solve $A^\top w=\mathbf 0$.

**Checks:** $\sum_i\sigma_i^2=\|A\|_F^2$ (the sum of the squares of all entries); for square $A$, $\sigma_1\cdots\sigma_n=|\det A|$.
:::

:::warn Do not take $U$ from a separate eigen-decomposition of $AA^\top$
Each eigenvector is defined only up to sign, and only **one** pairing of $u_i$ with $v_i$ satisfies $Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$. If you compute $U$ and $V$ independently, a sign can come out wrong and $U\Sigma V^\top\ne A$. Always get $V$ from $A^\top A$ and then **define** $u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i$. (These $u_i$ are automatically perpendicular: $u_i^\top u_j=\dfrac{v_i^\top A^\top Av_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}=\dfrac{\lambda_j\,v_i^\top v_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}=0$ for $i\neq j$.)
:::

:::example $B=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix}$ by hand (revision notes, Example 3)
**Step 1 — the matrix that measures stretching.**
$$
B^\top B=\begin{bmatrix}3&4\\0&5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}25&20\\20&25\end{bmatrix}.
$$
**Step 2 — stretch factors.** $\det(B^\top B-\lambda I)=(25-\lambda)^2-400=(\lambda-45)(\lambda-5)$, so
$$
\sigma_1=\sqrt{45}=3\sqrt5\approx6.71,\qquad \sigma_2=\sqrt5\approx2.24 .
$$
Check: $\sigma_1\sigma_2=15=|\det B|$, and $\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2=50=9+0+16+25=\|B\|_F^2$.

**Step 3 — stretched directions.** For $\lambda=45$, $(B^\top B-45I)v=\begin{bmatrix}-20&20\\20&-20\end{bmatrix}v=\mathbf 0$ gives equal entries; for $\lambda=5$ the entries are opposite:
$$
v_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad v_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}.
$$
**Step 4 — where they land.**
$$
u_1=\frac{Bv_1}{\sigma_1}=\frac{1}{3\sqrt5}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}3\\9\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\3\end{bmatrix},\qquad
u_2=\frac{Bv_2}{\sigma_2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}-3\\1\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}-3\\1\end{bmatrix}.
$$
So
$$
B=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-3\\3&1\end{bmatrix}}_{U}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}3\sqrt5&0\\0&\sqrt5\end{bmatrix}}_{\Sigma}\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}}_{V^\top}.
$$
**The sign trap.** $BB^\top$ also has $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(3,-1)$ as an eigenvector for $\sigma_2$; using it instead of $u_2$ gives $\begin{bmatrix}0&3\\5&4\end{bmatrix}\neq B$. Note also that the eigenvalues of $B$ itself are $3$ and $5$, with non-perpendicular eigenvectors: **the SVD is not the eigen-decomposition.**
:::

:::example The filter $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&-0.8\\0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$ (slide p17, revision notes Example 4)
**Step 1.** $A^\top A=\begin{bmatrix}2&-0.8\\-0.8&1.64\end{bmatrix}$, with trace $3.64$ and determinant $2(1.64)-0.64=2.64$.

**Step 2.** $\lambda^2-3.64\lambda+2.64=(\lambda-2.64)(\lambda-1)$, so $\sigma_1=\sqrt{2.64}\approx1.62$ and $\sigma_2=1$.

**Step 3.** Unit eigenvectors: $v_1\approx(-0.78,\ 0.62)$ and $v_2\approx(-0.62,\ -0.78)$ (perpendicular).

**Step 4.** $u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i$ gives $u_1\approx(-0.79,\ 0.38,\ -0.48)$ and $u_2\approx(0,\ -0.78,\ -0.62)$. Since $m=3>r=2$, one more column is needed, a unit vector perpendicular to both; it solves $A^\top w=\mathbf 0$, giving $u_3=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2.64}}(-1,-0.8,1)\approx(-0.62,-0.49,0.62)$ — the **normal** of the sheet. So
$$
A=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}-0.79&0&-0.62\\0.38&-0.78&-0.49\\-0.48&-0.62&0.62\end{bmatrix}}_{U\ (3\times3)}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}1.62&0\\0&1.0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}}_{\Sigma\ (3\times2)}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}-0.78&0.62\\-0.62&-0.78\end{bmatrix}}_{V^\top\ (2\times2)},
$$
the decomposition on the slide. Check: $Av_1\approx(-1.28,\ 0.62,\ -0.78)=1.62\,u_1$.
:::

**P1.** A diagonal matrix with non-negative entries is already its own SVD: $U=I_2$, $\Sigma=A$, $V=I_2$. The singular values are $3$ and $1$; $e_1=(1,0)$ is stretched by 3 and $e_2$ by 1.

**P2.**
$$
A^\top A=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix},
$$
with eigenvalues $2$ and $0$ (trace 2, determinant 0) and unit eigenvectors $v_1=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$, $v_2=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$. The singular values are $\sqrt2$ and $0$. The two columns are equal and non-zero, so $\operatorname{rank}A=1$ — exactly one non-zero singular value.

**P3.** (a)
$$
U\Sigma V^\top=\begin{bmatrix}3/5&-4/5\\4/5&3/5\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\4&0\end{bmatrix}=A .
$$
(b) $Ax=(3x_1,\,4x_1)$, so $\|Ax\|=\sqrt{9x_1^2+16x_1^2}=5|x_1|$, largest ($=5=\sigma_1$) at $x=\pm(1,0)=\pm v_1$. (c) Every point is sent onto the line spanned by $(3,4)$: the direction $e_1$ is stretched by 5 and the perpendicular direction $e_2$ is collapsed to zero.
:::

:::example Two more "check yourself" cases (revision notes)
- **A negative diagonal entry.** $D=\operatorname{diag}(3,-2)$ has singular values $3$ and $2$ — they cannot be negative. The minus sign moves into $U$: $D=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\,\operatorname{diag}(3,2)\,I$. A flip is a rotation-like step, so it belongs in $U$, not in the stretch.
- **Symmetric matrices.** If $A=A^\top$, then $\sigma_i=|\lambda_i|$ and $U$, $V$ agree up to signs. For $M=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\2&0\end{bmatrix}$ ($\lambda=4,-1$) the singular values are $4$ and $1$.
:::

:::remember
Compute an SVD as: eigenvalues of $A^\top A$ give $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}$, its unit eigenvectors give $V$, and $u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i$ gives $U$ — never take $U$ and $V$ from two separate eigen-decompositions.
:::

## 5. Geometric interpretation: rotate, stretch, rotate

:::figs
l07-p15|Vᵀ (change of basis in ℝⁿ), Σ (scaling), U (change of basis in ℝᵐ)
l07-p16|The unit circle becomes an ellipse with semi-axes σᵢuᵢ
l07-p17|The filter: change of basis in ℝ², append a basis vector, change of basis in ℝ³
:::

Read $Ax=U\Sigma V^\top x$ from right to left (slide p15):

1. **$V^\top$ — transformation (change of basis) in $\mathbb{R}^n$.** It re-describes $x$ using the perpendicular directions $v_1,\dots,v_n$ as axes; for a picture, it rotates (or reflects) the input. Lengths and angles do not change.
2. **$\Sigma$ — scaling in the new basis.** It stretches the $i$-th axis by $\sigma_i$, and changes the dimension from $n$ to $m$:
   - if $m<n$, **drop** the last $n-m$ coordinates (the zero columns of $\Sigma$);
   - if $m>n$, **append** $m-n$ zero coordinates (the zero rows of $\Sigma$).
3. **$U$ — transformation (change of basis) in $\mathbb{R}^m$.** It rotates the result onto the output directions $u_1,\dots,u_m$.

:::thm Circles become ellipses
The image of the unit circle (ball) under $A$ is an ellipse (ellipsoid) whose **semi-axes are $\sigma_1u_1,\ \sigma_2u_2,\ \dots,\ \sigma_ru_r$**: the lengths of the axes are the singular values, their directions are the left singular vectors, and $\sigma_1$ is the largest stretch $A$ can produce.
:::

**Why.** A unit vector $x=\sum_ic_iv_i$ (with $\sum_ic_i^2=1$) is sent to $Ax=\sum_i\sigma_ic_iu_i$. The $u_i$ are perpendicular, so the coordinate along $u_i$ is $\sigma_ic_i$, which reaches at most $\sigma_i$ (when $x=v_i$).

**For $B$** (§4): $V^\top$ is a rotation by $-45^\circ$, $\Sigma$ stretches the axes by $3\sqrt5\approx6.71$ and $\sqrt5\approx2.24$, and $U$ is a rotation by $+71.6^\circ$. The ellipse has area $\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2=15\pi$, so $B$ multiplies areas by $15=|\det B|$.

**For the filter** (slide p17): the unit circle on the canvas becomes an ellipse with semi-axes $1.62\,u_1$ and $1.0\,u_2$, both lying in the sheet $y_3=y_1+0.8y_2$. The third direction $u_3$ is perpendicular to the sheet and never reached — this is the extra zero row of $\Sigma$ ("append 1 more basis"). After stretching, the picture sits in a plane inside $\mathbb{R}^3$, and $U$ rotates that plane onto the sheet.

### What the matrix reaches, and what it destroys

If $r$ singular values are non-zero:

- $u_1,\dots,u_r$ span the **column space** $\mathcal C(A)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^m$ (dimension $r$): all outputs that can occur;
- $v_{r+1},\dots,v_n$ span the **null space** $\mathcal N(A)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ (dimension $n-r$): a zero singular value squashes that direction to $\mathbf 0$;
- $u_{r+1},\dots,u_m$ are never reached; they span $\mathcal N(A^\top)$.

:::example A matrix that destroys a direction
$R=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&4\\2&4\end{bmatrix}$ (second column $=2\times$ first) has $\sigma_1=3\sqrt5$ and $\sigma_2=0$. Its "ellipse" has zero width: the unit circle is **flattened onto a segment** along $u_1$, from $-\sigma_1u_1$ to $\sigma_1u_1$, of length $2\sigma_1=6\sqrt5\approx13.4$. The direction $v_2=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}(-2,1)$ is lost for good — no matrix can undo the flattening.

*Check:* $R^\top R=\begin{bmatrix}9&18\\18&36\end{bmatrix}$ has trace $45$ and determinant $0$, so $\lambda=45,0$ and $\sigma=3\sqrt5,0$.
:::

:::remember
Every linear map rotates ($V^\top$), stretches along perpendicular axes ($\Sigma$, dropping or appending coordinates), and rotates again ($U$): the unit circle becomes an ellipse with semi-axes $\sigma_iu_i$.
:::

## 6. Rank and rank estimation

:::figs
l07-p18|Rank = number of non-zero singular values; rank estimation
:::

:::thm Rank from singular values
The rank of a matrix equals the **number of non-zero singular values**.
:::

**The slide's reason.** $A^\top A$ and $A$ have the same rank (L03: $\mathcal N(A^\top A)=\mathcal N(A)$), and the rank of a symmetric square matrix equals its number of non-zero eigenvalues; those eigenvalues are the $\sigma_i^2$.

:::derive a second proof, straight from $A=U\Sigma V^\top$
$U$ and $V^\top$ are invertible (rotations lose no information), and multiplying by an invertible matrix does not change the rank. So $\operatorname{rank}A=\operatorname{rank}\Sigma$, and the rank of a diagonal matrix is the number of its non-zero diagonal entries.

*Caution (revision notes):* "rank = number of non-zero eigenvalues" is true for **symmetric** matrices such as $A^\top A$, but not for every square matrix: $\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ has rank 1 and both eigenvalues 0. Its singular values are $1$ and $0$ — one non-zero, matching the rank.
:::

### Application: estimating the rank of noisy data (slide p18)

Suppose we have $n$ data points $a_1,\dots,a_n\in\mathbb{R}^m$ with $n\gg m$, stacked as the columns of $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$. If the points satisfy some linear relations — they all lie in an $r$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$ — then $A$ has rank $r$. But measured data carry errors, so $A$ will probably have **full rank** $m$. Still, only $r$ of the singular values will be **large**; the others will be close to zero (as small as the noise). Using the SVD, we estimate an **approximate rank** by counting the singular values that are much larger than the others.

:::example Eight noisy points near a plane (revision notes, Example 5)
Eight points in $\mathbb{R}^3$ lie on the plane $y_3=y_1+y_2$ (true rank 2), but each entry carries noise of size about $0.05$. The singular values of the $3\times8$ data matrix are
$$
\sigma_1\approx5.83,\qquad \sigma_2\approx2.20,\qquad \sigma_3\approx0.15 .
$$
Two large and one tiny: **approximate rank 2**, without being told about the plane. The direction $u_3\approx(-0.60,-0.54,0.59)$ is almost the normal $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(-1,-1,1)$ of the true plane — the direction in which the data barely vary. There is no universal cutoff: look for a clear **gap** ($2.20\to0.15$), since with noise no singular value is exactly zero.

*Check yourself:* a $4\times100$ data matrix with singular values $10,\ 9.5,\ 0.01,\ 0.008$ has approximate rank **2**.
:::

:::remember
rank $A$ = the number of non-zero singular values; with noisy data, count the singular values above the clear gap to get the approximate rank.
:::

## 7. Matrix approximation

:::figs
l07-p19|The k-rank approximation
:::

**A matrix as a sum of layers (slide p19).** Using the SVD,
$$
A=U\Sigma V^\top=\sum_{i=1}^{\min(m,n)}u_i\,\sigma_i\,v_i^\top .
$$
If $\operatorname{rank}A=r\le\min(m,n)$, the summation can be truncated at $r$ with no loss: $A=\sum_{i=1}^ru_i\sigma_iv_i^\top$. Each layer $u_iv_i^\top$ is an $m\times n$ matrix of rank one (a column times a row), with strength $\sigma_i$; the first layer is the strongest and each later layer adds a finer correction — like a photograph built up from a coarse outline.

:::def The k-rank approximation
Keeping only the first $k$ singular vectors and singular values,
$$
A\approx A_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_i\,\sigma_i\,v_i^\top,\qquad \operatorname{rank}A_k=k .
$$
:::

**How good is it?** The error $A-A_k=\sum_{i>k}\sigma_iu_iv_i^\top$ is the sum of the discarded layers, so:

- the largest stretch of the error matrix is $\sigma_{k+1}$;
- the total squared error is
$$
\|A-A_k\|_F^2=\sigma_{k+1}^2+\sigma_{k+2}^2+\cdots+\sigma_r^2 ;
$$
- the fraction of the "energy" kept is $\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i^2}{\sum_i\sigma_i^2}$.

By the Eckart–Young theorem (used without proof), **no other rank-$k$ matrix does better.** (2025 Mid MCQ 22: $A_k$ minimises $\|A-A_k\|_F$ and $\|A-A_k\|_F^2=\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2$ — both true; keeping more components never *increases* the error.)

:::origc [2025 Mid Part I · MCQ 22 ↗](pdfs/past/Mid-2025-Part1.pdf#page=4)
- **2025 MCQ 22.** The best rank-$k$ approximation of $A$ is given by (Eckart–Young): (a) $A_k=\sum_{i=1}^k\sigma_iu_iv_i^\top$ minimises $\|A-A_k\|_F$ (b) $\|A-A_k\|_F^2=\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2$ (c) using the smaller singular directions preserves the matrix information in the approximation (d) keeping more components always increases the reconstruction error
:::

:::derive why $\|A-A_k\|_F^2=\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2$
$A-A_k=U\,\Sigma_{\text{rest}}\,V^\top$, where $\Sigma_{\text{rest}}$ is $\Sigma$ with the first $k$ diagonal entries set to zero. Multiplying by orthogonal matrices does not change the Frobenius norm (it is a length, and rotations preserve lengths), so $\|A-A_k\|_F=\|\Sigma_{\text{rest}}\|_F=\sqrt{\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2}$. With $k=0$ this also gives $\|A\|_F^2=\sum_i\sigma_i^2$.
:::

**Geometry.** Dropping the layer $\sigma_2u_2v_2^\top$ removes the short axis of the ellipse: $A_1$ sends the circle to the **segment** along the long axis. What is lost is the half-width $\sigma_2$; the thinner the ellipse already is, the better a line approximates it.

:::example $B$ and the filter (revision notes, Examples 6–7)
- **$B$.** $B_1=\sigma_1u_1v_1^\top=3\sqrt5\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\3\end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\end{bmatrix}=\frac32\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\3&3\end{bmatrix}$ and $B-B_1=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}3&-3\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}$. The total squared error is $2.25+2.25+0.25+0.25=5=\sigma_2^2$, and the energy kept is $45/50=90\%$: one layer captures 90% of $B$.
- **The filter.** $A_1\approx\begin{bmatrix}1&-0.8\\-0.49&0.39\\0.61&-0.49\end{bmatrix}$; the largest error stretch is $\sigma_2=1$ and the energy kept is $2.64/3.64\approx72.5\%$. Here $\sigma_2$ is not small, so one layer is a poor approximation.
- *Check yourself:* singular values $4,3,0,0$: $A_1$ keeps $16/25=64\%$; $A_2=A$ exactly, since $r=2$.
:::

:::example Problem Set 7, Problem 4 — singular values $10,\ 2,\ 0.5$
:::orig [PSet 7 · Problem 4 ↗](pdfs/psets/PSet-07.pdf#page=2)
A data matrix has singular values $10$, $2$ and $0.5$. (a) What is its rank? (b) If we keep only the largest singular value, what rank approximation do we obtain, and what is the Frobenius norm of the discarded part? (c) What fraction of the squared Frobenius norm does this rank-one approximation retain?
:::
(a) All three are non-zero, so the rank is **3**.
(b) Keeping $\sigma_1=10$ gives a **rank-one** approximation; the discarded part has
$$
\|A-A_1\|_F=\sqrt{2^2+0.5^2}=\sqrt{4.25}=\frac{\sqrt{17}}{2}\approx2.06 .
$$
(c) Retained fraction $=\dfrac{10^2}{10^2+2^2+0.5^2}=\dfrac{100}{104.25}=\dfrac{400}{417}\approx0.959$, about **95.9%**.
:::

### Why it matters: compression

An $m\times n$ matrix stores $mn$ numbers. $A_k$ needs only $k$ vectors $u_i\in\mathbb{R}^m$, $k$ numbers $\sigma_i$ and $k$ vectors $v_i\in\mathbb{R}^n$:
$$
\text{storage}(A_k)=mk+k+kn=k(m+n+1).
$$
Images are approximately low rank — their singular values fall off quickly — so a small $k$ keeps almost all of the energy. Choosing $k$ is the same decision as estimating the approximate rank: look for where the singular values drop.

:::example Problem Set 7, Problem 9 — image compression
:::orig [PSet 7 · Problem 9 ↗](pdfs/psets/PSet-07.pdf#page=4)
An image is a matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ of pixel intensities with rank-$k$ SVD approximation $A_k=U_k\Sigma_kV_k^\top$. (a) How many real numbers are needed to store $A$ directly, and to store the three factors of $A_k$? (b) Compare these for a $1000\times800$ image with $k=20$. (c) Explain why increasing $k$ improves fidelity but reduces compression.
:::
(a) Directly: $mn$. Factors: $mk+k+kn=k(m+n+1)$.
(b) $1000\times800=800{,}000$ values directly; with $k=20$, $20(1000+800+1)=36{,}020$ values, about **4.5%**.
(c) Adding components can only reduce (or keep) the best rank-$k$ error, so more detail is preserved; but each extra component costs $m+n+1$ more numbers, so the saving shrinks.

The same count in the mids: 2025 Mid P1 ($2048\times1536$, $k=80$: $286{,}800$ instead of $3{,}145{,}728$, a 90.88% saving) and 2024 Mid P1 ($1920\times1080$, $k=120$: $360{,}120$ instead of $2{,}073{,}600$).

**In NumPy** (revision notes): `U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A)` returns $U$ ($3\times3$), the singular values `s` and $V^\top$; the rank-1 approximation is `s[0] * np.outer(U[:, 0], Vt[0])`. NumPy may flip the signs of a pair $u_i,v_i$; the product is unchanged.

## 8. SVD versus eigen-decomposition

| | Eigen-decomposition $A=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}$ | SVD $A=U\Sigma V^\top$ |
|---|---|---|
| Which matrices | square only (and not always: rotations, $\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$) | **every** real matrix, any shape |
| The factors | $Q$ may be non-orthogonal; $\Lambda$ may be complex or negative | $U$, $V$ always real orthogonal; $\sigma_i\ge0$ |
| Directions | one set ($Aq=\lambda q$, same space) | two sets ($Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$, input → output) |
| Symmetric $A$ | $A=Q\Lambda Q^\top$ | agrees up to signs: $\sigma_i=\lvert\lambda_i\rvert$ |

| Idea | Key fact | In the examples |
|---|---|---|
| Definite matrices | symmetric: sign of $x^\top Ax$ follows the signs of the eigenvalues; $x^\top A^\top Ax=\|Ax\|^2$ so $A^\top A$, $AA^\top$ are PSD | $S$ PD, $F$ PSD, $M$ indefinite |
| SVD | $A=U\Sigma V^\top$, $Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$ | shapes $3\times3$, $3\times2$, $2\times2$ for the filter |
| Geometry | rotate, stretch, rotate: circle → ellipse with semi-axes $\sigma_iu_i$ | rank-1 $R$: circle → segment |
| Computing | $v_i$ eigenvectors of $A^\top A$, $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i}$, $u_i=Av_i/\sigma_i$ | $\sigma=1.62,1$ (filter); $3\sqrt5,\sqrt5$ ($B$) |
| Rank | rank = number of $\sigma_i>0$; approximate rank from the gap | $5.83,2.20,0.15\Rightarrow$ rank 2 |
| Approximation | $A_k=\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_iu_iv_i^\top$; error from the discarded $\sigma_i$; storage $k(m+n+1)$ | $B_1$ keeps 90% |
