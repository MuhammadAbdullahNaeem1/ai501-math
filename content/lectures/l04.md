## 1. The supervised learning setup

:::figs
l04-p03|Oxygen-saturation example
:::

:::def Training data
$$
D=\{(x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2),\dots,(x_n,y_n)\}\subseteq\mathcal X^d\times\mathcal Y
$$
$x_i\in\mathcal X^d$: feature vector of example $i$ in a $d$-dimensional **feature space**. $y_i\in\mathcal Y$: its **label**, from the **label space**.
:::

| Problem | Label space $\mathcal Y$ |
|---|---|
| **Regression** | $\mathcal Y=\mathbb{R}$ — prediction on a continuous scale |
| **Binary classification** | $\{0,1\}$ or $\{-1,1\}$ or $\{1,2\}$ |
| **$M$-class classification** | $\{1,2,\dots,M\}$ |

**Slide example**: data of 200 patients (age, cholesterol, glucose, BMI, height, heart rate, calorie intake, number of steps — $d=8$, $n=200$) → model $h$ → prediction of oxygen saturation (regression).

:::example 2026 Quiz 1, Q1 — Regression, classification or neither?
Cumulative percentage score → **regression**. Letter grade → **classification**. *Probability* that electricity prices decrease → **regression** (a number in $[0,1]$). *Whether* prices decrease → **classification**. Number of vehicles in the next hour from camera footage → **regression** (a count). Grouping students into cohorts with **no labels recorded** → **neither** (that is unsupervised clustering).
Revision brainteaser: tomorrow's max temperature (regression); no-ball or not (binary); blood group (8-class); runs off the next over (ordered integers — regression usually better, 37 classes also defensible if justified).
:::

**Learning.** We assume the pairs $(x_i,y_i)$ are drawn from some **unknown** distribution $P(X,Y)$ (nature's secret question bank — we only see the questions we drew). Goal: learn the machine (model, function, **hypothesis**) $h$ so that for a new pair $(x,y)\sim P$,
$$
h(x)=y\ \text{with high probability (classification)}\qquad\text{or}\qquad h(x)\approx y\ \text{in some optimal sense (regression)}.
$$

:::def Hypothesis class $\mathcal H$
The set of possible functions / candidate models (linear models, neural networks, decision trees, …). We wish to select $h\in\mathcal H$.
**How?** (1) Define $\mathcal H$ for the problem; (2) evaluate the performance of each candidate and choose the best — which needs a **loss function**.
:::

:::remember
Supervised learning = labelled pairs from an unknown $P(X,Y)$, a chosen family $\mathcal H$, and a loss to pick the best $h\in\mathcal H$; real-valued labels → regression, categories → classification.
:::

## 2. Loss functions

A loss $\mathcal L$ quantifies the error of predicting $y$ using $h$ and $x$. Every loss below is **normalised by $n$** — a loss **per sample** — so datasets of different sizes are comparable. **All are non-negative.**

:::def 0/1 loss
$$
\mathcal L_{0/1}(h)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big(1-\delta_{h(x_i)-y_i}\big),\qquad \delta_k=\begin{cases}1&k=0\\0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}
$$
Each term is 1 for a mistake, 0 if correct, so it **counts the mistakes** made by $h$ on $D$ (divided by $n$). **Not used frequently for training** because it is non-differentiable and non-continuous (flat with jumps: a blindfolded hiker feels no slope).
:::

:::def Squared loss (MSE) and RMSE
$$
\mathcal L_{sq}(h)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big(h(x_i)-y_i\big)^2,\qquad
\mathcal L_{rms}(h)=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big(h(x_i)-y_i\big)^2}.
$$
Loss grows **quadratically** with the error in each sample (one error of 10 costs as much as a hundred errors of 1). Smooth, easy to optimise. RMSE is in the **same units as $y$**.
:::

:::def Absolute loss (MAE)
$$
\mathcal L_{abs}(h)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big|h(x_i)-y_i\big|.
$$
Grows **linearly** with the error; used in regression and **suited for noisy data** (outliers can't dominate).
:::

:::example Revision mini-example
True $y=(97,94,99,90)$, predicted $(96,95,99,84)$, errors $(-1,1,0,-6)$:
MSE $=\frac{1+1+0+36}{4}=9.5$, RMSE $=\sqrt{9.5}\approx3.08$, MAE $=\frac{1+1+0+6}4=2$. Patient 4 alone gives $36/38\approx95\%$ of the squared loss but only $6/8=75\%$ of the absolute loss.
:::

:::thm Best constant predictor (PSet 4, P5)
With $h(x)=c$: the squared loss is minimised by the **mean**, the absolute loss by the **median**.
:::

:::proof
$\frac{d}{dc}\frac1n\sum(y_i-c)^2=-\frac2n\sum(y_i-c)=0\Rightarrow c=\bar y$ (second derivative $2>0$ ⇒ minimum).
For $\sum|y_i-c|$ the slope between data points is $\#\{y_i<c\}-\#\{y_i>c\}$: it decreases while more points lie above $c$ and increases after; the minimum is where half lie on each side — the median.
$y=\{2,3,4,5,100\}$: mean $22.8$ (dragged by the outlier), median $4$ (barely moves). Labels $\{1,2,3,10\}$: mean 4, any $c\in[2,3]$ minimises MAE; change 10 → 1000 and the mean jumps to 251.5 while the median stays. That is exactly why MAE is "suited for noisy data".
:::

:::remember
0/1 counts mistakes (good for reporting, bad for training); MSE punishes big errors quadratically and is smooth; MAE is linear and robust to outliers (mean vs median).
:::

## 3. Learning, overfitting and the train/test split

:::figs
l04-p12|The train–test split
:::

**Learning is an optimisation problem**:
$$
h^\ast=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal H}\mathcal L(h).
$$
(Slides write "min"; we want the minimiser.) This gives low loss on our data — but **how can we ensure $h$ gives low loss on inputs not in $D$?**

:::def The memoriser
$$
h(x)=\begin{cases}y_i,&\exists\,(x_i,y_i)\in D\ \text{with}\ x_i=x,\\0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
$$
0% loss on the training data (fits every point), large error on inputs not in $D$ — the **first glimpse of overfitting** (a student who memorised last year's paper).
:::

**Fix: the train/test split.** Split $D$ into $D_{TR}$ (training, 70/80/90%) and $D_{TE}$ (test, 30/20/10%). Learn on training data only and evaluate on test:
$$
h^\ast=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal H}\frac{1}{|D_{TR}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D_{TR}}\mathcal L(x,y\mid h),\qquad
\epsilon_{TE}=\frac{1}{|D_{TE}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D_{TE}}\mathcal L(x,y\mid h^\ast).
$$

**How to split:** the split should capture the variations in the distribution — **i.i.d. (random) sampling** for ordinary data; for **time series, split with respect to time** (train on the past, test on the future).

:::warn
**You can only use the test dataset once, after deciding on the model using the training data.** Peeking, tweaking and re-testing turns the test set into training data and makes the reported error optimistic. Also compute normalisation statistics on the training set only.
:::

**Validation set.** Often $D$ is split three ways: $D_{TR}$ 80%, $D_{VA}$ 10%, $D_{TE}$ 10%. Validation data evaluate the loss of an $h$ learned on the training data; if the validation loss is high, change the hypothesis/model.

| Set | Used for (Ripley, 1996) | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| **Training** | learning — fitting the **parameters** of the model | textbook exercises |
| **Validation** | tuning the **hyper-parameters** (number of hidden units, polynomial order, $k$) | mock exams |
| **Test** | only to assess the performance of a **fully specified** model | final exam, used once |

:::def $K$-fold cross-validation (outline; used to choose $k$)
Split the training data into $K$ folds (5 or 10). For $j=1,\dots,K$ train on $K-1$ folds and compute the validation loss $\ell_j$ on fold $j$. CV loss $=\frac1K\sum_j\ell_j$. Every example is used for validation exactly once; the test set stays sealed. Better than one noisy validation slice when data are scarce.
:::

:::example Revision brainteaser — which set, legitimate?
Choosing $k=3$ vs $k=7$: validation/CV ✓. Computing network weights by gradient descent: training ✓. Reporting "91% accurate": test ✓ (if opened once). Test accuracy low → add features → re-test on the same test set: ✗ (test used for model selection).
:::

:::remember
Train fits parameters, validation tunes hyper-parameters, test is opened once; zero training error (the memoriser) proves nothing.
:::

## 5. k-Nearest Neighbours (kNN)

:::figs
l04-p18|The idea: label the query like its neighbours
l04-p20|1-NN and k-NN
:::

**Idea**: similar features have similar labels. Two classes, two features: a query point surrounded by red points should be labelled red. Algorithm overview: given a new test point $x$, find the **most similar** points in the training data, and classify $x$ "like" them.

Questions the slides pose: **How do we determine similarity?** (distance metric) **How many neighbours?** ($k$) **How to resolve inconsistencies among neighbours?** (majority vote, tie rules)

**1-NN** — the simplest ML classifier: use the label of the **closest** known point. **Generalisation**: take the $k$ nearest neighbours and assign the most frequent label. Slide pictures: with $k=3$ the label is red, with $k=7$ the same query becomes blue — **the answer can change with $k$**.

:::def Formal definition
$D=\{(x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_n,y_n)\}$, $x_i$ features, $y_i\in\{1,\dots,M\}$ class labels. For a **query point** $x$, the **neighbour set** $S_x\subseteq D$ contains the $k$ points closest to $x$, and
$$
h(x)=\text{majority vote (mode) of the labels in }S_x .
$$
**Regression version** (average, or inverse-distance weighted average):
$$
h(x)=\frac1k\sum_{(x_i,y_i)\in S_x}y_i,\qquad h(x)=\frac{\sum_{S_x}y_i/\operatorname{dist}(x,x_i)}{\sum_{S_x}1/\operatorname{dist}(x,x_i)}.
$$
:::

**Characteristics:**

- **Instance-based (lazy) learning**: builds no model during training; simply **stores the training data**.
- **Flexible**: adapts to unseen data by computing distances when a prediction is needed; can draw very irregular boundaries.
- **No assumptions about the distribution** of the data.
- **Non-parametric**: no parameters are learned.
- **Hyper-parameters**: $k$ (number of neighbours) and the **distance metric**.
- **Complexity (time and storage) of prediction grows with the training-set size.**

### Practical issues (slides p25–27)

- **Use odd $k$ for binary classification** — a 2-class vote can then never tie. (With 3 classes, odd $k$ does **not** prevent ties: $k=3$ can give 1–1–1.)
- **Ties**: use prior information (e.g. the more common class), or fall back to the **1-NN** or **$(k-1)$-NN** classifier.
- **Missing values**: replace with the **average value of the feature** (computed on training data).
- **Categorical variables — Hamming distance**:
$$
\operatorname{dist}(x,x')=\sum_{i=1}^d\big(1-\delta_{x_i-x'_i}\big)=\#\{i:x_i\neq x'_i\}.
$$
$(A+,\text{smoker},\text{male},\text{urban})$ vs $(O+,\text{smoker},\text{female},\text{urban})$ ⇒ distance 2. Coding cities as 1, 2, 3 and using Euclidean distance invents a fake geometry.
- **Numeric distances**: Minkowski $\operatorname{dist}_p(x,x')=\big(\sum_j|x_j-x'_j|^p\big)^{1/p}$ — $p=2$ Euclidean (default), $p=1$ Manhattan, $p\to\infty$ Chebyshev. Different metrics give differently shaped neighbourhoods (diamond / circle / square) and possibly different neighbours.
- **Mismatch in the scale of features**: a distance maps $d$ dimensions to one scalar, so values should be of the same order along each dimension. **Solution: data normalisation** — min–max $x_j\mapsto\frac{x_j-\min_j}{\max_j-\min_j}\in[0,1]$ or z-score $x_j\mapsto\frac{x_j-\mu_j}{\sigma_j}$, statistics from the training set only.

:::example Scaling flips the prediction (2026 Quiz 5, Q2)
Training: $P_1=(20,5000,0)$ Low risk, $P_2=(65,9500,4)$ High risk (age, WBC, visits). Test $T=(25,9500,1)$.
(a) Raw: $d^2(T,P_1)=25+20\,250\,000+1=20\,250\,026$, $d^2(T,P_2)=1600+0+9=1609$ ⇒ 1-NN: **High risk** (WBC dominates).
(b) Min–max (ranges 45, 4500, 4): $P_1=(0,0,0)$, $P_2=(1,1,1)$, $T=(0.11,1,0.25)$. $d^2(T,P_1)=0.0121+1+0.0625=1.0746$, $d^2(T,P_2)=0.7921+0+0.5625=1.3546$ ⇒ **Low risk**.
(c) 1,000 patients, 50-NN instead of 1-NN: **variance decreases** (smoother boundary, averaging over more neighbours), **bias increases** (stronger smoothing assumption, misses fine local structure).
Revision example: age vs income in PKR — raw distance calls a 60-year-old closer to a 25-year-old than a 26-year-old; after z-scoring the 26-year-old is (correctly) nearest.
:::

### Choice of $k$

- $k=1$: **sensitive to noise, high variance**; a single mislabelled point carves out its own island. **Increasing $k$ makes the algorithm less sensitive to noise.**
- $k=n$: every query has the same neighbours (all points) ⇒ always predicts the overall majority class — maximally stable but blind. **Decreasing $k$ enables capturing finer structure of the space.**
- **Idea**: pick $k$ not too large, not too small (data-dependent). **How?** Learn it from the data: split into training and validation; start from $k=1$ and iterate, using (5- or 10-fold) **cross-validation**, computing the validation loss with the training data; choose the $k$ that minimises validation loss. **This is the only learning required for kNN.**
- Bias–variance: small $k$ ⇒ low bias, high variance; large $k$ ⇒ high bias, low variance (2025 Mid MCQ 19; 2024 MCQ 8). A highly non-linear boundary favours a small $k$ (PSet 4 P6 d).
- Training error at $k=1$ is 0 (each point is its own neighbour) — meaningless; only validation error counts.

### Algorithm, time and storage complexity (slide p32)

| Step | Cost |
|---|---|
| 1. distance from the test point to every point in $D$ ($n$ points, $d$-dimensional) | $O(dn)$ |
| 2. find the $k$ closest: $k$-th smallest distance by median-of-medians, then collect points below it | $O(n)+O(n)$ |
| 3. most frequent label in $S_x$ | $O(k)$ |
| **Computational complexity per query** | $O(dn)$ |
| **Space complexity** (store all training data) | $O(dn)$ |

No sorting needed ($O(n\log n)$); a heap gives $O(n\log k)$. For $m$ test points: $O(mnd)$. MNIST ($n=60\,000$, $d=784$): $\approx4.7\times10^7$ multiply-adds per query. Face search with $n=10^6$, $d=128$: $\approx1.3\times10^8$ operations and $\approx512$ MB at 4 bytes/number; doubling data doubles query time, training time stays ≈ 0. **Most models: slow training, fast prediction; kNN: free training, expensive prediction.** The complexity depends on the **training-set size and number of features**, not the validation set (2024 MCQ 7).

:::example kNN by hand (2025 Mid P5, 2025 Quiz 2 Q1, PSet 4 P6)
**Euclidean, $k=3$**: training $(1,2)A,(2,1)A,(3,4)B,(4,3)B,(5,5)B$; test $(2,3)$. Distances $\sqrt2,2,\sqrt2,2,\sqrt{13}$. Nearest three (ties broken by list order): $(1,2)A$, $(3,4)B$, $(2,1)A$ ⇒ **A**.
**Manhattan, $k=3$**: test $(4,4)$ against $A(1,7)R,B(2,3)R,C(3,5)B,D(5,2)B,E(6,6)B,F(7,1)R,G(8,4)R$: distances $6,3,2,3,4,6,4$ ⇒ neighbours $C(B),B(R),D(B)$ ⇒ **Blue**.
**PSet 4 P6**: test $(0,0,0)$; distances $3,2,\sqrt{10},\sqrt5,\sqrt2,\sqrt3$ ⇒ $k=1$: Obs 5 **Green**; $k=3$: Obs 5 (G), 6 (R), 2 (R) ⇒ **Red**.
:::

:::remember
kNN stores the data and votes among the $k$ closest points: normalise features first, choose $k$ by cross-validation (small $k$ = high variance, large $k$ = high bias), and pay $O(dn)$ per prediction.
:::

## 6. Curse of dimensionality (Worksheet 2, P3; outline)

Features uniform on $[0,1]$, independent; kNN uses training points within $0.05$ in **every** feature (a window of side 0.1).

| $p$ (features) | fraction of data in the window |
|---|---|
| 1 | $0.1=10\%$ |
| 2 | $0.1^2=1\%$ |
| 100 | $0.1^{100}=10^{-100}$ — essentially none |

To capture 10% of the data we need side $L=0.1^{1/p}$: $p=1$: 0.1; $p=2$: 0.316; $p=100$: **0.977** — almost the whole range of every feature. In high dimensions "nearest" neighbours are not near, so kNN's core assumption (similar inputs ⇒ similar labels) breaks down. This motivates dimensionality reduction (PCA, L06+).

:::remember
In high dimensions the volume near any point is exponentially tiny, so nearest neighbours stop being similar.
:::

## 7. Evaluating a classifier: accuracy and its trap

The 0/1 loss averaged over test points is the **misclassification rate** — an estimate of the probability that a point is incorrectly classified. For each test point the loss is 0 or 1.
$$
\text{Accuracy}=1-\text{misclassification rate}.
$$
**Issue: not meaningful when classes are imbalanced or skewed.**

:::example The no-ball example
Predict whether a bowler will **not** bowl a no-ball. 15 no-balls in an innings of 315 balls: no-ball = class 0 (4.76%), legal ball = class 1 (95.24%). A model that says "Yes (legal)" **all the time** has $300/315\approx95\%$ accuracy — "very accurate", but useless: it never catches a no-ball. **Imbalanced classes.**
:::

## 8. TP, TN, FP, FN and the confusion matrix

:::figs
l04-p40|The confusion matrix
l04-p41|Heart-disease example
l04-p42|No-ball example
:::

Binary problem, $\mathcal Y=\{0,1\}$ (0 = negative, 1 = positive). $y$ = actual labels (ground truth, gold labels/standard); classifier $h(x)=\hat y$. A prediction is "True" if $\hat y=y$, "False" if $\hat y\neq y$.

| | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **TP** | number of points with $y=1$ classified as $\hat y=1$ |
| **TN** | $y=0$ classified as $\hat y=0$ |
| **FP** (false alarm) | $y=0$ classified as $\hat y=1$ |
| **FN** (a miss) | $y=1$ classified as $\hat y=0$ |

**Decoding trick**: the second word is what the model **said**; the first says whether it was **right**. "False negative" = said negative, wrongly — a sick patient sent home.

:::def Confusion matrix (contingency table) — slide layout
Rows = **predicted** class $\hat y$, columns = **true** class $y$:

| | Actual 1 (Positive) | Actual 0 (Negative) | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Predicted 1** | TP | FP | predicted total positives |
| **Predicted 0** | FN | TN | predicted total negatives |
| **Total** | $P=TP+FN$ (actual positives) | $N=FP+TN$ (actual negatives) | |

Diagonal = correct, off-diagonal = mistakes. (The slide prints "N = P + TN"; it should read $N=FP+TN$.) **Many textbooks and scikit-learn transpose this** (truth on rows) — always check the axis labels (Worksheet 2 uses the transposed layout).
:::

**Slide examples:**

| Heart disease (1 = disease) | $y=1$ | $y=0$ | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\hat y=1$ | TP = 100 | FP = 10 | 110 |
| $\hat y=0$ | FN = 5 | TN = 50 | 55 |
| Total | P = 105 | N = 60 | 165 |

Out of 165 cases: predicted "Yes" 110 times and "No" 55 times; in reality "Yes" 105 times and "No" 60 times.

| No-ball (1 = legal ball) | $y=1$ | $y=0$ | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\hat y=1$ | TP = 298 | FP = 7 | 305 |
| $\hat y=0$ | FN = 2 | TN = 8 | 10 |
| Total | P = 300 | N = 15 | 315 |

The model (trained on 15 years of bowlers) predicted 305 regular balls and 10 no-balls (8 correct, 2 incorrect); it missed 7 real no-balls (FP = 7, since "positive" = legal).

:::remember
Rows = what the model said, columns = the truth; $P=TP+FN$ and $N=FP+TN$ are column totals.
:::

## 9. Metrics from the confusion matrix

:::figs
l04-p45|Metric definitions
:::

Each metric is a fraction of **one row or one column**: column metrics condition on the truth, row metrics on the prediction.

| Metric | Formula | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| **Accuracy** | $\dfrac{TP+TN}{P+N}$ | overall, how often correct? |
| **Error rate** | $1-\text{Acc}=\dfrac{FP+FN}{P+N}$ | overall, how often wrong? |
| **Sensitivity / Recall / TPR** $S_e$ | $\dfrac{TP}{TP+FN}=\dfrac{TP}{P}$ | when it's actually positive, how often does it predict positive? |
| **Specificity / TNR** $S_p$ | $\dfrac{TN}{TN+FP}=\dfrac{TN}{N}=1-FPR$ | when actually negative, how often predict negative? |
| **False positive rate** | $\dfrac{FP}{TN+FP}=\dfrac{FP}{N}$ | actual negative — how often predicted positive? |
| **Precision** | $\dfrac{TP}{TP+FP}$ | when it predicts positive, how often is it positive? |
| **Negative predictive value** | $\dfrac{TN}{TN+FN}$ | when it predicts negative, how often is it negative? |

**Heart-disease numbers (slides p46–47):** accuracy $(100+50)/165=0.91$; error $(10+5)/165=0.09$; sensitivity $100/105=0.95$; FPR $10/60=0.17$; specificity $50/60=0.83$; precision $100/110=0.91$; NPV $50/55=0.91$.

**No-ball numbers:** accuracy $306/315=0.971$, sensitivity $298/300=0.993$, specificity $8/15=0.533$ (**exposes the problem** — only half the no-balls are caught), precision $298/305=0.977$.

Net analogy: recall = fraction of all fish in the lake that ended up in your net; precision = fraction of what's in your net that is fish. A net scooping the whole lake has perfect recall, terrible precision.

:::warn Precision depends on prevalence; sensitivity and specificity do not
**PSet 4 P8**: 2030 people, TP 20, FP 180, FN 10, TN 1820 ⇒ sensitivity $20/30=0.667$, specificity $1820/2000=0.91$, FPR 0.09, **precision $20/200=0.10$**, NPV $1820/1830=0.995$, accuracy $1840/2030=0.906$. Only 30 are sick (1.5% prevalence), so 9% FPR on 2000 healthy people (180 FPs) swamps the 20 TPs. Still a useful screen: a negative is almost always right.
**2026 Quiz 5 Q3**: 10 000 patients, 1% prevalence ⇒ 100 positives, 9900 negatives. Recall 100% ⇒ TP = 100, FN = 0. Precision 10% ⇒ $100/(100+FP)=0.1$ ⇒ FP = 900, TN = 9000. Accuracy $9100/10000=91\%$, FPR $900/9900=1/11\approx9.09\%$. The flaw: 900 healthy people get false alarms. An **ROC curve would not expose this**: the huge TN count keeps FPR low; look at precision instead.
:::

:::remember
Recall = TP/P (did we catch the positives?), specificity = TN/N, FPR = FP/N, precision = TP/(TP+FP) (can we trust a positive?), NPV = TN/(TN+FN).
:::

## 10. When to use which metric; trade-offs

:::figs
l04-p50|Precision–recall trade-off
:::

**Decide which mistake is more expensive, then watch the metric that punishes it** (slide p48):

- **Disease detection: we do not want FN** (sending a sick patient home can be fatal) ⇒ watch **sensitivity** $TP/(TP+FN)$. Cancer classifier (2024/2025 Mid MCQ): prefer **FP ≫ FN**.
- **Fraud detection: we do not want FP** (blocking honest customers) ⇒ watch **specificity** $TN/(TN+FP)$ and **precision** $TP/(TP+FP)$. Spam filtering is similar: prioritise specificity (2025 Quiz 3).

**Precision–sensitivity (recall) trade-off** (p50): recall = how good we are at detecting diseased people; precision = how many diagnosed unhealthy really are. **Diagnose everyone unhealthy** ⇒ $S_e=1$, but precision may be low (TN = 0 inflates FP); for the heart data precision falls to $P/(P+N)=105/165=0.64$. We want both high (ideally 1) ⇒ **combine them: F1-score**.

**Sensitivity–specificity trade-off** (p51): $S_p$ and $S_e$ measure how good we are at detecting healthy and diseased people respectively. **Diagnose everyone healthy** ⇒ $S_p=1$ but $S_e=0$. Ideally $S_p=S_e=1$, but that is unrealistic.

**The threshold slider.** Most classifiers output a score compared to a threshold. Healthy and sick score distributions overlap, so lowering the threshold catches more sick people (sensitivity ↑) but raises false alarms (specificity ↓), and vice versa.

:::example Loan classifier recall (2024 Mid MCQ 11)
Classifier predicts "will pay back"; recall 83% ⇒ of the customers who **would** pay back, 83% are approved and **17% have their application wrongly rejected**. It says nothing about "83% of all customers".
:::

## 11. ROC curve and AUC

:::figs
l04-p52|ROC curve and AUC
:::

:::def ROC curve
Slide the threshold from strict to lenient and plot, for each threshold,
$$
(\text{FPR},\text{TPR})=(1-S_p,\ S_e).
$$
- **TPR (sensitivity)**: how many correct positive results occur among all positive samples.
- **FPR ($1-$ specificity)**: how many incorrect positive results occur among all negative samples.
- **Best possible method**: $S_e=S_p=1$ — the **upper-left corner** $(0,1)$.
- **Random guess**: a point on the **diagonal** (line of no-discrimination) — no power.
- **AUC** (area under the ROC curve) quantifies the **power** of the classifier: 1 perfect, 0.5 random. The "alarmist" sits at $(1,1)$, the "optimist" at $(0,0)$.
:::

**AUC meaning (beyond the slides)**: the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a **higher score** than a randomly chosen negative — a ranking quality, independent of any single threshold. A curve below the diagonal (AUC 0.2) is "reliably wrong": flip the predictions to get AUC 0.8.

:::example ROC points and AUC by hand (PSet 4, P10)
20 instances sorted by score, labels p p n p p p n n p n | p n p n n n p n p n (10 p, 10 n), scores 0.9 … 0.1.
- $t=0.54$: TP 5, FP 1 ⇒ $(0.1,0.5)$; accuracy $(5+9)/20=0.70$.
- $t=0.51$: TP 6, FP 3 ⇒ $(0.3,0.6)$. $t=0.38$: TP 8, FP 5 ⇒ $(0.5,0.8)$.
- AUC: for each positive count negatives scored below it: $10,10,9,9,9,7,6,5,2,1$ ⇒ $68/100=0.68$. (Staircase check: TPR at each negative $0.2,0.5,0.5,0.6,0.7,0.8,0.8,0.8,0.9,1.0$, sum 6.8 × 0.1 = 0.68.)
:::

:::remember
ROC plots TPR vs FPR over all thresholds; top-left is perfect, the diagonal is random, AUC = P(random positive outranks random negative).
:::

## 12. F-scores and Matthews correlation coefficient

:::figs
l04-p56|Why the harmonic mean: min ≤ HM ≤ GM ≤ AM ≤ max
:::

Higher recall can be bought with lower precision, so we need one measure combining them. Combined measures: **F1 score**, **Matthews correlation coefficient**, 11-point average precision, the breakeven point.

:::def F-scores — weighted harmonic mean
$$
F_\beta=\frac{1+\beta^2}{\dfrac{1}{\text{Precision}}+\dfrac{\beta^2}{\text{Recall}}},\quad\beta\ge0;\qquad
F_1=\frac{2}{\frac1{\text{Precision}}+\frac1{\text{Recall}}}=\frac{2\,\text{Precision}\cdot\text{Recall}}{\text{Precision}+\text{Recall}}=\frac{2TP}{2TP+FP+FN}.
$$
$\beta>1$ weights recall more (F2 for disease screening); $\beta<1$ weights precision more (F0.5 for fraud). Heart data: $F_1=200/215=0.93$, $F_2=0.94$, $F_{0.5}=0.92$.
:::

**Why the harmonic mean?** We could use the arithmetic (AM) or geometric (GM) mean, but **HM penalises the model the most** — a conservative average. For two positive numbers $\min\le HM\le GM\le AM\le\max$, and **an improvement in HM implies an improvement in AM or GM**. Slide plot (recall fixed at 70%): HM hugs the minimum; at 5% precision AM still says 37.5% while F1 honestly says 9.3%. Round-trip analogy: 60 km at 30 km/h and back at 60 km/h averages 40 km/h (the HM), not 45.

:::example
Fraud model flags one transaction, correctly; 50 frauds exist. Precision 1, recall 0.02: AM 0.51 (misleading), F1 $=\frac{2(1)(0.02)}{1.02}\approx0.039$ (truthful).
Worksheet 2 (MNIST 5-detector, truth on rows): TN 53 892, FP 687, FN 1891, TP 3530 ⇒ precision $3530/4217=0.837$, recall $3530/5421=0.651$, $F_1=7060/9638=0.733$; AM 0.744, GM 0.738 ⇒ HM ≤ GM ≤ AM ✓. Accuracy $57\,422/60\,000=0.957$, yet "always not-5" scores $54\,579/60\,000=0.910$ — accuracy is dominated by the easy majority class.
:::

:::def Matthews correlation coefficient (beyond the slides, in the outline)
$$
\text{MCC}=\frac{TP\cdot TN-FP\cdot FN}{\sqrt{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)}}\in[-1,1].
$$
Uses **all four cells** (F1 never looks at TN). It is the correlation coefficient (L01) between the true and predicted label vectors: $+1$ perfect, $0$ chance, $-1$ perfectly wrong (defined as 0 if a factor in the denominator is 0).
:::

| Model | Accuracy | F1 (class 1 positive) | MCC |
|---|---|---|---|
| heart disease | 0.91 | 0.93 | 0.80 |
| cricket model | 0.97 | 0.99 | 0.64 |
| cricket "always Yes" | 0.95 | 0.98 | 0 |

PSet 4 P9: 91 positives, 9 negatives, TP 90, FN 1, TN 0, FP 9 ⇒ accuracy 0.90, F1 $180/190=0.95$, MCC $=\frac{0-9}{\sqrt{99\cdot91\cdot9\cdot1}}\approx-0.03$ — only MCC tells the truth (it never identifies a negative). Heart: MCC $=\frac{5000-50}{\sqrt{110\cdot105\cdot60\cdot55}}=\frac{4950}{6173.7}\approx0.80$.

**F1 depends on which class is "positive"**: make no-ball the positive class ⇒ TP′ 8, FN′ 7, FP′ 2, TN′ 298 ⇒ $F_1'=16/25=0.64$ (vs 0.985). MCC is unchanged (symmetric under swapping classes). On imbalanced data make the rare, important class positive, or report MCC.

:::remember
F1 $=2TP/(2TP+FP+FN)$ is the harmonic mean of precision and recall (dragged down by the worse one); MCC uses all four cells and is the honest single number on imbalanced data.
:::
