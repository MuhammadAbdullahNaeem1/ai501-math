A square matrix can be understood through the directions it scales without turning. In order, this lecture covers: eigenvectors and eigenvalues, defined through the eigenvalue equation and the "only scaling" picture; how to compute them from the characteristic polynomial, with the vocabulary of eigenspectrum and eigenspace; the tutorial example; the eigen-decomposition $A=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}$; its interpretation as a change of basis, a scaling and a change back; the determinant as the product of the eigenvalues; the eigen-decomposition of the inverse; powers of a matrix; the meaning of a zero eigenvalue; and symmetric matrices with the spectral theorem. A clearly marked **preview** of quadratic forms, positive semi-definiteness and the SVD follows, because the previous two mid exams tested them.

:::intuition The photo filter (revision notes)
A photo filter moves each pixel at position $x$ to $Sx$, where $S=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$; a round logo becomes a tilted oval. Follow three pixels:
$$
S\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
S\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\3\end{bmatrix}=3\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
S\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}=1\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}.
$$
The first pixel is pushed off its line, but two special directions are **not tilted at all**: the diagonal is stretched by a factor of $3$, and the anti-diagonal is left unchanged. These directions are the eigenvectors, and $3$ and $1$ are the eigenvalues. Everything about $S$ — how it scales area, how to undo it, what happens when it is applied repeatedly — follows from them.
:::

## 1. Eigenvectors and eigenvalues

:::figs
l06-p04|Only scaling: Aq = λq
:::


:::background multiples of a vector, and 'staying on the same line'
**A scalar multiple** of a vector points along the same line: $3\,(1,2)=(3,6)$ is three times as long in the same direction, and $-1\,(1,2)=(-1,-2)$ points the opposite way along the same line.

**Most vectors get turned by a matrix.** With $A=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$,
$$
A\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix},
$$
which points in a **different** direction from $(1,0)$.

**A few special vectors only get stretched.**
$$
A\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\3\end{bmatrix}=3\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}.
$$
The output is just $3$ times the input. Such a vector is an **eigenvector**, and the stretch factor $3$ is its **eigenvalue**. "Eigen" is German for "own" — these are the matrix's own special directions.
:::


:::def Eigenvectors and eigenvalues
For square matrices, **eigenvectors** and **eigenvalues** are the vectors and numbers that make up the **eigen-decomposition** of a matrix; they reveal its structure. For $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$, a vector $q\in\mathbb{R}^n$ with $q\neq\mathbf 0$ is an **eigenvector** of $A$ if
$$
Aq=\lambda q\qquad(\text{the eigenvalue equation})
$$
for some number $\lambda$, which is called the **eigenvalue** of $A$ associated with $q$.
:::

**The linear-transformation picture.** Feeding $q$ into the system $A$ returns $\lambda q$: the action is **only scaling**. Geometrically, $Aq$ lies on the same line through the origin as $q$. Along that line $A$ stretches (if $|\lambda|>1$), shrinks (if $|\lambda|<1$), flips (if $\lambda<0$) or squashes to the origin (if $\lambda=0$) — but it never moves a point off the line.

Three remarks on the definition:

- The condition $q\neq\mathbf 0$ matters, because $A\mathbf 0=\lambda\mathbf 0$ holds for every $\lambda$ and says nothing.
- **Eigenvectors are defined only up to scale**, since $A(cq)=c\,Aq=\lambda(cq)$. What matters is the direction; we usually normalise to $\|q\|_2=1$, and even then the sign is free.
- Only **square** matrices have eigenvalues, because $Aq$ and $q$ must live in the same space.

For example, $(0,1)$ is not an eigenvector of $S$, since $S(0,1)^\top=(1,2)^\top$ is not a multiple of $(0,1)$; but $(-2,-2)$ is, since $S(-2,-2)^\top=(-6,-6)^\top=3\,(-2,-2)^\top$.

:::remember
An eigenvector is a non-zero direction that $A$ only scales: $Aq=\lambda q$.
:::

## 2. Computing eigenvalues and eigenvectors

:::background determinants, quadratic equations, and why det(A − λI) = 0
#### The 2×2 determinant
$$
\det\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}=ad-bc .\qquad\text{Example: }\det\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\4&2\end{bmatrix}=3\cdot2-1\cdot4=2 .
$$

#### Subtracting $\lambda I$
$\lambda I$ is the identity matrix scaled by $\lambda$, so subtracting it takes $\lambda$ away from the **diagonal entries only**:
$$
A-\lambda I=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&0\\0&\lambda\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2-\lambda&1\\1&2-\lambda\end{bmatrix}.
$$

#### Solving a quadratic equation
To solve $\lambda^2-4\lambda+3=0$, look for two numbers that **multiply to $3$** and **add to $-4$**: they are $-1$ and $-3$. So $(\lambda-1)(\lambda-3)=0$, and the solutions are $\lambda=1$ and $\lambda=3$. If you cannot spot the factors, use the formula
$$
\lambda=\frac{4\pm\sqrt{4^2-4\cdot1\cdot3}}{2}=\frac{4\pm2}{2}\in\{1,3\}.
$$

#### Why we need $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$
We want a **non-zero** vector $q$ with $(A-\lambda I)q=\mathbf 0$ — that is, the matrix $A-\lambda I$ must squash some non-zero vector to zero. From L02, a matrix does that exactly when it is **singular**, i.e. when its determinant is $0$. So the eigenvalues are precisely the values of $\lambda$ that make $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$.
:::

Move everything to one side of the eigenvalue equation, writing $\lambda q=\lambda Iq$:
$$
Aq=\lambda q\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Aq-\lambda Iq=\mathbf 0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad (A-\lambda I)\,q=\mathbf 0 .
$$
We want a **non-zero** $q$ that $A-\lambda I$ sends to zero. If $A-\lambda I$ were invertible, the only solution would be $q=(A-\lambda I)^{-1}\mathbf 0=\mathbf 0$. So a non-zero solution exists exactly when $A-\lambda I$ is singular, that is, when
$$
p(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I)=0 .
$$
The function $p$ is the **characteristic polynomial** of $A$, and it has degree $n$. (The slide writes $\det(Aq-\lambda q)$ as shorthand; the determinant is that of the **matrix** $A-\lambda I$.)

The vocabulary:

- $A$ has $n$ eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n$ (the roots of $p$, counted with multiplicity) and correspondingly $n$ eigenvectors.
- The **eigenspectrum** (or spectrum) is the set of eigenvalues.
- The **eigenspace** is the span of the eigenvectors; for a particular $\lambda$ it is $\mathcal N(A-\lambda I)$, the null space of L03.

:::def The recipe
1. Write down $A-\lambda I$, subtracting $\lambda$ from the diagonal entries only.
2. Compute $p(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I)$. For a $2\times2$ matrix there is a shortcut:
$$
p(\lambda)=\lambda^2-(a+d)\,\lambda+(ad-bc)=\lambda^2-\operatorname{tr}(A)\,\lambda+\det A .
$$
3. Solve $p(\lambda)=0$.
4. For each eigenvalue $\lambda_i$, solve $(A-\lambda_iI)q=\mathbf 0$. One row is enough: the other rows are multiples of it, precisely because we chose $\lambda_i$ to make the matrix singular.
5. Choose a simple $q$, normalise it, and **check** that $Aq=\lambda q$.

Two sanity checks: $\sum_i\lambda_i=\operatorname{tr}A$ and $\prod_i\lambda_i=\det A$.
:::

:::example The slide's tutorial problem: $A=\begin{bmatrix}4&-2\\-2&1\end{bmatrix}$
**Step 1 — the characteristic polynomial.**
$$
\det(A-\lambda I)=\det\begin{bmatrix}4-\lambda&-2\\-2&1-\lambda\end{bmatrix}
=(4-\lambda)(1-\lambda)-(-2)(-2)
=(\lambda^2-5\lambda+4)-4
=\lambda^2-5\lambda .
$$
**Step 2 — the eigenvalues.** $\lambda^2-5\lambda=\lambda(\lambda-5)=0$ gives $\lambda_1=0$ and $\lambda_2=5$. As a check, $0+5=5=\operatorname{tr}A$ and $0\cdot5=0=\det A$.

**Step 3 — the eigenvector for $\lambda=0$.** Solve $(A-0I)v=\mathbf 0$:
$$
\begin{bmatrix}4&-2\\-2&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}v_1\\v_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix}.
$$
The second row gives $v_2=2v_1$, and substituting into the first row gives $4v_1-2(2v_1)=0$, which is consistent. Hence $v=v_1(1,2)$, with $\|v\|=v_1\sqrt5$; choosing $v_1=1/\sqrt5$,
$$
\mathbf v_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}.
$$
**Step 4 — the eigenvector for $\lambda=5$** (completing the slide). Here
$$
A-5I=\begin{bmatrix}-1&-2\\-2&-4\end{bmatrix},
$$
and the first row gives $v_1=-2v_2$, so
$$
\mathbf v_2=\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\begin{bmatrix}-2\\1\end{bmatrix}.
$$
**Check.** $A(-2,1)^\top=(-10,5)^\top=5\,(-2,1)^\top$. Moreover $\mathbf v_1^\top\mathbf v_2=0$, as it must be for a symmetric matrix. Note that $\lambda=0$ means $A$ is singular: column 1 is $-2$ times column 2.
:::

:::example The photo filter $S=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$ and the practice filter $M=\begin{bmatrix}3&2\\2&0\end{bmatrix}$
**For $S$.** $p(\lambda)=(2-\lambda)^2-1=\lambda^2-4\lambda+3=(\lambda-3)(\lambda-1)$.
$$
\lambda=3:\ \begin{bmatrix}-1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}v=\mathbf 0\ \Rightarrow\ v_2=v_1,\quad q_1=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix};
\qquad
\lambda=1:\ \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}v=\mathbf 0\ \Rightarrow\ v_2=-v_1,\quad q_2=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}.
$$
**For $M$.** $p(\lambda)=(3-\lambda)(-\lambda)-4=\lambda^2-3\lambda-4=(\lambda-4)(\lambda+1)$.
$$
\lambda=4:\ \begin{bmatrix}-1&2\\2&-4\end{bmatrix}v=\mathbf 0\ \Rightarrow\ v_1=2v_2,\quad q_1=\frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix};
\qquad
\lambda=-1:\ \begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}v=\mathbf 0\ \Rightarrow\ v_2=-2v_1,\quad q_2=\frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix}.
$$
Checking, $M(2,1)^\top=(8,4)^\top=4\,(2,1)^\top$ and $M(1,-2)^\top=(-1,2)^\top=-(1,-2)^\top$. So $M$ stretches the direction $(2,1)$ by $4$ and **flips** the direction $(1,-2)$.

**PSet 6, Problem 3.** For $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&4\\2&3\end{bmatrix}$, $p(\lambda)=\lambda^2-4\lambda-5=(\lambda-5)(\lambda+1)$, with eigenvectors $(1,1)$ and $(-2,1)$. These are not orthogonal, because $A$ is not symmetric.
:::

:::derive the shortcut $\lambda^2-\operatorname{tr}(A)\,\lambda+\det A$
Expand the determinant for a general $2\times2$ matrix:
$$
\det\begin{bmatrix}a-\lambda&b\\c&d-\lambda\end{bmatrix}=(a-\lambda)(d-\lambda)-bc=\lambda^2-(a+d)\lambda+(ad-bc).
$$
The middle coefficient $a+d$ is the trace (sum of the diagonal) and the last term $ad-bc$ is the determinant. So you can write the polynomial down without expanding anything.

**Bonus.** If the roots are $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$, then $\lambda^2-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\lambda+\lambda_1\lambda_2$ is the same polynomial, so comparing coefficients gives $\lambda_1+\lambda_2=\operatorname{tr}A$ and $\lambda_1\lambda_2=\det A$ — the two sanity checks in the recipe.
:::

:::remember
Solve $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$ for the eigenvalues, then $(A-\lambda I)q=\mathbf 0$ for each eigenvector, and check the results against the trace and the determinant.
:::

## 3. The eigen-decomposition $A=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}$

:::background multiplying by a diagonal matrix
**On the right, a diagonal matrix scales the columns.**
$$
\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&10\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\cdot5&2\cdot10\\3\cdot5&4\cdot10\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5&20\\15&40\end{bmatrix}:
$$
column 1 was multiplied by $5$ and column 2 by $10$.

**On the left, it scales the rows** instead. You only need the column version here.

**Putting columns side by side.** Writing $Q=[\,q_1\ q_2\,]$ means "the matrix whose first column is $q_1$ and second column is $q_2$". Then $AQ=[\,Aq_1\ Aq_2\,]$, because each column of a product is $A$ times the corresponding column (L02).
:::

Write the $n$ eigenvalue equations side by side,
$$
Aq_1=\lambda_1q_1,\quad Aq_2=\lambda_2q_2,\quad\dots,\quad Aq_n=\lambda_nq_n,
$$
and collect the eigenvectors as the columns of $Q$ and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of $\Lambda$:
$$
Q=\begin{bmatrix}(q_1)_1&(q_2)_1&\cdots&(q_n)_1\\(q_1)_2&(q_2)_2&\cdots&(q_n)_2\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\(q_1)_n&(q_2)_n&\cdots&(q_n)_n\end{bmatrix}=\big[\,q_1\ q_2\ \cdots\ q_n\,\big],
\qquad
\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1&0&\cdots&0\\0&\lambda_2&\cdots&0\\ \vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\0&0&\cdots&\lambda_n\end{bmatrix}.
$$
Column $i$ of $AQ$ is $Aq_i$, and column $i$ of $Q\Lambda$ is $\lambda_iq_i$ (multiplying by a diagonal matrix on the right scales the columns). So all $n$ equations together say
$$
AQ=Q\Lambda ,
$$
and multiplying on the right by $Q^{-1}$ gives the **eigen-decomposition** (EVD):
$$
\boxed{\;A=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}\;}
$$
The last step requires $Q$ to be invertible, that is, the eigenvectors must be linearly independent. This always holds for symmetric matrices (§9), and also whenever the $n$ eigenvalues are distinct.

:::example Decompositions of the two filters
For the photo filter,
$$
S=\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}}_{Q}
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}}_{\Lambda}
\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}}_{Q^{-1}=Q^\top}.
$$
Multiplying out confirms it:
$$
Q\Lambda Q^\top=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}3&-1\\3&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}4&2\\2&4\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}=S .
$$
For the practice filter, with the eigenvalues ordered $(4,-1)$,
$$
M=\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}4&0\\0&-1\end{bmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&-2\end{bmatrix},
$$
where here $Q^{-1}=Q^\top=Q$. Listing the eigenvalues in a different order swaps the columns of $Q$ along with them: what matters is the **pairing** of each $\lambda_i$ with its $q_i$.
:::

:::derive why $AQ=Q\Lambda$, column by column
Compute both sides one column at a time.

**Left side.** Column $i$ of $AQ$ is $Aq_i$. Because $q_i$ is an eigenvector, $Aq_i=\lambda_iq_i$.

**Right side.** Column $i$ of $Q\Lambda$ is column $i$ of $Q$ scaled by the $i$-th diagonal entry of $\Lambda$, which is $\lambda_iq_i$.

Every column agrees, so $AQ=Q\Lambda$. If $Q$ has an inverse, multiply both sides on the **right** by $Q^{-1}$:
$$
AQQ^{-1}=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad A=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}.
$$
:::

:::remember
$AQ=Q\Lambda$ gives $A=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}$, with the eigenvectors as the columns of $Q$ and the eigenvalues on the diagonal of $\Lambda$, in the same order.
:::

## 4. What the EVD reveals: change of basis → scaling → change back

:::figs
l06-p10|Q⁻¹ → Λ → Q
l06-p11|Visualisation: change of basis, scale, change back
:::


:::background what 'change of basis' means
**Coordinates depend on your axes.** The point $(1,0)$ on ordinary axes can also be described using the tilted axes $q_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ and $q_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(-1,1)$. In those axes it is $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ along $q_1$ and $-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ along $q_2$:
$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt2}q_1-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}q_2=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}-\frac12\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}.
$$
**"Change of basis"** just means re-describing the same point using different axes. $Q^{-1}x$ gives the new description; $Q$ converts back.

**Why choose the eigenvector axes?** Along those axes the matrix does nothing but stretch — no turning — so its action is as simple as possible.
:::


$$
x\in\mathbb{R}^n\ \longrightarrow\ \boxed{\ Q^{-1}\ }\ \longrightarrow\ \boxed{\ \Lambda\ }\ \longrightarrow\ \boxed{\ Q\ }\ \longrightarrow\ Ax
$$
The three boxes are a **transformation (change of basis)**, a **scaling (in the new basis)** and an **inverse transformation (change of basis back)**. Reading $Ax=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}x$ from right to left:

1. $c=Q^{-1}x$ gives the coordinates of $x$ in the eigenbasis, so that $x=Qc=\sum_ic_iq_i$;
2. $\Lambda c$ scales coordinate $i$ by $\lambda_i$, each coordinate separately, with **no mixing**;
3. $Q(\Lambda c)=\sum_i\lambda_ic_iq_i$ converts back to standard coordinates.

In a single formula:
$$
Ax=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i\,c_i\,q_i\qquad\text{where}\qquad x=\sum_{i=1}^nc_i\,q_i .
$$
A matrix that looks complicated in the standard basis is **only scaling** in its eigenbasis.

**The visualisation (slide p11).** A unit circle carrying the vectors $q_1,q_2$ is mapped by $A$ directly to an ellipse with axes $\lambda_1q_1$ and $\lambda_2q_2$. Going the long way round, $Q^{-1}$ turns $q_1,q_2$ onto $e_1,e_2$; $\Lambda$ stretches the circle into an ellipse aligned with the axes, $\lambda_1e_1$ and $\lambda_2e_2$; and $Q$ turns it back. Both routes produce the same ellipse.

:::example The red pixel, step by step
For the photo filter, $S=Q\operatorname{diag}(3,1)Q^\top$ where $Q$ is a rotation by $+45^\circ$; the sign of $q_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(-1,1)$ was chosen precisely to make $Q$ a rotation. The filter therefore **rotates by $-45^\circ$, stretches horizontally by $3$, and rotates back by $+45^\circ$**. Following $x=(1,0)$:
$$
c=Q^\top x=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\Lambda c=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}3\\-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
Q\Lambda c=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3\\-1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}=Sx .
$$
Designing a filter is easy in the eigenbasis. "Stretch the diagonal by $2$ and halve the anti-diagonal" is
$$
Q\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&\tfrac12\end{bmatrix}Q^\top=\begin{bmatrix}1.25&0.75\\0.75&1.25\end{bmatrix}.
$$
:::

:::example PSet 6, Problem 4
:::orig [PSet 6 · Problem 4 ↗](pdfs/psets/PSet-06.pdf#page=1) · [solution](pdfs/psets/PSet-06-Solution.pdf)
Every symmetric $A\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times2}$ can be written $A=Q\Lambda Q^\top$. Reading $Ax=Q\Lambda Q^\top x$ right to left: (i) $u=Q^\top x$, the coordinates of $x$ along the eigenvectors; (ii) $v=\Lambda u$, scale them by $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$; (iii) $y=Qv$, convert back. Use
$$A_1=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}=Q\begin{pmatrix}4&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}Q^\top,\quad A_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\2&0\end{pmatrix}=Q\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&-2\end{pmatrix}Q^\top,\quad Q=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},$$
and the points $x_1=(1,0)$, $x_2=(1,1)$, $x_3=(0,1)$. (a) Write $q_1,q_2,\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ for each matrix; check $Q^\top Q=I$ and compute $\det Q$ — rotation or reflection? (b) Compute $A_1x$ and $A_2x$ directly for each point. (c) Compute $u=Q^\top x$ for each point; which $u$ has a zero entry, and why? Check $\|u\|=\|x\|$. (d) Compute $v=\Lambda u$: along each eigenvector, is the point stretched or shrunk, by what factor, and is it flipped? (e) Compute $y=Qv$ and confirm it matches (b). (f) Which point is only scaled, never turned, by both matrices? Why does $A_1$ pull $x_1$ and $x_3$ towards the diagonal? Show $A_2=2\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and describe what it does. (g) Sketch the image of the unit circle under $A_1$ and $A_2$ and relate its semi-axes to the eigen-pairs.
:::
The matrices $A_1=\begin{bmatrix}3&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}$ (eigenvalues $4,2$) and $A_2=\begin{bmatrix}0&2\\2&0\end{bmatrix}$ (eigenvalues $2,-2$) share
$$
Q=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \det Q=-1,
$$
so this $Q$ is a reflection, and it preserves lengths: $\|Q^\top x\|=\|x\|$. The point $x_2=(1,1)$ lies along $q_1$, so both matrices merely scale it. $A_2=2\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$ reflects the plane across the diagonal and doubles every length, so it maps the unit circle to a circle of radius $2$; the flip is invisible in the shape but visible in the orientation, consistent with $\det A_2=-4<0$. $A_1$ maps the unit circle to an ellipse with semi-axis $4$ along $(1,1)$ and $2$ along $(1,-1)$, whose area is $8\pi=|\det A_1|\,\pi$.
:::

$A=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}$ means: rewrite $x$ in eigen-coordinates, scale coordinate $i$ by $\lambda_i$, and rewrite back — $Ax=\sum_i\lambda_ic_iq_i$.

## 5. Determinant in terms of eigenvalues

:::figs
l06-p12|det A = product of eigenvalues
:::


:::background the product rule for determinants
**The rule:** $\det(AB)=\det(A)\,\det(B)$ — the area-scaling of "do $B$, then $A$" is the product of the two scalings.

**A consequence:** since $Q^{-1}Q=I$ and $\det I=1$, we get $\det(Q^{-1})\det(Q)=1$, so $\det(Q^{-1})=\dfrac{1}{\det Q}$.

**The determinant of a diagonal matrix** is the product of the diagonal entries: $\det\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&5\end{bmatrix}=15$. Geometrically it stretches one axis by $3$ and the other by $5$, so areas grow by $15$.
:::


Using $\det(BC)=\det B\,\det C$, $\det(Q^{-1})=1/\det Q$, and the fact that the determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its diagonal entries,
$$
\det(A)=\det(Q\Lambda Q^{-1})=\det(Q)\,\det(\Lambda)\,\det(Q^{-1})=\det(Q)\Big(\prod_{i=1}^n\lambda_i\Big)\frac{1}{\det(Q)}=\prod_{i=1}^n\lambda_i .
$$
**If one of the eigenvalues is zero, the determinant of the matrix is zero.**

**Connecting this with the meaning of the determinant** (as the slide encourages). $|\det A|$ is the factor by which $A$ scales area or volume. In the eigenbasis, $A$ stretches axis $i$ by $\lambda_i$, so a unit box becomes a box of volume $\prod_i\lambda_i$, and the changes of basis $Q^{-1}$ and $Q$ cancel. For the photo filter, $\det S=3=3\cdot1$, so the logo's area grows from $\pi$ to $3\pi$. For the practice filter, $\det M=-4=4\cdot(-1)$: areas grow fourfold, and the minus sign records the flip. Applying $S$ twice multiplies areas by $9$.

In the same way, $\operatorname{tr}(A)=\sum_i\lambda_i$; for a $2\times2$ matrix this can be read directly from $p(\lambda)=\lambda^2-\operatorname{tr}(A)\,\lambda+\det A$.

:::remember
$\det A=\prod_i\lambda_i$ and $\operatorname{tr}A=\sum_i\lambda_i$; a zero eigenvalue means zero volume.
:::

## 6. EVD of the inverse

**$A^{-1}$ has the same eigenvectors as $A$, with eigenvalues $1/\lambda_i$.** Assume $A$ is invertible, so that every eigenvalue is non-zero, and multiply the eigenvalue equation by $A^{-1}$:
$$
Aq=\lambda q\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A^{-1}Aq=\lambda\,A^{-1}q\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A^{-1}q=\frac{1}{\lambda}\,q .
$$
So $q$ is an eigenvector of $A^{-1}$ with eigenvalue $1/\lambda$. In matrix form,
$$
A^{-1}=\big(Q\Lambda Q^{-1}\big)^{-1}=Q\Lambda^{-1}Q^{-1},\qquad \Lambda^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}\big(1/\lambda_1,\dots,1/\lambda_n\big).
$$

:::example Undo buttons
Undoing "stretch the diagonal by $3$" means "shrink the diagonal by $\tfrac13$" along the same directions:
$$
S^{-1}=Q\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac13&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}Q^\top=\frac13\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix},
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
S^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\end{bmatrix}=\frac13\begin{bmatrix}4-1\\-2+2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}.
$$
The practice filter's inverse has eigenvalues $\tfrac14$ and $\tfrac{1}{-1}=-1$: undoing a flip is flipping again. Since $(8,4)=4\,(2,1)$ lies along $q_1$, we get $M^{-1}(8,4)^\top=\tfrac14(8,4)^\top=(2,1)^\top$ without any further work.
:::

:::remember
Inverting $A$ inverts each eigenvalue and keeps the eigenvectors.
:::

## 7. Powers of a matrix

:::background powers of a diagonal matrix
**A diagonal matrix multiplied by itself** just squares the diagonal entries:
$$
\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}^2=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}9&0\\0&4\end{bmatrix},\qquad \begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&2\end{bmatrix}^k=\begin{bmatrix}3^k&0\\0&2^k\end{bmatrix}.
$$
That is why the eigen-decomposition is so useful for powers: it turns the hard problem ($A^k$) into the easy one ($\Lambda^k$).
:::

$$
A^2=AA=Q\Lambda\underbrace{Q^{-1}Q}_{=\,I}\Lambda Q^{-1}=Q\Lambda^2Q^{-1},
\qquad\text{and in general}\qquad
A^n=Q\Lambda^nQ^{-1},\quad \Lambda^n=\operatorname{diag}\big(\lambda_1^n,\dots,\lambda_n^n\big).
$$
**Connecting this with the linear-transformation picture** (as the slide encourages). Applying $A$ $k$ times means going round the loop $k$ times; each inner $Q^{-1}Q$ cancels, so we change basis once, scale $k$ times, and change back once.

More generally, **any polynomial** $f(A)=c_0I+c_1A+c_2A^2+\cdots$ has the same eigenvectors as $A$, with eigenvalues $f(\lambda_i)$; a term $A^{-1}$ contributes $1/\lambda_i$. In particular, adding $cI$ shifts every eigenvalue by $c$: in PSet 6, Problem 3, $A+I$ has eigenvalues $6$ and $0$, so $A+I$ is singular.

:::example Exam favourites
**Powers of the photo filter.**
$$
S^k=Q\begin{bmatrix}3^k&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}Q^\top=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}3^k+1&3^k-1\\3^k-1&3^k+1\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
S^5=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}244&242\\242&244\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}122&121\\121&122\end{bmatrix},
$$
with no matrix multiplication at all. Repeated application pulls every vector towards the eigenvector with the largest $|\lambda|$, so the logo becomes a needle along the diagonal.

**PSet 6, Problem 5.** For $A=\begin{bmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}$ the eigenvalues are $1$ and $3$, with $q_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ and $q_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(-1,1)$, so
$$
A^k=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}1+3^k&1-3^k\\1-3^k&1+3^k\end{bmatrix}.
$$
If a $2\times2$ matrix $B$ has eigenvalues $3$ and $4$, then $\det(B-\lambda I)=(3-\lambda)(4-\lambda)=\lambda^2-7\lambda+12$.

**2025 Mid, Problem 8.** Let $C=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$.

1. *Why are $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ likely eigenvectors?* Equal diagonal entries and equal off-diagonal entries treat the two coordinates symmetrically, so the "sum" direction and the "difference" direction are preserved: $C(1,1)^\top=(3,3)^\top$ and $C(1,-1)^\top=(1,-1)^\top$.
2. *The decomposition.* Normalising,
$$
Q=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad Q^{-1}=Q^\top .
$$
3. *$C^2$ from the decomposition.*
$$
C^2=Q\Lambda^2Q^\top=\frac12\left(9\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}+1\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{bmatrix}\right)=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}10&8\\8&10\end{bmatrix}=\boxed{\begin{bmatrix}5&4\\4&5\end{bmatrix}}.
$$

**2025 Mid, MCQ 13.** If $A$ has eigenvalues $1$ and $6$, then $f(A)=4A^3+6A^{-1}+2I$ has eigenvalues
$$
f(1)=4+6+2=12,\qquad f(6)=4\cdot216+1+2=867,
$$
which is option (b).

**2024 Mid, MCQ 21.** If $A$ has eigenvalues $2$ and $5$, then $3A^3+2I$ has eigenvalues $3\cdot8+2=26$ and $3\cdot125+2=377$. Option (a) lists $26$ and $127$; the first value is right and the second is what $\lambda^3+2$ would give. The method — evaluate $f(\lambda_i)$ — is what earns the marks, so trust your own computation.
:::

:::derive why $A^2=Q\Lambda^2Q^{-1}$
$$
A^2=AA=(Q\Lambda Q^{-1})(Q\Lambda Q^{-1})=Q\Lambda\,(Q^{-1}Q)\,\Lambda Q^{-1}=Q\Lambda\,I\,\Lambda Q^{-1}=Q\Lambda^2Q^{-1}.
$$
The $Q^{-1}$ at the end of the first copy meets the $Q$ at the start of the second copy, and they cancel to $I$. With $k$ copies, $k-1$ such pairs cancel, leaving $A^k=Q\Lambda^kQ^{-1}$.
:::

:::remember
$A^k=Q\Lambda^kQ^{-1}$, and every polynomial in $A$ (and $A^{-1}$) acts on the eigenvalues in the same way: $f(A)$ has eigenvalues $f(\lambda_i)$.
:::

## 8. Zero eigenvalues ⇔ dependent columns

Suppose $\lambda=0$ is an eigenvalue with eigenvector $q\neq\mathbf 0$. Then
$$
Aq=\lambda q=\mathbf 0 .
$$
Since $Aq$ is the combination of the columns of $A$ with weights given by the entries of $q$ — not all of them zero — we have found a non-trivial combination of the columns equal to zero. By definition, **the columns of $A$ are linearly dependent**.

:::thm Four equivalent facts
For a square matrix $A$:
$$
0\ \text{is an eigenvalue}\iff\text{the columns are dependent}\iff\det A=0\iff A\ \text{has no inverse}.
$$
A **negative** eigenvalue is harmless, since a flip can be undone; only a **zero** eigenvalue loses information.
:::

:::example The flatten filter
$$
F=Q\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}Q^\top=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}
$$
has two identical columns, and its eigenvector $(-1,1)$ is exactly the recipe for the dependence, $-a_1+a_2=\mathbf 0$. Its determinant is $\tfrac14-\tfrac14=0$. The pixels $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ both land on $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, so the original photo cannot be recovered and no inverse exists. And $F^k=F$: flattening twice is the same as flattening once.

The link with L05: redundant features give $X^\top X$ a **zero eigenvalue**, which is why it becomes singular. In 2024 MCQ 20, a zero eigenvalue implies dependent columns and a zero determinant, and the matrix is not invertible.
:::

:::remember
$\lambda=0$ means some non-zero $q$ is sent to $\mathbf 0$: the columns are dependent, $\det=0$, and there is no inverse.
:::

## 9. Symmetric matrices — the spectral theorem

:::figs
l06-p16|Spectral theorem
:::


:::background orthogonal matrices, recapped
**An orthogonal matrix** $Q$ has columns that are perpendicular to each other and each of length $1$ (L02). Then $Q^\top Q=I$, so **its inverse is simply its transpose**: $Q^{-1}=Q^\top$. No calculation needed — just flip the matrix.

**Geometrically**, an orthogonal matrix is a rotation (or a reflection): it turns the plane without stretching or squashing anything.

So for a symmetric matrix, $A=Q\Lambda Q^\top$ says: **rotate, stretch along perpendicular axes, rotate back.**
:::


:::thm The spectral theorem
For a **symmetric** matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ there exists an **orthonormal basis** of $\mathbb{R}^n$ consisting of eigenvectors of $A$, and **every eigenvalue is real**. Consequently $Q^\top Q=I$, so $Q^{-1}=Q^\top$ ($Q$ is an **orthonormal matrix**), and
$$
\boxed{\;A=Q\Lambda Q^\top\;}
$$
:::

:::proof Why $Q^{-1}=Q^\top$ fits with symmetry (slide p16)
Transposing $A=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}$, and using $\Lambda^\top=\Lambda$, gives
$$
A^\top=\big(Q^{-1}\big)^\top\Lambda\,Q^\top .
$$
Since $A=A^\top$, the two expressions describe the same matrix, and choosing $Q^{-1}=Q^\top$ makes them identical term by term. The spectral theorem guarantees that this choice is always available.

**Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.** Let $Aq_1=\lambda_1q_1$ and $Aq_2=\lambda_2q_2$ with $\lambda_1\neq\lambda_2$. Then
$$
\lambda_1\,q_1^\top q_2=(Aq_1)^\top q_2=q_1^\top A^\top q_2=q_1^\top Aq_2=\lambda_2\,q_1^\top q_2 ,
$$
so $(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\,q_1^\top q_2=0$, and therefore $q_1^\top q_2=0$.
:::

**In summary, a symmetric matrix has orthonormal eigenvectors and real eigenvalues.** Geometrically it acts by rotating (the orthonormal $Q^\top$), stretching along **perpendicular** axes ($\Lambda$), and rotating back ($Q$), so it always maps the unit circle to a clean ellipse whose axes point along the eigenvectors.

:::example Badly behaved, non-symmetric matrices
The matrix
$$
L=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\1&1\end{bmatrix}
$$
has the same eigenvalues as $S$, namely $3$ and $1$, but its eigenvectors $(2,1)$ and $(0,1)$ are **not perpendicular**. It still has a decomposition,
$$
L=Q\Lambda Q^{-1},\qquad Q=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\1&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad Q^{-1}=\frac12\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\neq Q^\top ,
$$
but its change of basis is a skew rather than a rotation.

The rotation by $90^\circ$, $R=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}$, has characteristic polynomial $\lambda^2+1$, which has **no real roots**: every direction is turned, and none is merely scaled. Symmetry is what guarantees real eigenvalues.

In 2025 MCQ 15, for symmetric $A$: the eigenvalues are real (true); eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal (true); $A=Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with orthonormal $Q$ (true); "$A$ cannot be diagonalised by an orthogonal matrix" (false). In 2024 MCQ 15: $A^\top=A$ (true); "$A$ must be diagonal" (false); real eigenvalues (true); orthogonal eigenvectors (true).
:::

:::remember
A symmetric matrix satisfies $A=Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with real eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors: rotate, stretch, rotate back.
:::

## 10. Preview: quadratic forms, PSD, SVD (outline Week 6; tested in 2024/2025 mids)

The 2026 slides so far end with the spectral theorem. The outline's Week 6 continues with the topics below, and **both previous mid exams tested them**, so it is worth knowing at least these facts.

**Quadratic forms and definiteness.** For a symmetric matrix, substituting $A=Q\Lambda Q^\top$ gives
$$
x^\top Ax=x^\top Q\Lambda Q^\top x=\sum_{i=1}^n\lambda_i\,\big(q_i^\top x\big)^2 .
$$
It follows that:

- $A$ is **positive semi-definite (PSD)** exactly when $x^\top Ax\ge0$ for all $x$, which holds exactly when **every eigenvalue is non-negative**, and also exactly when $A=B^\top B$ for some matrix $B$;
- $A$ is **positive definite** exactly when $x^\top Ax>0$ for every $x\neq\mathbf 0$, that is, when every eigenvalue is positive;
- $\det A>0$ alone does **not** guarantee PSD: $\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1)$ has determinant $1$. (In 2024 MCQ 22, statements (a), (b) and (c) are true and (d) is false.)
- Gram matrices are PSD, since $v^\top X^\top Xv=\|Xv\|^2\ge0$; this is the "bowl" of least squares, and adding $\lambda I$ in ridge regression makes it positive definite.

**The singular value decomposition (SVD).** Every matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$, of any shape, can be factored as
$$
A=U\Sigma V^\top ,
$$
where $U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}$ and $V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ are orthogonal and $\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ is diagonal, holding the **singular values** $\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\cdots\ge0$.

- **The link with the EVD.** From the factorisation,
$$
A^\top A=V\,\Sigma^\top\Sigma\,V^\top,\qquad AA^\top=U\,\Sigma\Sigma^\top\,U^\top ,
$$
so the columns of $V$ (the right singular vectors) are eigenvectors of $A^\top A$, the columns of $U$ (the left singular vectors) are eigenvectors of $AA^\top$, and $\sigma_i=\sqrt{\lambda_i(A^\top A)}$. Moreover $Av_i=\sigma_iu_i$.
- **The geometry.** $V^\top$ rotates or reflects in $\mathbb{R}^n$; $\Sigma$ scales (and pads or projects when $m\neq n$); $U$ rotates or reflects in $\mathbb{R}^m$. The unit circle is mapped to an ellipse with semi-axes $\sigma_iu_i$, and the right singular vectors give the corresponding input directions.
- **Determinants.** $\det(A^\top A)=\prod_i\sigma_i^2$. With $\sigma=(6,3,2)$ this is $36\cdot9\cdot4=1296$ (2025 MCQ 9); with $\sigma=(5,3,1)$ it is $225$ (2024 MCQ 24).
- **Low-rank approximation (the Eckart–Young theorem).** Among all matrices of rank $k$,
$$
A_k=\sum_{i=1}^k\sigma_i\,u_iv_i^\top
\qquad\text{minimises}\qquad \|A-A_k\|_F,\qquad\text{and}\qquad \|A-A_k\|_F^2=\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2 .
$$
Storing $A_k$ costs $mk+k+kn$ numbers. In the 2025 Mid, Problem 1, a $2048\times1536$ image of rank $80$ needs
$$
2048\cdot80+80+80\cdot1536=163\,840+80+122\,880=286\,800
$$
numbers instead of $3\,145\,728$, a saving of $90.88\%$; in the 2024 Mid, a $1920\times1080$ image with $k=120$ needs $360\,120$ instead of $2\,073\,600$, a saving of $82.64\%$.
- **The best rank-one approximation** is $B_1=\sigma_1u_1v_1^\top$. In the 2025 Mid, Problem 10, with $\sigma_1=5$, $u_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)$ and $v_1=(1,0)$,
$$
B_1=5\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}5/\sqrt2&0\\5/\sqrt2&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix},
$$
and the eigenvalues of $B^\top B$ are $\sigma_1^2=25$ and $\sigma_2^2=4$.
- **PCA** keeps the top $k$ principal components, those with the largest eigenvalues of the covariance matrix; projecting onto them minimises the $\ell_2$ reconstruction error, and discarding the small components reduces noise and redundancy.
