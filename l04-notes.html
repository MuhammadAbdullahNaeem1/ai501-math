Intuition: The bridge from L03

In L01–L03 a matrix was a machine we knew and we asked what it produces. In ML it is reversed: we see input–output examples and must find the machine. The data are still vectors (each patient is a point in Rd\mathbb{R}^dRd) and a dataset is a matrix with one row per patient. Our first learner, kNN, uses nothing more than the distances of L01.

#2. Loss functions

A loss L\mathcal LL quantifies the error of predicting yyy using hhh and xxx. Every loss below is normalised by nnn — a loss per sample — so datasets of different sizes are comparable. All are non-negative.

Definition: 0/1 loss

L0/1(h)=1n∑i=1n(1−δh(xi)−yi),δk={1k=00otherwise\mathcal L_{0/1}(h)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big(1-\delta_{h(x_i)-y_i}\big),\qquad \delta_k=\begin{cases}1&k=0\\0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}L0/1​(h)=n1​i=1∑n​(1−δh(xi​)−yi​​),δk​={10​k=0otherwise​
Each term is 1 for a mistake, 0 if correct, so it counts the mistakes made by hhh on DDD (divided by nnn). Not used frequently for training because it is non-differentiable and non-continuous (flat with jumps: a blindfolded hiker feels no slope).

Definition: Squared loss (MSE) and RMSE

Lsq(h)=1n∑i=1n(h(xi)−yi)2,Lrms(h)=1n∑i=1n(h(xi)−yi)2.\mathcal L_{sq}(h)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big(h(x_i)-y_i\big)^2,\qquad \mathcal L_{rms}(h)=\sqrt{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\big(h(x_i)-y_i\big)^2}.Lsq​(h)=n1​i=1∑n​(h(xi​)−yi​)2,Lrms​(h)=n1​i=1∑n​(h(xi​)−yi​)2​.

#3. Learning, overfitting and the train/test split

Learning is an optimisation problem:
h∗=arg⁡min⁡h∈HL(h).h^\ast=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal H}\mathcal L(h).h∗=argh∈Hmin​L(h).
(Slides write "min"; we want the minimiser.) This gives low loss on our data — but how can we ensure hhh gives low loss on inputs not in DDD?

Definition: The memoriser

h(x)={yi,∃ (xi,yi)∈D with xi=x,0,otherwise.h(x)=\begin{cases}y_i,&\exists\,(x_i,y_i)\in D\ \text{with}\ x_i=x,\\0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}h(x)={yi​,0,​∃(xi​,yi​)∈D with xi​=x,otherwise.​
0% loss on the training data (fits every point), large error on inputs not in DDD — the first glimpse of overfitting (a student who memorised last year's paper).

Fix: the train/test split. Split DDD into DTRD_{TR}DTR​ (training, 70/80/90%) and DTED_{TE}DTE​ (test, 30/20/10%). Learn on training data only and evaluate on test:
h∗=arg⁡min⁡h∈H1∣DTR∣∑(x,y)∈DTRL(x,y∣h),ϵTE=1∣DTE∣∑(x,y)∈DTEL(x,y∣h∗).h^\ast=\arg\min_{h\in\mathcal H}\frac{1}{|D_{TR}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D_{TR}}\mathcal L(x,y\mid h),\qquad \epsilon_{TE}=\frac{1}{|D_{TE}|}\sum_{(x,y)\in D_{TE}}\mathcal L(x,y\mid h^\ast).h∗=argh∈Hmin​∣DTR​∣1​(x,y)∈DTR​∑​L(x,y∣h),ϵTE​=∣DTE​∣1​(x,y)∈DTE​∑​L(x,y∣h∗).

How to split: the split should capture the variations in the distribution — i.i.d. (random) sampling for ordinary data; for time series, split with respect to time (train on the past, test on the future).

Common trap

You can only use the test dataset once, after deciding on the model using the training data. Peeking, tweaking and re-testing turns the test set into training data and makes the reported error optimistic. Also compute normalisation statistics on the training set only.

Validation set. Often DDD is split three ways: DTRD_{TR}DTR​ 80%, DVAD_{VA}DVA​ 10%, DTED_{TE}DTE​ 10%. Validation data evaluate the loss of an hhh learned on the training data; if the validation loss is high, change the hypothesis/model.

Split the training data into KKK folds (5 or 10). For j=1,…,Kj=1,\dots,Kj=1,…,K train on K−1K-1K−1 folds and compute the validation loss ℓj\ell_jℓj​ on fold jjj. Remember

Train fits parameters, validation tunes hyper-parameters, test is opened once; zero training error (the memoriser) proves nothing.

#4. Classification methods — the map

Predicting a categorical output is classification. Methods grouped by their tool: frequency table → Bayesian methods, decision trees; covariance matrix → linear discriminant analysis, logistic regression; similarity function → k-nearest neighbour (this lecture); others → neural networks, support vector machines.

#5. k-Nearest Neighbours (kNN)

Idea: similar features have similar labels. Two classes, two features: a query point surrounded by red points should be labelled red. Algorithm overview: given a new test point xxx, find the most similar points in the training data, and classify xxx "like" them.

Questions the slides pose: How do we determine similarity? (distance metric) How many neighbours? (kkk) How to resolve inconsistencies among neighbours? (majority vote, tie rules)

1-NN — the simplest ML classifier: use the label of the closest known point. Generalisation: take the kkk nearest neighbours and assign the most frequent label. Slide pictures: with k=3k=3k=3 the label is red, with k=7k=7k=7 the same query becomes blue — the answer can change with kkk.

Definition: Formal definition

D={(x1,y1),…,(xn,yn)}D=\{(x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_n,y_n)\}D={(x1​,y1​),…,(xn​,yn​)}, xix_ixi​ features, yi∈{1,…,M}y_i\in\{1,\dots,M\}yi​∈{1,…,M} class labels. For a query point xxx, the neighbour set Sx⊆DS_x\subseteq DSx​⊆D contains the kkk points closest to xxx, and
h(x)=majority vote (mode) of the labels in Sx.h(x)=\text{majority vote (mode) of the labels in }S_x .h(x)=majority vote (mode) of the labels in Sx​.
Regression version (average, or inverse-distance weighted average):
h(x)=1k∑(xi,yi)∈Sxyi,h(x)=∑Sxyi/dist⁡(x,xi)∑Sx1/dist⁡(x,xi).h(x)=\frac1k\sum_{(x_i,y_i)\in S_x}y_i,\qquad h(x)=\frac{\sum_{S_x}y_i/\operatorname{dist}(x,x_i)}{\sum_{S_x}1/\operatorname{dist}(x,x_i)}.h(x)=k1​(xi​,yi​)∈Sx​∑​yi​,h(x)=∑Sx​​1/dist(x,xi​)∑Sx​​yi​/dist(x,xi​)​.

Characteristics:

  • Instance-based (lazy) learning: builds no model during training; simply stores the training data.
  • Flexible: adapts to unseen data by computing distances when a prediction is needed; can draw very irregular boundaries.
  • No assumptions about the distribution of the data.
  • Non-parametric: no parameters are learned.
  • Hyper-parameters: kkk (number of neighbours) and the distance metric.
  • Complexity (time and storage) of prediction grows with the training-set size.

#Practical issues (slides p25–27)

  • Use odd kkk for binary classification — a 2-class vote can then never tie. (With 3 classes, odd kkk does not prevent ties: k=3k=3k=3 can give 1–1–1.)
  • Ties: use prior information (e.g. the more common class), or fall back to the 1-NN or (k−1)(k-1)(k−1)-NN classifier.
  • Missing values: replace with the average value of the feature (computed on training data).
  • Categorical variables — Hamming distance:
    dist⁡(x,x′)=∑i=1d(1−δxi−xi′)=#{i:xi≠xi′}.\operatorname{dist}(x,x')=\sum_{i=1}^d\big(1-\delta_{x_i-x'_i}\big)=\#\{i:x_i\neq x'_i\}.dist(x,x′)=i=1∑d​(1−δxi​−xi′​​)=#{i:xi​=xi′​}.
    (A+,smoker,male,urban)(A+,\text{smoker},\text{male},\text{urban})(A+,smoker,male,urban) vs (O+,smoker,female,urban)(O+,\text{smoker},\text{female},\text{urban})(O+,smoker,female,urban) ⇒ distance 2. Coding cities as 1, 2, 3 and using Euclidean distance invents a fake geometry.
  • Numeric distances: Minkowski dist⁡p(x,x′)=(∑j∣xj−xj′∣p)1/p\operatorname{dist}_p(x,x')=\big(\sum_j|x_j-x'_j|^p\big)^{1/p}distp​(x,x′)=(∑j​∣xj​−xj′​∣p)1/p — p=2p=2p=2 Euclidean (default), p=1p=1p=1 Manhattan, p→∞p\to\inftyp→∞ Chebyshev. #Choice of kkk

  • k=1k=1k=1: sensitive to noise, high variance; a single mislabelled point carves out its own island. Increasing kkk makes the algorithm less sensitive to noise.
  • k=nk=nk=n: every query has the same neighbours (all points) ⇒ always predicts the overall majority class — maximally stable but blind. Decreasing kkk enables capturing finer structure of the space.
  • Idea: pick kkk not too large, not too small (data-dependent). How? Learn it from the data: split into training and validation; start from k=1k=1k=1 and iterate, using (5- or 10-fold) cross-validation, computing the validation loss with the training data; choose the kkk that minimises validation loss. This is the only learning required for kNN.
  • Bias–variance: small kkk ⇒ low bias, high variance; large kkk ⇒ high bias, low variance (2025 Mid MCQ 19; 2024 MCQ 8). A highly non-linear boundary favours a small kkk (PSet 4 P6 d).
  • Training error at k=1k=1k=1 is 0 (each point is its own neighbour) — meaningless; only validation error counts.

#6. Curse of dimensionality (Worksheet 2, P3; outline)

Features uniform on [0,1][0,1][0,1], independent; kNN uses training points within 0.050.050.05 in every feature (a window of side 0.1).

ppp (features) fraction of data in the window

Remember

In high dimensions the volume near any point is exponentially tiny, so nearest neighbours stop being similar.

#7. Evaluating a classifier: accuracy and its trap

The 0/1 loss averaged over test points is the misclassification rate — an estimate of the probability that a point is incorrectly classified. For each test point the loss is 0 or 1.
Accuracy=1−misclassification rate.\text{Accuracy}=1-\text{misclassification rate}.Accuracy=1−misclassification rate.
Issue: not meaningful when classes are imbalanced or skewed.

#10. When to use which metric; trade-offs

Decide which mistake is more expensive, then watch the metric that punishes it (slide p48):

  • Disease detection: we do not want FN (sending a sick patient home can be fatal) ⇒ watch sensitivity TP/(TP+FN)TP/(TP+FN)TP/(TP+FN). Cancer classifier (2024/2025 Mid MCQ): prefer FP ≫ FN.
  • Fraud detection: we do not want FP (blocking honest customers) ⇒ watch specificity TN/(TN+FP)TN/(TN+FP)TN/(TN+FP) and precision TP/(TP+FP)TP/(TP+FP)TP/(TP+FP). Spam filtering is similar: prioritise specificity (2025 Quiz 3).

Precision–sensitivity (recall) trade-off (p50): recall = how good we are at detecting diseased people; precision = how many diagnosed unhealthy really are. Diagnose everyone unhealthy ⇒ Se=1S_e=1Se​=1, but precision may be low (TN = 0 inflates FP); for the heart data precision falls to P/(P+N)=105/165=0.64P/(P+N)=105/165=0.64P/(P+N)=105/165=0.64. We want both high (ideally 1) ⇒ combine them: F1-score.

Sensitivity–specificity trade-off (p51): SpS_pSp​ and SeS_eSe​ measure how good we are at detecting healthy and diseased people respectively. Diagnose everyone healthy ⇒ Sp=1S_p=1Sp​=1 but Se=0S_e=0Se​=0. Ideally Sp=Se=1S_p=S_e=1Sp​=Se​=1, but that is unrealistic.

The threshold slider. Most classifiers output a score compared to a threshold. Healthy and sick score distributions overlap, so lowering the threshold catches more sick people (sensitivity ↑) but raises false alarms (specificity ↓), and vice versa.

Worked example: Loan classifier recall (2024 Mid MCQ 11)

Classifier predicts "will pay back"; recall 83% ⇒ of the customers who would pay back, 83% are approved and 17% have their application wrongly rejected. It says nothing about "83% of all customers".

#11. ROC curve and AUC

Definition: ROC curve

Slide the threshold from strict to lenient and plot, for each threshold,
(FPR,TPR)=(1−Sp, Se).(\text{FPR},\text{TPR})=(1-S_p,\ S_e).(FPR,TPR)=(1−Sp​, Se​).

  • TPR (sensitivity): how many correct positive results occur among all positive samples.
  • FPR (1−1-1− specificity): how many incorrect positive results occur among all negative samples.
  • Best possible method: Se=Sp=1S_e=S_p=1Se​=Sp​=1 — the upper-left corner (0,1)(0,1)(0,1).
  • Random guess: a point on the diagonal (line of no-discrimination) — no power.
  • AUC (area under the ROC curve) quantifies the power of the classifier: 1 perfect, 0.5 random. The "alarmist" sits at (1,1)(1,1)(1,1), the "optimist" at (0,0)(0,0)(0,0).

AUC meaning (beyond the slides): the probability that a randomly chosen positive gets a higher score than a randomly chosen negative — a ranking quality, independent of any single threshold. A curve below the diagonal (AUC 0.2) is "reliably wrong": flip the predictions to get AUC 0.8.
